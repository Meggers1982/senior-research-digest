### 1. Taking Multiple Medications Linked to Higher Fall Injury Risk — But Not All Drugs Are Equal

**Journal:** *Drugs Aging* | **Published:** June 24, 2026
**PMID:** 42343007 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40266-026-01312-x

**The study:** Italian researchers analyzed health records of 32,236 community-dwelling adults aged 65 and older (68.8% female, average age 80) who had fall-related hospitalizations between 2018-2019, matched with controls who did not fall. Those using "fall-risk-increasing drugs" (FRIDs) — such as opioids, antidepressants, anticonvulsants, antihistamines, and muscle relaxants — had a 51% higher risk of fall-related hospitalization. However, when researchers separated blood pressure medications from other FRIDs, they found antihypertensives alone showed no significant link to fall injuries, while other FRIDs increased risk substantially.

**Why it matters:** This study helps clarify which medications truly drive fall risk, suggesting older adults and their doctors shouldn't necessarily worry about blood pressure medications causing falls but should carefully evaluate other drug classes.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you take opioids, antidepressants, sleep aids, or antihistamines, talk with your doctor or pharmacist about fall risk — but don't stop blood pressure medications out of fear of falling without consulting your healthcare team first.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living staff and families can use this evidence to advocate for medication reviews focusing on opioids, antidepressants, and sedatives rather than assuming all medications equally contribute to fall risk.

**Caveats:** Observational design (cannot prove causation); Italian population may not generalize to all settings; relied on hospital records rather than direct patient assessment.

### 2. Stopping Sleep Medications Is Safe for Older Adults When Done Gradually

**Journal:** *Age and Ageing* | **Published:** May 4, 2026
**PMID:** 42089738 | **DOI:** 10.1093/ageing/afag112

**The study:** Researchers systematically reviewed 30 studies involving approximately 11,000 older adults who attempted to stop chronic use of benzodiazepines and similar sleep/anxiety medications. Across different approaches — gradual dose reduction, patient education, clinician-led interventions, and health system changes — withdrawal symptoms were typically mild and temporary. Serious adverse events were rare. Structured gradual tapering achieved the best success rates for discontinuation.

**Why it matters:** Many older adults worry that stopping sleep medications will cause severe withdrawal or unbearable insomnia. This comprehensive review shows that with proper support, deprescribing is generally safe and achievable.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you've been taking sleep or anxiety medications for years and want to stop, know that gradual, supported tapering is generally safe — ask your doctor about a structured plan rather than stopping abruptly.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Caregivers and senior living professionals should encourage residents concerned about long-term sedative use that evidence-based tapering programs exist, and referral to such programs can reduce medication burden safely.

**Caveats:** High heterogeneity across studies made pooled analysis impossible; most studies were conducted in Western healthcare systems; individual responses to deprescribing vary.

### 3. Opioid-Benzodiazepine Combination Linked to Nearly Triple the Frailty Risk

**Journal:** *Experimental Gerontology* | **Published:** June 12, 2026
**PMID:** 42285234 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.exger.2026.113201

**The study:** Using nationally representative U.S. survey data from 2005-2018, researchers examined 18,434 adults aged 50 and older. Those chronically prescribed both opioids and benzodiazepines had 2.88 times higher odds of frailty compared to those taking neither drug class. Adding a third medication (gabapentinoids like gabapentin or pregabalin) increased odds to 3.71 times higher. The association held across all demographic subgroups examined.

**Why it matters:** Beyond the well-known respiratory risks of combining opioids and benzodiazepines (which carry an FDA black-box warning), this study reveals a strong link to frailty — the vulnerability syndrome that predicts falls, disability, and death.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you take both pain medications and anxiety/sleep medications together, ask your doctor whether both are truly necessary — this combination is associated with increased frailty beyond the known respiratory risks.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Families and caregivers should note that loved ones on combined opioid-benzodiazepine therapy may be at elevated frailty risk, making medication review discussions with prescribers particularly important.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design (cannot establish causation — frailty might lead to more prescriptions rather than vice versa); self-reported medication use; observational data subject to unmeasured confounding.

### 4. Blood Pressure Medications May Protect Against Muscle Loss in Hospitalized Seniors

**Journal:** *Aging Clinical and Experimental Research* | **Published:** July 8, 2026
**PMID:** 42414787 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40520-026-03452-x

**The study:** Turkish researchers studied 206 hospitalized patients aged 65 and older (60% female, median age 78). While 40% had sarcopenia (significant muscle loss), polypharmacy itself was not independently linked to sarcopenia. However, those taking angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) — a common blood pressure medication class — were significantly less likely to have sarcopenia, even after accounting for other factors.

**Why it matters:** ARBs are already widely prescribed for blood pressure and heart protection. If they also help preserve muscle mass, this adds another potential benefit for older adults concerned about maintaining strength and independence.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're worried about losing muscle strength as you age, don't assume your blood pressure medications are hurting you — certain types (ARBs) may actually help protect your muscles, though more research is needed before changing medications based on this alone.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For caregivers and senior living professionals, this study suggests that medication reviews should consider potential muscle-protective effects of certain drugs, not just their primary purpose.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design (cannot prove ARBs caused lower sarcopenia); single-center Turkish hospital; relatively small sample size (N=206); observational association only.

### 5. Half of Older Adults Worldwide Take Five or More Medications — and the Rate Is Rising

**Journal:** *The Journals of Gerontology: Series A* | **Published:** July 7, 2026
**PMID:** 42412515 | **DOI:** 10.1093/gerona/glag176

**The study:** This massive meta-analysis pooled 545 studies covering over 16.6 million older adults from 56 countries. Globally, 50.4% of older adults met criteria for polypharmacy (typically defined as five or more medications). The prevalence has increased significantly over time. Each additional medication was associated with progressively higher risks: 5% increased mortality risk, 2% increased emergency visits, and 4% increased hospitalizations per medication added.

**Why it matters:** This definitive global estimate confirms that polypharmacy affects roughly half of all older adults and that medication burden translates directly into measurable health risks that accumulate with each pill added.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you take five or more medications, you're in good company — but each additional medication slightly increases your risk of hospitalization and other problems, making it worth asking your doctor periodically whether every medication is still necessary.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living administrators and family caregivers should recognize that polypharmacy is now the norm rather than the exception, making routine medication reviews a standard best practice rather than an exception.

**Caveats:** High heterogeneity across studies (prevalence ranged from 7% to 94% in different populations); observational associations cannot prove causation; definitions of polypharmacy varied somewhat across studies.

### 6. Interdisciplinary Polypharmacy Clinic Improves Physical Function in Seniors

**Journal:** *European Geriatric Medicine* | **Published:** March 7, 2026
**PMID:** 41793562 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s41999-026-01442-w

**The study:** A Spanish hospital tested a specialized outpatient clinic where geriatricians and pharmacists jointly reviewed medications for 104 community-dwelling adults aged 75+ (average age 86, 66% female) taking five or more drugs. Over six months, the team made 376 medication recommendations (64% accepted by patients and doctors). Physical performance scores improved by an average of 1.1 points on a standard test (SPPB), and patients had fewer primary care and emergency visits.

**Why it matters:** This practical model shows that having pharmacists and geriatric specialists work together to review medications can improve older adults' physical abilities and reduce healthcare utilization — not just reduce pill counts.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're taking many medications and feel your physical abilities are declining, ask whether your health system offers a specialized medication review clinic — a team approach may help both your medication burden and your physical function.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living communities and families advocating for older relatives can seek out or request interdisciplinary polypharmacy review services, which appear to offer benefits beyond simply stopping medications.

**Caveats:** Single-center study; no randomized control group; modest sample size (N=104); only 64% of recommendations were accepted.

### 7. Pharmacists in Cancer Clinics Catch Hundreds of Drug Problems in Older Patients

**Journal:** *Journal of Geriatric Oncology* | **Published:** June 26, 2026
**PMID:** 42364399 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jgo.2026.103036

**The study:** At Princess Margaret Cancer Centre in Toronto, pharmacists spent one year reviewing medications for 205 older cancer patients (average age 81, taking an average of 11.5 medications) referred to the geriatric oncology clinic. They identified 300 drug therapy problems, with adverse drug reactions being most common. Two-thirds of their recommendations were accepted by the clinical team. Average review time was about 1.7 hours per patient.

**Why it matters:** Older adults with cancer face unique medication challenges — cancer treatments plus chronic disease medications create complex regimens. This pilot demonstrates that dedicated pharmacist review catches problems that would otherwise be missed.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're being treated for cancer and take multiple medications for other conditions, ask whether a pharmacist can review your complete medication list — they may identify problems your oncologist hasn't had time to notice.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Family caregivers accompanying older adults to cancer treatment should advocate for comprehensive medication reviews, particularly before starting new cancer therapies.

**Caveats:** Quality improvement pilot without control group; single cancer center; pharmacist time requirement (1.7 hours/patient) may limit scalability.

### 8. Hospitalization Often Misses Opportunities to Fix Inappropriate Prescribing

**Journal:** *Drugs Aging* | **Published:** June 2026
**PMID:** 42047996 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40266-026-01299-5

**The study:** Researchers systematically reviewed 21 studies examining what happens to inappropriate medications when older adults (65+) are hospitalized. Results were mixed: about half the studies showed improvement in prescribing quality by discharge, but the other half found persistent or even increased inappropriate prescribing. Success depended heavily on having structured medication reviews, pharmacist involvement, and systematic use of prescribing quality tools.

**Why it matters:** Hospital stays represent a critical window to fix medication problems, but this opportunity is frequently missed without deliberate systems in place. "Usual care" is inconsistent.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** When you or a family member is hospitalized, don't assume someone will automatically review all your medications — ask whether a pharmacist has checked your complete list and whether any changes are being made before discharge.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Families and senior living staff receiving residents back from hospitals should request medication reconciliation documentation and shouldn't assume the hospital "fixed" any pre-existing prescription problems.

**Caveats:** Observational studies only; high variability across healthcare systems studied; "usual care" definition varied by setting.

### 9. Certain Periods of Rapid Health Decline Signal Higher Risk — Polypharmacy Is a Key Marker

**Journal:** *The Lancet Healthy Longevity* | **Published:** May 2026
**PMID:** 42150586 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.lanhl.2026.100848

**The study:** Analyzing primary care records for over 1.1 million UK adults aged 50+, researchers identified "accelerated deficit accumulation" — periods when health problems pile up faster than usual. About 9% of people experienced such periods, averaging 530 days in duration. Polypharmacy was one of the conditions disproportionately linked to these acceleration periods, along with heart failure, atrial fibrillation, and mobility problems. Having an accelerated period within the past 3 years was associated with 49% higher mortality risk, 20% higher hospitalization risk, and 30% higher hip fracture risk.

**Why it matters:** This study shows that how quickly someone's health declines matters independently of how many conditions they have. Polypharmacy appears to be both a marker and possibly a contributor to these dangerous acceleration periods.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you've noticed several new health problems developing over a relatively short time, this may signal a period of heightened vulnerability — consider discussing this pattern with your doctor and whether a comprehensive medication review might help.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Caregivers and senior living staff who notice residents accumulating new diagnoses or medications rapidly should flag this pattern to healthcare providers as a potential warning sign requiring proactive intervention.

**Caveats:** Observational design; UK population; acceleration periods identified retrospectively; polypharmacy may be consequence rather than cause of rapid decline.

### 10. New Guidelines Needed: Current Advice for Polypharmacy Falls Short on Implementation

**Journal:** *Ageing Research Reviews* | **Published:** May 2026
**PMID:** 41679655 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.arr.2026.103060

**The study:** Chinese and international researchers systematically evaluated 21 clinical guidelines for managing polypharmacy and multiple chronic conditions in older adults. Using four validated assessment tools, they found consistent weaknesses: guidelines scored well on stating their purpose (82%) and presenting recommendations clearly (61%), but poorly on methodological rigor (17%) and practical applicability (8%). Only 43% of recommended content was properly reported, and only 14% of guidelines fully addressed patient preferences and long-term monitoring.

**Why it matters:** Even when guidelines exist for managing complex medication situations in older adults, they often lack the practical detail clinicians need to implement them and rarely incorporate what matters most to patients themselves.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If your doctor says they're "following guidelines" for your medications, know that current guidelines may not fully account for your personal preferences and circumstances — speak up about what matters most to you.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living professionals and family advocates should understand that published polypharmacy guidelines have significant gaps, supporting the value of individualized geriatric assessment over cookbook approaches.

**Caveats:** Evaluated guideline quality, not patient outcomes; included only English and Chinese language guidelines; guidelines assessed were published through August 2025.

### 11. Informal Caregivers Struggle with Medication Management — Often Without Training

**Journal:** *International Journal of Older People Nursing* | **Published:** July 2026
**PMID:** 42434829 | **DOI:** 10.1111/opn.70094

**The study:** This mixed-methods systematic review synthesized 16 studies examining how informal caregivers (usually family members) manage medications for older adults living at home. Caregivers reported taking on responsibilities ranging from picking up prescriptions to administering complex regimens, often after a health crisis with little preparation. Many reported errors, stress, and difficulty getting guidance from healthcare professionals. Women and younger caregivers reported higher burden.

**Why it matters:** As more older adults age at home with complex medication needs, family caregivers become de facto medication managers — usually without the training or support healthcare workers receive.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If family members help manage your medications, make sure they have the information and tools they need — pill organizers, updated medication lists, and contact information for your pharmacist can prevent errors.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Healthcare systems and senior living organizations should systematically assess and train family caregivers on medication management, not assume competence or wait for errors to occur.

**Caveats:** Studies were heterogeneous in design and populations; most from Western countries; focused on community-dwelling older adults only.

### 12. European Pharmacists Want More Training in Geriatric Care — Polypharmacy Tops the List

**Journal:** *European Geriatric Medicine* | **Published:** May 14, 2026
**PMID:** 42135573 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s41999-026-01504-z

**The study:** Researchers surveyed 346 pharmacists across Europe about their knowledge, interest, and perceived relevance of 33 geriatric topics. Pharmacists consistently rated their knowledge lower than their interest or the relevance to their work. Polypharmacy and deprescribing ranked as the top training priority, followed by chronic pain management and depression. The findings emerged from a multilingual survey conducted in 24 languages.

**Why it matters:** Pharmacists are increasingly recognized as key players in optimizing medications for older adults, but this study reveals they feel underprepared — and highly motivated to learn more about polypharmacy specifically.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Your local pharmacist may be eager to help with your medications but might not have received specific geriatric training — if you have complex needs, ask about consulting with a pharmacist who specializes in older adult care.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living organizations should consider that community pharmacists want geriatric training, creating opportunities for partnerships in medication management education and review programs.

**Caveats:** Self-selected sample of pharmacists (may not represent all pharmacists); self-reported knowledge gaps rather than tested competency; European focus.

### 13. Statins Don't Appear to Harm Cognition in Older Adults — And May Protect

**Journal:** *GeroScience* | **Published:** June 2026
**PMID:** 42010230 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11357-026-02259-y

**The study:** This narrative review synthesized evidence from randomized trials, observational studies, and genetic analyses on whether statin cholesterol medications affect cognition in older adults. Most randomized trials found no harmful cognitive effects. Some observational studies suggested possible protective associations. Large genetic studies (Mendelian randomization) involving over 100,000 people found no causal link between statin-related genes and dementia risk. Two large ongoing trials (PREVENTABLE and STAREE) will provide more definitive answers.

**Why it matters:** Many older adults or their families worry about "statin brain fog." This comprehensive review finds little evidence to support stopping statins based on cognitive concerns alone — but notes special considerations for those already experiencing cognitive impairment.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you've been avoiding or considering stopping your statin because of memory concerns, current evidence doesn't support that decision — but discuss any cognitive symptoms with your doctor to evaluate all possible causes.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Caregivers and senior living professionals should know that cognitive complaints in statin users warrant investigation but shouldn't automatically be attributed to the medication based on current evidence.

**Caveats:** Narrative review (not systematic); older adults underrepresented in many trials reviewed; individual responses may vary; two definitive trials still ongoing.

### 14. Heart Failure Drug Trials Rarely Include Patients Over 75 — Limiting Guidance for Most Patients

**Journal:** *Drugs Aging* | **Published:** May 22, 2026
**PMID:** 42174326 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40266-026-01302-z

**The study:** Researchers critically reviewed major heart failure medication trials that form the basis of current guidelines. Mean participant ages ranged from 61-67 years, with very limited inclusion of patients 75+ and almost none 80+. While relative treatment benefits appeared preserved with aging, older participants experienced higher rates of blood pressure drops, kidney problems, and treatment discontinuation. Frailty, functional status, and cognition were almost never measured.

**Why it matters:** Heart failure primarily affects older adults, yet the clinical trials guiding treatment systematically exclude the patients who need the drugs most. This creates uncertainty about optimal dosing and realistic expectations for the oldest patients.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have heart failure and find it difficult to tolerate the medications your doctor prescribes, know that the recommended doses were tested mainly in younger people — it may be reasonable to accept lower doses that you can actually take.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living staff and families should understand that heart failure medication guidelines are based on evidence from substantially younger populations, supporting individualized rather than protocol-driven approaches.

**Caveats:** Narrative review methodology; did not conduct new patient-level analyses; recommendations are clinical opinion based on limited evidence for oldest patients.

### 15. Cardiovascular Polypharmacy Linked to Kidney Problems in Primary Care Patients

**Journal:** *Drugs Aging* | **Published:** May 2026
**PMID:** 42056610 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40266-026-01297-7

**The study:** Australian researchers analyzed records of 13,568 primary care patients prescribed lipid-lowering medications (median age 63, 54% male). One-third had cardiovascular-related polypharmacy (more than five heart/blood pressure medications). Those with cardiovascular polypharmacy had 45% higher odds of kidney dysfunction. Adults 80+ had over 8 times higher odds of cardiovascular polypharmacy compared to younger patients. Diabetes and chronic kidney disease strongly predicted polypharmacy.

**Why it matters:** Cardiovascular medications are lifesaving, but this study highlights that complex cardiovascular regimens may come with kidney-related costs, particularly in the oldest patients who need careful monitoring.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you take many heart and blood pressure medications, make sure your kidney function is monitored regularly — ask your doctor how often you should have blood tests to check this.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living nurses and family caregivers should ensure that residents on multiple cardiovascular medications have routine kidney function monitoring documented in their care plans.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design (cannot prove polypharmacy caused kidney problems); Australian primary care population; kidney dysfunction defined by single lab values.

### 16. Frailty Screening Tool Using Pharmacy Data Works Well in Community Settings

**Journal:** *Drugs Aging* | **Published:** June 15, 2026
**PMID:** 42295695 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40266-026-01307-8

**The study:** Belgian researchers validated a frailty screening tool that uses only information available in pharmacy records: age, sex, insurance status, number of chronic medications, and specific medication use patterns. Testing it against an established frailty measure in 2,191 older adults (average age 74), the tool showed good discrimination (area under the curve 0.77) with 64% sensitivity and 77% specificity. Among those with polypharmacy, sensitivity improved to 77%.

**Why it matters:** This tool enables community pharmacists to flag potentially frail older adults for further assessment without requiring clinical examinations or patient questionnaires — using data they already have.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Your pharmacist may soon be able to identify if you're at risk for frailty just by looking at your medication records — this could lead to earlier interventions to help you stay independent.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living organizations and family caregivers should know that pharmacy-based frailty screening tools are being validated, potentially enabling earlier identification of residents needing enhanced support.

**Caveats:** External validation study in Belgian population; screening tool (not diagnostic); requires recalibration for different populations; 36% of frail individuals would be missed.

### 17. Prescribing Cascades — When Drugs Cause Symptoms Treated with More Drugs — Remain Underrecognized

**Journal:** *Drugs Aging* | **Published:** April 8, 2026
**PMID:** 41949780 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40266-026-01295-9

**The study:** Italian and Dutch researchers conducted an umbrella review of existing reviews plus an updated systematic review, identifying 190 studies describing prescribing cascades — situations where a medication causes a side effect that gets treated with another medication. They catalogued 84 different adverse effects leading to cascades. The most common culprits were antidepressants, dementia drugs, antipsychotics, sleep medications, and blood pressure medications, with psychiatric, neurological, and gastrointestinal symptoms being the most frequent triggers.

**Why it matters:** Prescribing cascades are a hidden driver of polypharmacy that often goes unrecognized. Understanding which medications and symptoms most commonly trigger cascades can help clinicians and patients prevent unnecessary medication accumulation.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you develop a new symptom after starting a medication and your doctor prescribes another drug to treat it, ask whether the new symptom might be a side effect — addressing the cause may be better than adding another pill.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living staff and family caregivers should watch for the pattern of new symptoms appearing after medication changes, as these may represent prescribing cascades that could be resolved by stopping or changing the original medication.

**Caveats:** Retrospective observational evidence predominates; cascade identification is challenging even with good data; no intervention studies included.

### 18. Best Practices Published for Prescribing in People with Moderate to Severe Frailty

**Journal:** *Age and Ageing* | **Published:** May 4, 2026
**PMID:** 42200477 | **DOI:** 10.1093/ageing/afag154

**The study:** UK geriatricians published clinical guidance on medication management for people with moderate to severe frailty (Clinical Frailty Scale scores suggesting significant dependency or worse). They emphasize that standard drug trial evidence doesn't apply well to this population due to reduced life expectancy and increased vulnerability to medication harms. The guidance outlines a pragmatic approach balancing individual goals, circumstances, and the realistic time horizons needed for medication benefits to emerge.

**Why it matters:** This represents expert consensus on how to think about medications in the most vulnerable older adults — acknowledging that what works for healthier older adults may cause more harm than benefit in those with significant frailty.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or someone you love has significant frailty requiring substantial daily assistance, medication decisions should be based on your personal goals and realistic timelines — not just what guidelines say for healthier people.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living professionals should be aware of this new frailty-specific prescribing guidance and consider requesting that prescribers apply these principles for residents with moderate to severe frailty.

**Caveats:** Expert consensus rather than new research; guidance is general rather than drug-specific; implementation requires clinical judgment.

### 19. Rural Thailand Study Quantifies Massive Health Burden from Polypharmacy

**Journal:** *Aging Clinical and Experimental Research* | **Published:** June 23, 2026
**PMID:** 42334761 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40520-026-03435-y

**The study:** Researchers analyzed health data from 587,905 older adults (age 60+) across 46 hospitals in rural northeastern Thailand. Nearly 60% had polypharmacy, contributing over 4 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) of health burden. About 32% were taking potentially inappropriate medications. Diabetes caused the highest disease burden despite lower prevalence than hypertension, illustrating the importance of measuring impact beyond simple counts.

**Why it matters:** This massive population study from a middle-income country demonstrates that polypharmacy and inappropriate prescribing are global challenges, not just wealthy-country problems, and quantifies their population-level impact.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Medication management challenges affect older adults worldwide — if you're living in or connected to a lower-resource setting, know that polypharmacy concerns are just as valid and medication review is just as important.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Global health organizations and international senior care networks should recognize that polypharmacy burden is substantial in middle-income countries, requiring attention beyond high-income healthcare systems.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design; Thai rural population may not generalize to other settings; DALY estimates are gross burden, not causally attributed; hospital-attending population only.

### 20. App-Based Medication Reminders Plus Pharmacist Support Help African American Seniors

**Journal:** *Senior Care Pharmacist* | **Published:** June 1, 2026
**PMID:** 42288945 | **DOI:** 10.4140/TCP.n.2026.139

**The study:** This pilot study enrolled older African American adults (65+, 87.5% female) in Houston, Texas who were taking five or more medications. Participants used a smartphone medication reminder app and received two one-hour pharmacist-led telehealth sessions over six months. Those reporting forgotten medications dropped from 74% to 44%. Health technology use increased from 14% to 68%. Self-reported medication knowledge and satisfaction with pharmacist responsiveness both improved significantly.

**Why it matters:** Technology-based medication support combined with pharmacist consultation appears acceptable and helpful for this population, which has historically faced disparities in medication management support.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Smartphone apps designed to remind you about medications, combined with talking to a pharmacist, may help you take your medications more reliably — even if you haven't used health technology before.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living programs and family members supporting older African American adults can consider combining medication reminder apps with pharmacist telehealth services as a practical adherence support strategy.

**Caveats:** Small pilot study (N=40 analyzed); no control group; predominantly female sample; self-reported outcomes; 6-month follow-up only.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Multiple studies in this batch converge on a "hidden infrastructure" problem: the systems meant to catch medication harm—hospitals, guidelines, caregiver networks—are failing to do so reliably. Hospitalization often misses deprescribing opportunities (PMID 42047996); published polypharmacy guidelines lack practical applicability (PMID 41679655); informal caregivers manage complex regimens without training or support (PMID 42434829); and European pharmacists report major knowledge gaps despite high motivation (PMID 42135573).

**Why pitch this now:** As the new global meta-analysis (PMID 42412515) confirms that half of all older adults have polypharmacy—and each added pill raises mortality, hospitalization, and emergency visit risk—the question shifts from "Is polypharmacy a problem?" to "Why aren't our safety nets catching it?" The infrastructure gap is timely because healthcare systems are investing in medication reconciliation and deprescribing initiatives, yet evidence suggests these efforts remain inconsistent.

**Angle:** A feature titled "The Safety Net with Holes: Why Hospitals, Guidelines, and Families Keep Missing Medication Problems" could examine how each layer—acute care, clinical guidance, and home-based caregiving—fails to intercept polypharmacy harms, and profile emerging models (interdisciplinary clinics, pharmacy-based screening, digital tools) that show promise. Audience: senior living professionals and family caregivers seeking systemic solutions rather than individual tips.
