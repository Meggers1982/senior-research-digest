### Study 2: Older Cancer Patients Got Lower Opioid Doses — and Had the Same Pain Relief
**PMID:** 42853544 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Unmeasured mechanism presented as the explanation** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "If you or your doctor are worried that a lower opioid dose means you will be left in pain, this study suggests it does not have to: what mattered was frequent reassessment and adjustment."
- **Abstract says:** "Opioid doses ... were recorded at admission, at each opioid prescription change, and at discharge ... Despite age-related differences in opioid prescribing, comparable pain control was achieved across all age groups ... supporting individualized age-tailored opioid titration in specialist palliative care."
- **Problem:** The study compared doses and pain scores by age group; it did not measure how often patients were reassessed, so it cannot show that reassessment frequency was "what mattered." The same inference appears in "Why it matters" ("dose size matters less than how attentively it is adjusted").
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to: "this study suggests lower doses in a specialist unit were accompanied by comparable pain relief; the authors credit individualized, age-tailored titration.""

### Study 5: Noisy Breathing at the Bedside: 1 in 4 Bereaved Families Show PTSD Symptoms After Witnessing It
**PMID:** 42727789 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline generalizes a subgroup rate** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Noisy Breathing at the Bedside: 1 in 4 Bereaved Families Show PTSD Symptoms After Witnessing It"
- **Abstract says:** "The proportion screened positive was 26.9% for death rattle and 26.0% for agonal breathing among participants who perceived that these signs had been very distressing."
- **Problem:** The ~27% figure applies only to respondents who rated the experience as very distressing, not to all bereaved families who witnessed the signs. The body text states this correctly, but the headline does not.
- **Suggested fix:** "Revise to "1 in 4 Families Who Found It Very Distressing Screen Positive for PTSD Symptoms.""

**Issue B. Prevalence claim not in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "These breathing changes are nearly universal at the end of life and are often dismissed as harmless to the patient"
- **Abstract says:** "Death rattle and agonal breathing are distressing for most families. Healthcare providers often explain these signs as a part of the natural dying process."
- **Problem:** The abstract makes no claim about how common these breathing changes are among dying patients; "nearly universal" is unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Replace with "These breathing changes are common at the end of life and are usually explained to families as part of natural dying.""

**Issue C. Mild hype in framing** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "this is some of the strongest evidence yet that what families see and hear at the bedside can leave lasting psychological marks"
- **Abstract says:** "Bereaved family members exposed to death rattle and agonal breathing may have PTSD symptoms. Communication and care strategies to address family distress are needed."
- **Problem:** A cross-sectional screening survey cannot be characterized as "strongest evidence yet"; the authors use the hedged "may have PTSD symptoms."
- **Suggested fix:** "Soften to "this nationwide survey adds evidence that what families witness at the bedside may leave lasting distress.""

### Study 6: What Actually Makes or Breaks a Family Caregiver During Home Hospice
**PMID:** 42680076 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Screening number imprecise** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Researchers screened nearly 8,000 records and synthesized 19 qualitative studies from 13 countries"
- **Abstract says:** "Seven databases ... were searched, yielding 7933 records. After removing 3319 duplicates and screening 4647 records, 669 studies were assessed for eligibility."
- **Problem:** 7,933 records were retrieved; 4,647 were actually screened after de-duplication. "Screened nearly 8,000 records" conflates retrieval with screening.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say "retrieved nearly 8,000 records and screened 4,647.""

**Issue B. Background claim not in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Most home deaths depend on an unpaid family member"
- **Abstract says:** "Family caregivers are fundamental to enabling home-based end-of-life care, yet their well-being remains inadequately understood."
- **Problem:** The abstract does not report what proportion of home deaths rely on unpaid family carers; this statistic is unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to "Home-based end-of-life care depends fundamentally on family caregivers.""

### Study 7: Delirium Is Common at the End of Life — and Often Mistaken for Dementia
**PMID:** 42873521 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Hypoactive subtype "most often missed"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the quiet, withdrawn form of delirium is the one most often missed"
- **Abstract says:** "It is characterized by acute disturbances in attention and awareness that often fluctuate throughout the day and may present as hypoactive, hyperactive, or mixed psychomotor subtypes."
- **Problem:** The abstract lists the subtypes but makes no claim that the hypoactive form is most often missed. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Attribute to the broader literature or drop: "delirium can be easy to miss when a person is quiet and withdrawn rather than agitated.""

**Issue B. Reversibility overstated** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "because the cause is often reversible even during palliative care"
- **Abstract says:** "Effective prevention and management of delirium require a multifaceted, interprofessional approach that addresses modifiable risk factors, as well as the underlying acute medical causes."
- **Problem:** The abstract refers to modifiable risk factors and underlying acute causes but does not state how often delirium is reversible at the end of life.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change to "because there may be modifiable causes, including medications, that the team can address.""

### Study 9: When a Hospital Asks a Court to Take Over Your Decisions: What the Records Show
**PMID:** 42929333 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Interpretation stronger than reported associations** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "these data suggest that once a hospital files, the outcome is nearly automatic and that poorer, less-educated older adults fare worst"
- **Abstract says:** "Medicaid recipients had lower odds of being assigned to a guardian with whom they had a prior, personal relationship (p = 0.04). Lower educational attainment was associated with full guardianship (p = 0.02). More restrictive forms of guardianship were associated with decreased likelihood of being discharged home."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports two specific associations; "fare worst" implies a broader outcome comparison (e.g., in health or welfare) that the study did not measure.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to "...and that Medicaid recipients and people with less education were more likely to end up with a professional or more restrictive guardian.""

### Study 12: Bedside Signs Still Beat Scoring Systems for Predicting the Final Days
**PMID:** 42660371 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline overstates ranking of tools** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Bedside Signs Still Beat Scoring Systems for Predicting the Final Days"
- **Abstract says:** "In 166 cancer patients, PPI + SPS performed best, with AUC 0.72 ... Among individual predictors, SPS performed best, with AUC 0.70"
- **Problem:** The best-performing model was the Palliative Prognostic Index combined with clinical signs, not signs alone; signs only 'beat' the other tools when each was used individually.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to something like 'Bedside Signs Carry Most of the Weight in Predicting the Final Days' or note that the combination performed best by a modest margin."

**Issue B. Unlisted noncancer diagnoses** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "meaning accuracy remains limited for people dying of heart, lung, kidney, or neurologic disease"
- **Abstract says:** "Among 124 noncancer patients, PPI + SPS and SPS had similar AUCs of 0.69"
- **Problem:** The abstract only says 'noncancer'; the specific disease categories are not reported. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say simply 'for people dying of noncancer illnesses' unless the full text specifies the diagnoses."

**Issue C. All three tools in noncancer group** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "In 124 noncancer patients, the tools performed similarly but modestly (AUC about 0.69)"
- **Abstract says:** "Fitted model parameters were applied to the noncancer validation dataset ... PPI + SPS and SPS had similar AUCs of 0.69"
- **Problem:** Only the two best-performing models (SPS and PPI+SPS) were evaluated in the noncancer group, not all three tools.
- **Suggested fix:** "Specify that the two best-performing models were carried into the noncancer group."

### Study 13: Pancreatic Cancer Patients Say Palliative Care Was Explained to Them Badly — or Not at All

### Study 15: For Agitated Delirium at the End of Life, Lorazepam Hit Families' Comfort Goals Better Than Haloperidol Alone
**PMID:** 42586325 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Drug-specific suggestion from an underpowered secondary analysis** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Caregivers can reasonably ask the palliative team what medication plan is being used and whether a benzodiazepine-based regimen has been considered when agitation persists."
- **Abstract says:** "Caregiver-defined PSGs were available for 67 patients included in this secondary analysis ... lorazepam (odds ratio = 12.2; 95% CI = 2.0-75.1)"
- **Problem:** With roughly 13-14 patients per arm, very wide confidence intervals, and placebo reaching the goal 53.8% of the time, nudging families toward a specific drug class is a stretch from a preplanned secondary endpoint.
- **Suggested fix:** "Frame the angle around asking the team what sedation goal is being targeted and how it is being reassessed, rather than naming a drug class."

**Issue B. Placebo performance undercuts the framing** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "this is direct randomized evidence that the drug choice — and the family's own stated comfort goal — meaningfully changes whether that distress is relieved"
- **Abstract says:** "lorazepam 10/13 (76.9%), combination therapy 8/13 (61.5%), placebo 7/13 (53.8%), and haloperidol 3/14 (21.4%)"
- **Problem:** Placebo outperformed escalated haloperidol, so the finding is as much about haloperidol escalation failing as about any drug reliably relieving distress.
- **Suggested fix:** "Note explicitly that placebo also outperformed escalated haloperidol, which tempers the 'drug choice relieves distress' message."

### Study 17: Linking Nursing Homes to Palliative Care Teams Cut Hospital Trips and Eased Symptoms, Review Finds
**PMID:** 42929149 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Causal headline from a narrative synthesis** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Linking Nursing Homes to Palliative Care Teams Cut Hospital Trips and Eased Symptoms, Review Finds"
- **Abstract says:** "Outcomes were reported for aged care providers ... health systems (reduced acute care utilisation), and for older people who were nearing the end of life (less symptom distress)."
- **Problem:** The review reports outcomes described across heterogeneous studies with narrative synthesis; 'Cut' and 'Eased' assert a causal effect the design cannot establish.
- **Suggested fix:** "Use 'Linked to Fewer Hospital Trips and Less Symptom Distress, Review Finds'."

**Issue B. 'Nursing homes' narrows the sector studied** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Linking Nursing Homes to Palliative Care Teams"
- **Abstract says:** "interorganisational linkage strategies connecting palliative care services/teams and aged care providers"
- **Problem:** 'Aged care providers' in the source is broader than nursing homes and may include community/home aged care.
- **Suggested fix:** "Use 'aged care providers' or 'residential and community aged care' in at least one mention."

### Study 18: Feeding Tube Decisions in Advanced Cancer Hinge on a Narrow 'Window of Opportunity,' Clinicians Say
**PMID:** 42922280 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Direction of shifting nutritional goals** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "nutritional goals that shift as disease progresses, moving from maintaining weight toward comfort and pleasure in eating"
- **Abstract says:** "(4) Shifting nutritional priorities ... managing changing nutritional goals with disease progression"
- **Problem:** The abstract names the theme but does not specify that goals move from weight maintenance toward comfort and pleasure in eating.
- **Suggested fix:** "State the theme as 'nutritional priorities that shift as the disease progresses' without specifying the direction, unless confirmed in the full text."

**Issue B. Who the expectations differ between** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "mismatched expectations between patients and their families"
- **Abstract says:** "(3) Differing expectations and experiences of patients and family"
- **Problem:** The abstract's wording is ambiguous — it could mean patients' and families' expectations differ from professionals' — so asserting a patient-versus-family mismatch is an interpretation.
- **Suggested fix:** "Mirror the abstract's wording: 'differing expectations and experiences among patients and families'."

### Study 19: When Patients Say They Want to Die, Loss of Meaning — Not Pain — Is the Strongest Signal
**PMID:** 42918739 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. 'Feeling like a burden' presented as a driver** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "more often a signal of eroded meaning and feeling like a burden than a settled decision"
- **Abstract says:** "total scores were calculated both with and without the additional item "feeling like a burden to others" ... demoralization was the only independent predictor of WTHD (OR = 6.56)"
- **Problem:** Burden was handled as a scoring item in the symptom measure, not reported as an independent predictor; only demoralization was.
- **Suggested fix:** "Limit the claim to demoralization — meaninglessness, hopelessness, lost dignity — as the independent predictor."

### Study 20: Advance Care Planning Conversations in ALS Deliver What Patients Asked For — 91% of the Time
**PMID:** 42586324 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Causal claim contradicts the stated caveat** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "having a documented advance care planning conversation is the single most reliable way to make sure your preferences — including dying at home — are the ones carried out"
- **Abstract says:** "ACP discussion was positively associated with gastrostomy placement, Palliative Care activation, and home death ... 90.8% concordance between expressed choices and outcomes"
- **Problem:** This is a retrospective register study showing association; the digest's own caveat says it cannot prove ACP caused the outcomes, but the story angle tells readers it is the 'most reliable way' to secure their preferences.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to 'people who had documented ACP conversations were far more likely to receive care matching their stated wishes' and keep the association framing."

**Issue B. Rise in ACP attributed to the 2017 law** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "rates rose over the decade from 51.6% to 61.9% after Italy's 2017 self-determination law"
- **Abstract says:** "The ACP prevalence increased over time from 51.6% to 61.94% (P = 0.025) ... The ACP increase over the last decade reflects a growing emphasis on patient autonomy in Italy."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports an increase over time and attributes it to a broader cultural shift; it does not report that the increase occurred specifically after, or because of, Law 219/2017.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say rates rose over the study period from 51.6% to 61.9%, during an era that included Italy's 2017 self-determination law."

### Study 21: The Words Hospice Staff Use for End-of-Life Confusion May Change the Treatment Patients Get
**PMID:** 42910992 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Hypoactive delirium described as untreated** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Quiet, withdrawn (hypoactive) delirium was poorly recognized and almost never named or treated."
- **Abstract says:** "Hypoactive delirium was poorly identified, rarely named, with little discourse relating to its management in the dying phase."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports that clinicians rarely discussed managing hypoactive delirium, which is not the same as establishing that it was almost never treated. The digest converts a finding about clinician discourse into a claim about actual care delivered.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change to "...was poorly recognized, rarely named, and barely discussed when staff talked about management in the dying phase.""

**Issue B. Softer association stated as a direct effect** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "When staff did name delirium, decision-making about assessment and treatment was more transparent"
- **Abstract says:** "Use of 'delirium' seemed to be associated with more transparent decision-making regarding assessment and management"
- **Problem:** The abstract's hedged, associational wording ("seemed to be associated with") is presented as an established difference. The Caveats do note that causation cannot be established, which partly offsets this.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add the hedge: "When staff did name delirium, decision-making about assessment and treatment appeared to be more transparent.""

**Issue C. Reversible causes claim not in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Delirium at the end of life often has reversible contributors such as pain, infection, constipation or medication, and the label a clinician reaches for can determine whether anyone looks for those causes or simply reaches for a sedative."
- **Abstract says:** "The abstract makes no mention of reversible contributors to delirium, and reports only that sedative use "received more emphasis" when terms such as 'terminal', 'agitation' and 'confusion' were used."
- **Problem:** The reversible-causes background is standard clinical knowledge but is not supported by this abstract; the second clause also states more strongly than the study can support that terminology determines whether causes are investigated. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Attribute the background to general clinical knowledge rather than this study, and soften to "the label a clinician reaches for may shape how much attention goes to looking for a cause versus sedating.""

### Study 22: Agitation in the Final Days of Dementia Has No Agreed Definition — and No Validated Way to Measure It
**PMID:** 42885674 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Confusion with untreated pain overstated** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "it is frequently confused with the behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia or with untreated pain"
- **Abstract says:** ""Differentiating TA, or delirium from behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD) is often difficult" and "There is an association between pain, agitation, and delirium in various stages of dementia, particularly at the end of life.""
- **Problem:** The abstract describes difficulty distinguishing terminal agitation from BPSD, and separately notes an association with pain — it does not say terminal agitation is frequently mistaken for untreated pain.
- **Suggested fix:** "Rephrase to "it is often hard to tell apart from the behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia, and pain, agitation and delirium are closely intertwined at the end of life.""

**Issue B. Author affiliation characterized too narrowly** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the authors are affiliated with a single Australian aged- and palliative-care provider (HammondCare)"
- **Abstract says:** "Authors list HammondCare affiliations alongside Curtin University, The University of Sydney, Edith Cowan University, University of Canberra, UNSW and Monash University."
- **Problem:** All authors do hold HammondCare appointments, but each also holds academic affiliations; describing them as affiliated with a single provider is incomplete. No funding source is disclosed in the abstract.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change to "all authors hold appointments at HammondCare, an Australian aged- and palliative-care provider, alongside university affiliations.""

**Issue C. Scope of the syndrome narrowed to dementia** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Australian dementia and palliative care specialists reviewed what is known about terminal agitation — also called terminal restlessness or terminal delirium — in people dying with dementia."
- **Abstract says:** ""While TA is evident in dementia (including Alzheimer's disease), it is not exclusive to the condition and also occurs in other terminal illnesses, e.g., cancer.""
- **Problem:** The digest presents terminal agitation as a dementia-specific phenomenon; the authors explicitly note it occurs in other terminal illnesses. The review's focus is dementia, so this is a framing omission rather than an error.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add a clause: "...in people dying with dementia, though the authors note the syndrome also occurs in other terminal illnesses such as cancer.""
