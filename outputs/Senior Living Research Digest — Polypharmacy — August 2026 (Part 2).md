### 1. Korea's Hospital-Based Polypharmacy Management Program Cuts Readmissions and Healthcare Costs

**Journal:** *Drugs & Aging* | **Published:** August 8, 2026
**PMID:** 42570168 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40266-026-01325-6

**The study:** Researchers evaluated a Korean national pilot program in which hospital-based teams reviewed medications for patients aged 65 and older who were taking ten or more drugs (or five or more with additional risk factors). Using propensity-score matching, they compared 1,135 program participants to 1,125 matched controls drawn from national insurance claims data. Over 90 days, participants had a 15% lower risk of hospital readmission and incurred roughly $340 less in hospitalization costs per person.

**Why it matters:** Structured medication reviews delivered during a hospital stay can reduce preventable readmissions and ease financial strain on healthcare systems—benefits especially important for older adults juggling multiple chronic conditions.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you take many medications and are hospitalized, ask whether your hospital offers a pharmacist-led medication review before discharge—it may help you avoid another hospital stay.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Health systems and policymakers may look to Korea's program as a model for integrating pharmacist review into hospital care for high-risk older patients, potentially improving outcomes while controlling costs.

**Caveats:** Observational design using a historical control group (cannot establish causation); single-country setting may limit generalizability; unmeasured confounders possible despite matching.

### 2. Pharmacist-Led Deprescribing Triples Medication Reductions in Patients Taking Many Drugs

**Journal:** *Age and Ageing* | **Published:** July 2, 2026
**PMID:** 42472621 | **DOI:** 10.1093/ageing/afag209

**The study:** In a Dutch cluster-randomized trial, 58 community pharmacies enrolled 318 adults aged 75 and older who used multidose dispensing systems and took very high numbers of medications. Pharmacists trained in deprescribing performed clinical medication reviews for the intervention group. After six months, intervention patients had 2.5 medications reduced or stopped on average, compared with 1.7 in usual-care controls—a statistically significant difference. Health problems and quality of life remained stable in both groups.

**Why it matters:** A structured deprescribing intervention can meaningfully reduce pill burden in the highest-risk older adults without apparent harm, offering a scalable model for pharmacies worldwide.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you take many daily medications and use a pill-pack system, consider asking your pharmacist about a formal medication review—some drugs may no longer be necessary.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Pharmacy owners and healthcare planners can consider adopting deprescribing-focused reviews for patients with hyperpolypharmacy, particularly those using automated dispensing, to streamline regimens safely.

**Caveats:** Six-month follow-up is relatively short; no significant improvement in quality of life was detected; conducted in a single country (Netherlands).

### 3. Diuretics, Antipsychotics, and Polypharmacy Linked to Injurious Falls in Australian Older Adults

**Journal:** *Drugs & Aging* | **Published:** July 23, 2026
**PMID:** 42489786 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40266-026-01320-x

**The study:** Researchers followed 952 community-dwelling Australians aged 65 and older (median follow-up eight years) and linked their medication records to emergency department visits for fall injuries. After adjusting for clinical factors, polypharmacy (five or more medications) was associated with a 2.6-fold higher risk of injurious falls; diuretic use carried a nearly three-fold risk, and antipsychotic use a 2.2-fold risk. Diuretics accounted for the largest share of fall injuries attributable to medications at the population level.

**Why it matters:** Identifying specific drug classes—rather than just total pill counts—can help clinicians and patients prioritize which medications to reassess for fall prevention.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you take a diuretic ("water pill") or an antipsychotic, ask your doctor whether your dose can be lowered or whether alternatives exist, especially if you've experienced dizziness or unsteadiness.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Caregivers and senior living staff should be aware that diuretics are a leading contributor to fall injuries; routine medication reviews targeting these agents may reduce emergency visits.

**Caveats:** Observational design (cannot prove causation); single geographic region (Geelong, Australia); some medication categories had small sample sizes.

### 4. Certain Sedatives and Antiepileptics Strongly Tied to Repeat Falls in Danish Seniors

**Journal:** *European Geriatric Medicine* | **Published:** August 12, 2026
**PMID:** 42584792 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s41999-026-01568-x

**The study:** A one-year prospective study tracked 241 community-dwelling adults aged 75 and older in Denmark, using monthly fall calendars verified by telephone. Among the 74% taking at least one fall-risk-increasing drug, baseline use of benzodiazepine-related drugs, strong anticholinergics, and antiepileptics was significantly associated with having two or more falls during follow-up, with hazard ratios ranging from about 2.5 to nearly 5.

**Why it matters:** The findings highlight that not all fall-risk medications are equal—benzodiazepines, anticholinergics, and antiepileptics warrant special scrutiny in older adults prone to falling.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you've fallen more than once in the past year and take a sleeping pill, anti-anxiety medication, or seizure drug, talk with your doctor about whether a safer alternative exists.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Clinicians conducting fall-risk assessments should prioritize reviewing benzodiazepine-related drugs, strong anticholinergics, and antiepileptics, even in relatively well-functioning older patients.

**Caveats:** Small sample (N=241); single municipality in Denmark; observational design; causation cannot be established.

### 5. Focused Deprescribing of Specific Drug Classes Appears Feasible—but Clinical Benefits Remain Uncertain

**Journal:** *Age and Ageing* | **Published:** August 3, 2026
**PMID:** 42607278 | **DOI:** 10.1093/ageing/afag243

**The study:** An umbrella review synthesized 27 systematic reviews examining "focused deprescribing"—targeted withdrawal or dose reduction of specific drug classes such as benzodiazepines, antidepressants, anticholinergics, and fall-risk-increasing drugs—in older adults across clinical settings. Reductions in medication use were most consistent for benzodiazepines and other psychotropics, especially when combined with behavioral therapy. However, clinical outcomes like mortality, falls, and cognition were inconsistently reported and rarely improved. Most underlying reviews were rated critically low quality.

**Why it matters:** While stopping certain drug classes is achievable, evidence that doing so improves hard clinical outcomes for older adults is still lacking—underscoring the need for better-designed trials.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Stopping a long-term sleeping pill or sedative may be possible with proper support, but don't expect an immediate change in overall health—work with your healthcare team to set realistic goals.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living medical directors and primary care physicians should recognize that focused deprescribing is a reasonable strategy for reducing medication burden, but current evidence does not guarantee improvements in falls, cognition, or survival.

**Caveats:** Umbrella review of largely low-quality systematic reviews; certainty of evidence generally low or very low; heterogeneity across studies.

### 6. Opioid-Benzodiazepine Co-Prescription Linked to Three-Fold Higher Frailty in Older Americans

**Journal:** *Experimental Gerontology* | **Published:** June 12, 2026 (online)
**PMID:** 42285234 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.exger.2026.113201

**The study:** Analyzing nationally representative data from 18,434 U.S. adults aged 50 and older across seven NHANES cycles (2005–2018), researchers found that chronic co-prescription of opioids and benzodiazepines (≥90 days) was associated with 2.88 times the odds of frailty compared with using neither drug. Adding a gabapentinoid raised the odds to 3.71. The association persisted across all subgroups and multiple sensitivity analyses.

**Why it matters:** Beyond the well-known respiratory risks, long-term combined use of opioids and benzodiazepines appears tightly linked to frailty—a condition that predicts disability, hospitalization, and death in older adults.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you take both an opioid painkiller and a sedative like a benzodiazepine on a long-term basis, discuss with your doctor whether one or both can be tapered; these combinations may accelerate decline.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Prescribers and care teams should screen patients on chronic opioid-benzodiazepine regimens for signs of frailty and consider deprescribing strategies where clinically appropriate.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design (cannot determine causation); frailty index based on self-reported questionnaire items; potential residual confounding despite extensive adjustment.

### 7. High Anticholinergic Burden Doubles Severe Treatment Toxicity Risk in Older Cancer Patients

**Journal:** *Drugs & Aging* | **Published:** July 22, 2026
**PMID:** 42487095 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40266-026-01316-7

**The study:** Dutch researchers analyzed 434 patients aged 70 and older starting chemotherapy or targeted therapy for cancer. Those with a high anticholinergic burden score (≥3) experienced grade 3 or higher treatment toxicity significantly more often than those with lower scores (adjusted odds ratio 1.99). Frailty independently predicted toxicity as well.

**Why it matters:** Medications with anticholinergic effects—common in many seniors' regimens—may compound the side effects of cancer treatment, suggesting that medication review before chemotherapy could improve tolerability.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Before starting cancer treatment, ask your oncology team to review all your medications, including over-the-counter drugs that may have hidden anticholinergic effects.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Oncologists and geriatricians should calculate anticholinergic burden as part of pre-treatment assessments; reducing this burden might lower the risk of severe chemotherapy side effects.

**Caveats:** Single-center study in the Netherlands; observational design; anticholinergic burden calculated at one time point.

### 8. Blood-Based Alzheimer's Biomarkers Show Promise—but Polypharmacy and Frailty Complicate Interpretation

**Journal:** *Age and Ageing* | **Published:** July 2, 2026
**PMID:** 42476554 | **DOI:** 10.1093/ageing/afag213

**The study:** A clinical commentary reviewed advances in blood-based biomarkers for Alzheimer's disease, particularly plasma p-tau217, which has shown high accuracy for detecting amyloid pathology across multiple platforms. The authors noted that polypharmacy, chronic kidney disease, and frailty can influence absolute biomarker concentrations but appear not to negate diagnostic accuracy when results are interpreted in context.

**Why it matters:** Simple blood tests may soon supplement or replace invasive spinal taps or costly brain scans for Alzheimer's diagnosis—though clinicians must account for the complex health profiles of older patients.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Emerging blood tests for Alzheimer's disease may make diagnosis easier, but your doctor will still need to consider your overall health—including medications and kidney function—when interpreting results.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Memory clinics and primary care practices should prepare to integrate blood-based Alzheimer's biomarkers into comprehensive geriatric assessments, rather than using them in isolation.

**Caveats:** Commentary/narrative review (no new primary data); blood-based tests not yet universally validated for all older populations; guidelines recommend use only in symptomatic patients seen by specialists.

### 9. Underweight Stroke Patients on Many Medications Recover Less Function

**Journal:** *Drugs & Aging* | **Published:** August 4, 2026
**PMID:** 42550458 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40266-026-01326-5

**The study:** A Japanese retrospective study of 1,245 stroke patients (median age 75) admitted to a rehabilitation ward found that polypharmacy was associated with modestly lower motor function at discharge—but only among underweight patients (BMI < 18.5). In normal-weight and obese patients, the number of medications did not significantly affect recovery.

**Why it matters:** Nutritional status may modify how polypharmacy affects stroke rehabilitation outcomes, suggesting that underweight patients deserve especially careful medication review.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you are recovering from a stroke and are underweight, work closely with your care team to ensure your medications are truly necessary—extra pills may slow your progress.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Rehabilitation staff should flag underweight stroke patients for pharmacist consultation, as polypharmacy may have a disproportionate impact on their functional recovery.

**Caveats:** Retrospective single-center study in Japan; effect size modest; causation cannot be established.

### 10. Pharmacist-Led Medication Review Uncovers Drug Problems in Older Cancer Patients Before Treatment

**Journal:** *Journal of Geriatric Oncology* | **Published:** June 26, 2026
**PMID:** 42364399 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jgo.2026.103036

**The study:** Over one year, pharmacists at Toronto's Princess Margaret Cancer Centre reviewed 205 patients (mean age 81) referred to the Older Adults with Cancer Clinic. Patients averaged 11.5 medications. Pharmacists identified 300 drug therapy problems—most commonly adverse drug reactions—with about two-thirds of recommendations accepted by the geriatric oncology team. The average review took roughly 1.5 hours per patient.

**Why it matters:** Embedding pharmacist expertise in geriatric oncology clinics can catch medication issues before cancer treatment begins, potentially preventing complications in a high-risk population.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're starting cancer treatment and take many medications, ask whether a pharmacist can review your full medication list—it may prevent unwanted side effects.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Cancer centers serving older patients should consider dedicated pharmacist staffing for pre-treatment medication reconciliation; most identified problems were accepted by clinicians.

**Caveats:** Single-center quality improvement project; no control group; labor-intensive (over 100 minutes per patient on average).

### 11. Expert Consensus Offers Practical Guidance on Opioid Use for Chronic Pain in Older Adults

**Journal:** *Drugs & Aging* | **Published:** August 21, 2026
**PMID:** 42627454 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40266-026-01329-2

**The study:** A multidisciplinary Portuguese panel reviewed evidence and reached consensus on safe opioid prescribing for older adults with chronic pain. They emphasized "start low, go slow" dosing, preference for opioids with inactive metabolites or predictable clearance, careful selection of administration routes, and proactive management of constipation, cognitive effects, and fall risk. Distinct strategies were recommended for cancer versus non-cancer pain.

**Why it matters:** Chronic pain is common in older adults, but physiological changes make opioids riskier; this consensus provides a roadmap for balancing pain relief with safety.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you need opioid pain medication, ask your prescriber about starting at a low dose and choosing a formulation suited to your kidney and liver function—this can reduce side effects.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Clinicians and senior living nurses should follow age-specific dosing principles and monitor for constipation, confusion, and falls whenever opioids are prescribed to older residents.

**Caveats:** Consensus document (not a systematic review or trial); recommendations based on available evidence and expert opinion; further research needed for age-specific dosing algorithms.

### 12. Community Pharmacy Frailty Screening Tool Validated in Belgian Older Adults

**Journal:** *Drugs & Aging* | **Published:** June 15, 2026
**PMID:** 42295695 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40266-026-01307-8

**The study:** Researchers externally validated a frailty screening tool that uses routinely collected pharmacy data—age, sex, reimbursement status, number of chronic medications, and specific drug use—against the SHARE-FI frailty assessment in 2,191 community-dwelling Belgians (mean age 74). The tool achieved good discrimination (area under the curve 0.77) and, after recalibration, accurately estimated frailty risk, performing especially well among those with polypharmacy.

**Why it matters:** Community pharmacists could use electronic dispensing data to flag older customers at risk for frailty, enabling proactive interventions without additional patient questionnaires.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Your local pharmacist may soon be able to identify whether you're at risk for frailty just by reviewing your prescription history—this could prompt earlier conversations about staying strong and independent.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Pharmacy chains and health systems may consider integrating validated frailty screening tools into dispensing software to identify high-risk patients for targeted medication reviews or referrals.

**Caveats:** Validated in a Belgian population; performance may differ in other countries; screening tools require clinical follow-up to confirm frailty status.

### 13. Fall-Risk Drugs—Not Antihypertensives—Drive Injurious Falls in U.S. Older Adults

**Journal:** *Archives of Gerontology and Geriatrics* | **Published:** May 28, 2026 (online)
**PMID:** 42308749 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.archger.2026.106314

**The study:** Using two-year follow-up data from 1,273 Black and White community-dwelling adults (median age 83) in the Health, Aging and Body Composition Study, researchers examined associations between fall-risk-increasing drugs and emergency visits for fall injuries. Antihypertensive use alone was not linked to falls, but use of other fall-risk drugs—antidepressants, anticonvulsants, opioids, antihistamines, and muscle relaxants—was associated with a 35% higher risk of injurious falls.

**Why it matters:** Blanket concerns about blood pressure medications and falls may be misplaced; more attention should focus on sedatives, opioids, and other centrally acting agents.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're worried about falling, don't stop your blood pressure medicine on your own—but do ask your doctor about other drugs like sedatives or pain pills that may increase your risk.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Fall prevention programs should target specific high-risk drug classes rather than broadly advising caution with all blood pressure medications, which showed no independent association with falls in this study.

**Caveats:** Observational cohort; two-year follow-up; sample limited to two U.S. sites; racial distribution may not generalize.

### 14. Hip Strength and Diuretic Use Are Top Predictors of Injurious Falls

**Journal:** *Archives of Gerontology and Geriatrics* | **Published:** June 8, 2026 (online)
**PMID:** 42296796 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.archger.2026.106333

**The study:** In a predictive modeling study of 952 Australians aged 65 and older, researchers found that adding hip abductor strength and diuretic use to a baseline model (age, sex, mobility, assistive device use, Timed Up and Go test) improved injurious fall prediction more than any other modifiable factors tested. Polypharmacy added little beyond these variables.

**Why it matters:** Strengthening hip muscles and reviewing diuretic necessity may be among the most impactful strategies for preventing fall injuries—more so than simply counting total medications.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Exercises that strengthen your hip muscles—like side leg raises—may help prevent serious falls; ask a physical therapist which movements are right for you.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Fall prevention programs should emphasize targeted strength training for hip abductors alongside careful review of diuretic prescriptions, rather than relying solely on polypharmacy counts.

**Caveats:** Observational; single Australian cohort; effect sizes modest; causation cannot be inferred.

### 15. Frailty, Malnutrition Risk, and Sarcopenia Together Multiply Fall Odds

**Journal:** *European Geriatric Medicine* | **Published:** August 22, 2026
**PMID:** 42631833 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s41999-026-01590-z

**The study:** A Turkish study of 1,219 geriatric outpatients (mean age 75.5) assessed frailty, malnutrition risk, and probable sarcopenia, then examined their association with self-reported falls in the previous year. Having any two of the three conditions more than doubled the odds of reporting a fall (adjusted odds ratio 1.94); having all three nearly tripled the odds. Polypharmacy was included as a covariate in sensitivity analyses but was not a primary focus.

**Why it matters:** Older adults with overlapping frailty, poor nutrition, and muscle weakness face compounding fall risk—highlighting the value of comprehensive geriatric assessment.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you've been told you're frail, at risk for malnutrition, or losing muscle, take extra precautions to prevent falls—and consider asking about nutrition support and exercise programs.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Geriatric clinics should screen for all three conditions together; addressing nutrition and muscle strength alongside frailty may yield greater fall reduction than tackling any single factor.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design (cannot establish causation); self-reported falls subject to recall bias; single outpatient clinic population.

### 16. Older Adults with Heart Failure Receive Less Guideline-Based Therapy—Even After Accounting for Kidney Function

**Journal:** *European Geriatric Medicine* | **Published:** July 14, 2026
**PMID:** 42446811 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s41999-026-01553-4

**The study:** German researchers analyzed medication use among 1,281 adults aged 70 and older with stage C or D heart failure from the MyoVasc cohort. Compared with younger adults with similar heart failure severity, older patients were significantly less likely to receive aldosterone antagonists and beta-blockers—therapies proven to improve survival—even after adjusting for kidney function and comorbidities. Instead, they were more likely to receive calcium channel blockers, which are not guideline-recommended for heart failure.

**Why it matters:** Age-related underuse of evidence-based heart failure medications may contribute to worse outcomes in older adults, suggesting a need for more assertive prescribing and monitoring.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have heart failure, ask your doctor whether you're receiving all the recommended medications—some effective treatments may be under-prescribed simply because of your age.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Cardiologists and geriatricians should review guideline-directed therapy in older heart failure patients and consider whether age-related concerns are inadvertently limiting access to beneficial drugs.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional observational study; single German cohort; unmeasured patient preferences may influence prescribing.

### 17. Statins and Cognition in Older Adults: Current Evidence Does Not Support Stopping Therapy

**Journal:** *GeroScience* | **Published:** April 20, 2026 (online)
**PMID:** 42010230 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11357-026-02259-y

**The study:** A narrative review synthesized evidence from randomized trials, observational studies, meta-analyses, and Mendelian randomization analyses on the cognitive effects of statins in older adults. Most trials found no harmful effect on cognition; some observational studies suggested possible protective associations. Two large ongoing trials—PREVENTABLE and STAREE—are expected to provide definitive answers.

**Why it matters:** Amid ongoing concern about statins and memory, current evidence does not justify stopping these cardiovascular drugs solely to protect cognition—but individualized decisions remain important for frail patients.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you've heard that statins cause memory problems, know that most research doesn't support that claim; talk to your doctor before making any changes to your medication.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Clinicians should reassure patients that evidence does not support discontinuing statins for cognitive reasons, while remaining attentive to polypharmacy and frailty when weighing overall risk-benefit.

**Caveats:** Narrative review (not a systematic review or meta-analysis); definitive data from large trials still pending; older adults remain underrepresented in many existing studies.

### 18. App-Based Medication Reminders Plus Telehealth Improve Adherence in African American Older Adults

**Journal:** *Senior Care Pharmacist* | **Published:** June 1, 2026
**PMID:** 42288945 | **DOI:** 10.4140/TCP.n.2026.139

**The study:** A six-month pilot in Houston enrolled 40 African American adults aged 65 and older taking five or more medications. Participants used a smartphone medication reminder app and received two pharmacist-led telehealth medication therapy management sessions. Self-reported medication knowledge improved significantly, the proportion forgetting medications dropped from 74% to 44%, and use of health technology rose from 14% to 68%.

**Why it matters:** Combining digital tools with pharmacist support can meaningfully improve medication adherence in a population facing disproportionate polypharmacy-related risks.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** A simple smartphone app paired with a pharmacist phone call may help you keep track of your medications—even if you've never used health technology before.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Community health programs and senior centers serving African American older adults should explore digital adherence tools coupled with pharmacist outreach to address medication management challenges.

**Caveats:** Small pilot study (N=40); no control group; predominantly female sample; self-reported outcomes.

### 19. Digital Twins Hold Promise for Personalized Geriatric Care—but Major Hurdles Remain

**Journal:** *Archives of Gerontology and Geriatrics* | **Published:** August 3, 2026 (online)
**PMID:** 42570533 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.archger.2026.106371

**The study:** A narrative review examined the potential of "digital twins"—continuously updated virtual replicas of individual patients—in geriatric care. Applications discussed include cardiovascular monitoring, fall prevention, dementia management, and polypharmacy optimization. The authors identified data privacy, interoperability, computational costs, and ethical concerns around consent in cognitively impaired patients as key barriers.

**Why it matters:** While digital twin technology could enable highly personalized, predictive care for older adults, significant technical and ethical challenges must be addressed before widespread adoption.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** In the future, a "digital twin" of your health data could help doctors predict problems before they happen—but privacy protections and practical systems still need development.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living technology planners and health system innovators should monitor digital twin advances, particularly for polypharmacy and fall-risk applications, while advocating for robust privacy and consent frameworks.

**Caveats:** Narrative review (no original data); most applications remain conceptual or early-stage; ethical and regulatory standards not yet established.

### 20. ARB Blood Pressure Medications Linked to Lower Sarcopenia Rates in Hospitalized Seniors

**Journal:** *Aging Clinical and Experimental Research* | **Published:** July 8, 2026
**PMID:** 42414787 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40520-026-03452-x

**The study:** Turkish researchers assessed 206 hospitalized patients aged 65 and older for sarcopenia using standardized criteria. While polypharmacy was common in both sarcopenic and non-sarcopenic groups (no significant difference), use of angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) was significantly more frequent among those without sarcopenia (40% vs. 15%). In multivariate analysis, ARB use remained independently associated with lower odds of sarcopenia.

**Why it matters:** This finding adds to emerging evidence that ARBs may have muscle-protective effects—potentially relevant when choosing blood pressure medications for older adults at risk of muscle loss.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you take blood pressure medication and are concerned about muscle loss, ask your doctor whether an ARB-type drug might be appropriate for you.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Clinicians managing hypertension in frail or sarcopenic older patients may consider ARBs as a preferred class, pending confirmation from longitudinal studies.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design (cannot prove causation); small sample (N=206); single-center study in Turkey; mechanism unclear.
