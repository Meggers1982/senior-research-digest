# Senior Living Research Digest: Sleep

This digest presents peer-reviewed research on sleep and aging, selected for relevance to older adults, their families, caregivers, and senior living professionals.

---

### 1. Poor Sleep Quality Linked to Unsteady Walking in Older Adults

**Journal:** *JMIR Aging* | **Published:** June 16, 2026
**PMID:** 42302301 | **DOI:** 10.2196/81630

**The study:** Researchers examined 72 adults (mean age 71, 51% female) using wearable sleep monitors and motion sensors during walking tests. They found that fragmented, unstable sleep was associated with greater side-to-side variability when walking—a pattern linked to falls. Better sleep quality and more REM sleep were associated with steadier gait.

**Why it matters:** Falls are a leading cause of injury in older adults. This research suggests that improving sleep quality could be one strategy to help maintain walking stability and reduce fall risk.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're experiencing restless or broken sleep, it may be worth discussing with your doctor—not just for how you feel during the day, but because sleep quality may relate to your balance and steadiness when walking.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living communities might consider incorporating sleep assessments as part of fall prevention programs, recognizing that nighttime rest may influence daytime mobility.

**Caveats:** Small sample (N=72), cross-sectional design (cannot establish causation), 90% non-Hispanic White participants (may not generalize to all populations), single-center study.

### 2. Smartphone App Shows Promise for Tracking Memory and Sleep in Those with Cognitive Concerns

**Journal:** *JMIR Aging* | **Published:** June 11, 2026
**PMID:** 42275527 | **DOI:** 10.2196/87926

**The study:** Researchers tested a smartphone app called "Sleep Memories" with 76 older adults (mean age 70) who had subjective cognitive decline or mild cognitive impairment. The app assessed memory for word-pairs before and after sleep. Over 50% completed the full testing protocol, and 81% who participated found the app acceptable.

**Why it matters:** Remote, app-based tools could help clinicians monitor how sleep affects memory consolidation in people at risk for dementia—without requiring overnight lab visits.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you've noticed memory concerns, apps that track sleep and memory at home may become useful tools for working with your healthcare provider—ask about emerging digital health options.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and care professionals, this research signals that technology-assisted memory monitoring is becoming more feasible, though participants with lower cognitive scores were less willing to participate.

**Caveats:** Small pilot study (N=76), single-site, 57% completion rate for full protocol, findings reflect feasibility rather than clinical outcomes.

### 3. Sleep Problems, Mood, and Thinking Difficulties Interconnected in Mild Cognitive Impairment

**Journal:** *Aging & Mental Health* | **Published:** June 9, 2026
**PMID:** 42265577 | **DOI:** 10.1080/13607863.2026.2683909

**The study:** Researchers assessed 127 Greek adults with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) for sleep problems (insomnia symptoms, nighttime awakenings, daytime dysfunction), psychological symptoms (depression, anxiety, stress), and cognitive status. Psychological burden was the strongest factor associated with sleep problems, but poorer cognitive status and earlier MCI onset were also linked to worse sleep.

**Why it matters:** For people with MCI, addressing depression and anxiety may be particularly important for improving sleep—and better sleep may support cognitive health.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have mild memory problems and also struggle with sleep, consider talking to your doctor about mood and stress—treating these together may help more than focusing on sleep alone.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Caregivers and clinicians should assess psychological well-being alongside sleep when caring for those with MCI, as integrated approaches may yield better outcomes.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design (cannot determine causation), single clinic sample from Greece (may not generalize broadly).

### 4. Mindfulness App Reduces Stress and Improves Sleep for Dementia Caregivers

**Journal:** *JMIR Aging* | **Published:** June 5, 2026
**PMID:** 42247635 | **DOI:** 10.2196/87316

**The study:** A smartphone-based mindfulness intervention was tested with 120 family caregivers of people with dementia in Hong Kong. The 18-day program used machine learning to deliver brief mindfulness prompts at optimal moments. After the intervention, caregivers reported significant reductions in stress, depression, and caregiver burden, plus improved sleep quality. Retention was 100%, and over 80% would recommend the app.

**Why it matters:** Family caregivers face chronic stress that harms their own health. This study shows that a brief, smartphone-delivered mindfulness program is acceptable and associated with meaningful improvements in well-being and sleep.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're caring for someone with dementia, brief mindfulness exercises delivered through your phone may help reduce stress and improve your sleep—small daily practices can add up.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living and memory care programs might explore offering similar app-based support to family caregivers, given the high acceptability and potential benefits shown here.

**Caveats:** Single-arm study with no control group (cannot confirm the intervention caused improvements), Hong Kong sample (cultural factors may limit generalizability).

### 5. Antioxidant-Rich Diet and Optimal Sleep Duration Together Linked to Better Cognitive Health

**Journal:** *Journal of Nutrition, Health & Aging* | **Published:** June 4, 2026
**PMID:** 42241923 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jnha.2026.100893

**The study:** In a Chinese cohort of 4,988 adults aged 60+, researchers followed participants for 10 years. Those with the highest "oxidative balance score" (reflecting antioxidant-rich diet and healthy lifestyle) had 22% lower odds of cognitive impairment. Both short and long sleep duration were associated with higher odds of cognitive problems. Combining poor oxidative balance with abnormal sleep showed the strongest association with cognitive decline.

**Why it matters:** This large, long-term study suggests that both what you eat and how you sleep may matter for protecting brain health as you age—and the combination may be especially important.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Eating plenty of antioxidant-rich foods (like fruits, vegetables, and whole grains) and aiming for a regular, adequate sleep schedule may work together to support your thinking and memory as you age.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living dining programs and wellness initiatives could emphasize the brain-health connection between nutrition and sleep, encouraging residents to address both.

**Caveats:** Observational design (cannot prove causation), self-reported sleep, single geographic region in China.

### 6. Sleep EEG Patterns May Help Identify Alzheimer's Disease Subtypes

**Journal:** *GeroScience* | **Published:** June 6, 2026
**PMID:** 42249255 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11357-026-02266-z

**The study:** Researchers analyzed overnight sleep EEG recordings from 42 patients with mild-to-moderate Alzheimer's disease and 58 cognitively healthy controls in Spain. Using machine learning, they identified three distinct subgroups among patients based on sleep brainwave patterns, which corresponded to different cerebrospinal fluid biomarker profiles related to Alzheimer's pathology.

**Why it matters:** Sleep studies could eventually offer a non-invasive window into the biological processes driving Alzheimer's disease, potentially helping personalize treatment approaches.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Sleep studies are increasingly being explored as a way to understand brain health. If you or a loved one has Alzheimer's concerns, ongoing research may eventually offer new tools for diagnosis and monitoring.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Clinicians and researchers should monitor developments in sleep-based biomarkers for Alzheimer's, as these could complement existing diagnostic approaches in memory care settings.

**Caveats:** Small sample (N=42 patients), single-center, exploratory analysis requiring validation in larger studies.

### 7. Combining Sleep and Exercise Interventions for Dementia Prevention: A New Trial Design

**Journal:** *GeroScience* | **Published:** June 6, 2026
**PMID:** 42249253 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11357-026-02337-1

**The study:** Researchers published the protocol for the CASE trial (Combating Alzheimer's through Sleep and Exercise), which will randomize 60 adults aged 55-80 (with and without mild cognitive impairment) to receive sleep-only, exercise-only, or combined interventions over 8 weeks. The study uses wearable technology and digital tools to deliver and monitor interventions.

**Why it matters:** This trial will test whether improving sleep and increasing physical activity together produces greater cognitive benefits than either alone—a promising approach to dementia prevention.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Researchers are actively testing whether combining better sleep habits with regular exercise could help protect your brain. Stay tuned for results, and in the meantime, both habits are worth cultivating.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living communities might consider how wellness programming can integrate sleep hygiene education with physical activity, anticipating evidence that combined approaches may be most effective.

**Caveats:** This is a study protocol—results are not yet available. Small sample size (N=60), single-site, exploratory design.

### 8. Sleep Disturbance Is a Key Factor in Depression Risk for Hospitalized Older Adults

**Journal:** *Clinical Interventions in Aging* | **Published:** May 15, 2026
**PMID:** 42165005 | **DOI:** 10.2147/CIA.S599044

**The study:** Researchers developed a screening tool for depression in 11,269 hospitalized patients aged 60+ in China. Sleep disturbance emerged as one of eight key factors—alongside gender, age, marital status, income source, chronic disease, pain, and self-rated health—that predicted depressive symptoms with high accuracy.

**Why it matters:** Nearly 29% of older inpatients showed depressive symptoms. A simple screening tool that includes sleep could help identify at-risk patients quickly, enabling earlier intervention.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're hospitalized and struggling with sleep, let your care team know—it may be a signal that you're at higher risk for depression and could benefit from extra support.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Hospital staff and senior care professionals should recognize sleep disturbance as a red flag for depression in older inpatients, prompting further assessment.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design, single hospital system in China, requires external validation.

### 9. Music Therapy Improves Cognition and Reduces Depression in Healthy Older Adults

**Journal:** *The Journals of Gerontology: Series B* | **Published:** May 7, 2026
**PMID:** 42161569 | **DOI:** 10.1093/geronb/gbag070

**The study:** In a randomized controlled trial, 62 cognitively healthy older adults (mean age ~80) received 20 weeks of structured music therapy—either one-to-one or in groups—while a control group received no intervention. All music therapy formats improved cognitive function (memory, attention, executive function) and reduced depressive symptoms, while the control group showed cognitive decline.

**Why it matters:** Music therapy, whether individual or group-based, may help maintain thinking skills and mood in healthy older adults—an accessible, enjoyable intervention.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Participating in music therapy—whether alone with a therapist or in a group—may help keep your mind sharp and lift your mood. Consider exploring music programs in your community.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living communities should consider offering structured music therapy as part of wellness programming, given evidence that it supports cognitive health and emotional well-being.

**Caveats:** Small sample (N=62), no active control condition (effects could partly reflect social engagement), limited diversity information.

### 10. Black Dementia Caregivers More Vulnerable to Sleep Problems from Role Overload

**Journal:** *The Journals of Gerontology: Series B* | **Published:** May 12, 2026
**PMID:** 42121379 | **DOI:** 10.1093/geronb/gbag088

**The study:** Using national data from 288 Black and non-Hispanic White caregivers of people with dementia, researchers found that feeling overwhelmed by caregiving (role overload) was associated with more sleep disturbance. This relationship was significantly stronger among Black caregivers than White caregivers.

**Why it matters:** Black caregivers may experience greater sleep harm from caregiving stress, highlighting the need for culturally tailored support.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're caring for someone with dementia and feeling overwhelmed, your sleep may be suffering. Seeking help—whether from family, community, or professionals—may protect your own health.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Healthcare providers and senior living professionals should recognize that Black caregivers may face heightened sleep vulnerability and could benefit from targeted respite and support services.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional data (cannot confirm causation), self-reported measures, limited to two racial groups.

### 11. Digital Health Monitoring Helps Low-Income Older Adults Manage Pain

**Journal:** *JMIR Aging* | **Published:** April 20, 2026
**PMID:** 42061838 | **DOI:** 10.2196/85611

**The study:** In a 6-week pilot in South Korea, 86 low-income older adults with chronic pain used a digital monitoring platform (smartwatch, motion sensors, chatbot surveys) integrated with community care services. The intervention group maintained stable pain-related function while the comparison group worsened. Elevated heart rate patterns and sleep disturbances predicted worse pain and depressive symptoms.

**Why it matters:** Combining digital health tools with human community care may help vulnerable older adults manage pain and maintain function—a scalable model for underserved populations.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Wearable devices and simple check-in apps may help you and your care team spot changes in your sleep or activity that relate to pain—technology can be a partner in managing chronic conditions.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Community care programs serving low-income seniors could explore integrating digital monitoring to identify health changes early and trigger timely interventions.

**Caveats:** Quasi-experimental design (not randomized), short duration, South Korean context may limit generalizability.

### 12. Unstable Sleep Patterns Over Time Linked to Higher Stroke Risk

**Journal:** *JMIR Aging* | **Published:** May 28, 2026
**PMID:** 42208063 | **DOI:** 10.2196/78914

**The study:** Researchers tracked sleep duration patterns over time in 5,603 participants from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study. An "inverted U-shaped" sleep trajectory—where sleep duration initially increased then decreased—was associated with 34% higher risk of hypertension and, for nighttime sleep specifically, 110% higher stroke risk compared to those with stable high sleep. Hypertension partially explained the stroke connection.

**Why it matters:** It's not just how much you sleep on any given night—consistency of sleep patterns over time may matter for heart and brain health.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Aim for consistent sleep habits over the years. If your sleep patterns have changed significantly, discuss this with your doctor, as it may relate to your cardiovascular health.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living wellness programs might monitor residents' long-term sleep patterns, as shifts over time could signal increased cardiovascular risk warranting clinical attention.

**Caveats:** Observational design (cannot prove causation), self-reported sleep, Chinese population.

### 13. Sleep, Self-Efficacy, and Depression All Relate to Cognitive Function in Chinese Elders

**Journal:** *Clinical Interventions in Aging* | **Published:** May 20, 2026
**PMID:** 42199483 | **DOI:** 10.2147/CIA.S594997

**The study:** In a cross-sectional survey of 2,030 older adults in Ningxia, China, poor sleep quality was associated with lower cognitive function. Self-efficacy (confidence in handling daily challenges) appeared to be involved in this relationship, and depressive symptoms strengthened the negative association between low self-efficacy and poorer cognition.

**Why it matters:** Sleep, mood, and confidence in managing daily life may all contribute to thinking abilities in older adults—addressing any one of these could potentially support cognitive health.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Good sleep, feeling capable in your daily life, and managing low mood may all be pieces of the puzzle for keeping your mind sharp. Talk to your doctor if you're struggling in any of these areas.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Care providers should consider sleep, mood, and self-efficacy together when assessing cognitive health in older adults, as these factors appear interconnected.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design (cannot establish causation), single region in China, self-reported measures.

### 14. Restricting Daytime Naps for a Year May Slow Memory Decline

**Journal:** *GeroScience* | **Published:** April 13, 2026
**PMID:** 41973339 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11357-026-02240-9

**The study:** In a randomized trial, 58 healthy older habitual nappers (ages 59-82) in Belgium were assigned to either continue their usual napping or receive coaching to reduce nap frequency and duration for 12 months. A group of non-nappers served as reference. The nap-control group showed significant decline in verbal memory recall, while the nap-intervention group maintained performance similar to non-nappers.

**Why it matters:** Frequent or prolonged daytime napping has been linked to cognitive decline. This study suggests that reducing nap habits is achievable and may help preserve memory in healthy older adults.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you nap often during the day, consider whether reducing napping might help maintain your memory. Brief coaching can help change this habit—discuss options with your healthcare provider.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living communities might explore behavioral programs to help residents modify excessive napping, as this could be a modifiable factor in cognitive health.

**Caveats:** Small sample (N=58 nappers), single-country study, results may not apply to those who nap due to medical conditions.

### 15. Loneliness and Sleep Problems Together Hit Mental Health Harder

**Journal:** *Aging & Mental Health* | **Published:** April 20, 2026
**PMID:** 42003598 | **DOI:** 10.1080/13607863.2026.2659780

**The study:** Using two waves of data from nearly 10,000 UK adults aged 50+, researchers found that loneliness was associated with poorer mental health, as were multiple dimensions of sleep problems (poor quality, medication use, disturbance, daytime dysfunction, long time to fall asleep, short duration). Crucially, the combination of loneliness and sleep problems was associated with even worse mental health than either alone.

**Why it matters:** Loneliness and poor sleep often co-occur in later life and may reinforce each other's negative effects on well-being. Addressing both together could be more effective than targeting either in isolation.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're feeling lonely and also struggling with sleep, know that these often go hand-in-hand and can affect your mood. Reaching out for social connection and addressing sleep problems may both help.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living staff should be alert to residents experiencing both loneliness and sleep difficulties, as this combination may signal elevated mental health risk requiring coordinated intervention.

**Caveats:** Observational design, self-reported measures, UK population.

### 16. Retinal Thickness and Sleep Quality Together Predict Cognitive Trajectory

**Journal:** *GeroScience* | **Published:** March 26, 2026
**PMID:** 41886217 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11357-026-02159-1

**The study:** In a 7-year study of 257 Taiwanese adults aged 65+, researchers measured retinal nerve fiber thickness (a marker of neurodegeneration) and assessed sleep quality and daytime sleepiness. Thicker retinal layers predicted better cognitive performance over time, and this association was strongest in those with good sleep quality and without excessive daytime sleepiness.

**Why it matters:** Retinal imaging combined with sleep assessment could help identify older adults at risk for cognitive decline, offering opportunities for early intervention.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Regular eye exams may reveal more than just vision problems—they could provide clues about brain health. Maintaining good sleep may help protect any cognitive advantage you have.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Clinicians might consider integrating retinal assessments with sleep evaluations as part of comprehensive cognitive health monitoring in older patients.

**Caveats:** Single geographic region (Taiwan), relatively small sample, observational design.

### 17. Lifestyle Factors Explain Some—But Not All—of the Link Between Mental Health and Memory Complaints

**Journal:** *Aging & Mental Health* | **Published:** April 28, 2026
**PMID:** 42051031 | **DOI:** 10.1080/13607863.2026.2656223

**The study:** Researchers analyzed data from over 5,000 dementia-free adults aged 60+ using a mobile health app in the Czech Republic. Hearing problems, depression, and perceived stress were strongly associated with more subjective memory complaints. Physical activity, mental activity, healthy diet, and optimal sleep were all associated with fewer complaints—but lifestyle only partially explained the mental health-memory link.

**Why it matters:** Healthy lifestyle habits are associated with fewer memory complaints, but mental health appears to have an independent relationship with subjective cognitive concerns.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Staying physically and mentally active, eating well, and sleeping enough are all associated with fewer memory complaints. But if you're stressed or down, addressing your mental health may be just as important.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior care providers should address both lifestyle and mental health when older adults report memory concerns, as each appears to contribute independently.

**Caveats:** App-based sample (may not represent general population), cross-sectional design, self-reported outcomes.

### 18. Relationship Quality and Sleep Influence Each Other Over Time in Older Couples

**Journal:** *The Journals of Gerontology: Series B* | **Published:** April 10, 2026
**PMID:** 41967084 | **DOI:** 10.1093/geronb/gbag067

**The study:** Using 8 years of data from 2,343 couples aged 50+ in England, researchers found that individuals' sleep quality and feelings of support from their spouse were positively linked. For women, increases in feeling strained by their spouse predicted later declines in sleep. Women's increased perception of spousal support predicted later improvements in men's sleep.

**Why it matters:** Sleep and relationship quality are intertwined in older couples. Supporting marital health may benefit sleep, and vice versa.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** How you get along with your partner can affect your sleep—and your sleep can affect your relationship. Open communication about both may help you support each other's well-being.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living programs that offer couples counseling or relationship enrichment may indirectly benefit residents' sleep health.

**Caveats:** Observational design, UK sample, self-reported measures.

### 19. Becoming a Grandparent Affects Sleep—Especially for Men

**Journal:** *The Journals of Gerontology: Series B* | **Published:** May 7, 2026
**PMID:** 42033775 | **DOI:** 10.1093/geronb/gbag077

**The study:** Analyzing data from over 6,000 adults in China over 12 years, researchers found that becoming a grandparent was associated with reduced nighttime sleep duration, particularly for men. Declines persisted for up to 9 years after the transition. Effects were concentrated among maternal grandparents and varied by employment and living arrangement.

**Why it matters:** Grandparenthood brings joy but also new responsibilities that may affect sleep. Understanding these patterns can help families plan for support.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you've recently become a grandparent and notice changes in your sleep, you're not alone—this transition often affects rest. Don't hesitate to set boundaries and ask for help.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Families and senior care providers should be aware that new grandparents, especially grandfathers, may experience sleep disruption and could benefit from support during this life transition.

**Caveats:** Chinese sample (cultural factors may limit generalizability), self-reported sleep, observational design.

### 20. Social Isolation and Loneliness Common Among Older Adults with Heart Disease

**Journal:** *Aging Clinical and Experimental Research* | **Published:** April 20, 2026
**PMID:** 42010225 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40520-026-03379-3

**The study:** Researchers surveyed 432 older adults with coronary heart disease in western Iran, assessing loneliness, social isolation, and social support. Sleep duration was among the factors significantly associated with these outcomes. Strong correlations existed among all three measures, indicating that those with less support also experienced more loneliness and isolation.

**Why it matters:** Older adults with heart disease are at heightened risk for social isolation and loneliness, which in turn affect physical and mental health. Sleep is one of several factors linked to these outcomes.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have heart disease and feel lonely or isolated, reaching out for social support may benefit both your emotional and physical health—including your sleep.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Healthcare providers caring for cardiac patients should screen for loneliness and social isolation, recognizing their connection to sleep and overall well-being.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design, single hospital in Iran, convenience sample.

### 21. Cognitive Reserve and Lifestyle Protect Executive Function in Healthy Aging

**Journal:** *Aging Clinical and Experimental Research* | **Published:** March 26, 2026
**PMID:** 41886157 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40520-026-03362-y

**The study:** In 204 cognitively healthy Italian older adults, researchers assessed cognitive reserve (education, occupational complexity, mental activities), physical activity, diet, and sleep duration. Those who adopted all four healthy lifestyle behaviors scored higher in executive function than 65% of the sample. Benefits of physical activity and diet were strongest among those with lower cognitive reserve, suggesting a compensatory effect.

**Why it matters:** Healthy lifestyle habits may help compensate for lower educational or occupational background in protecting thinking skills—it's never too late to benefit.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Staying active, eating well, and sleeping enough may be especially protective for your thinking skills if you didn't have advanced education or a complex career. Every healthy habit counts.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living wellness programming should emphasize that lifestyle interventions can benefit all residents, perhaps especially those with less formal education.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design, Italian sample, remote assessment during pandemic may limit generalizability.

### 22. Sleep Health in Older Adults: A Comprehensive Review

**Journal:** *Ageing Research Reviews* | **Published:** March 11, 2026
**PMID:** 41825783 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.arr.2026.103101

**The study:** This narrative review synthesizes research on how sleep changes with age, including reduced slow-wave and REM sleep, increased fragmentation, and circadian rhythm shifts. It covers common sleep disorders in older adults (insomnia affects 20-40%; obstructive sleep apnea affects over 30%) and their associations with cognitive decline, cardiovascular disease, falls, and mortality.

**Why it matters:** Understanding what's normal versus problematic in older adult sleep helps individuals and providers distinguish between changes to accept and those requiring treatment.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Some sleep changes are a normal part of aging, but persistent problems like chronic insomnia or loud snoring deserve attention. Effective treatments—including non-drug options—exist for many sleep disorders.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living staff should be trained to recognize signs of common sleep disorders in residents and facilitate appropriate referrals, as untreated sleep problems have significant health consequences.

**Caveats:** Narrative review (not a systematic analysis), summarizes existing evidence rather than presenting new data.
