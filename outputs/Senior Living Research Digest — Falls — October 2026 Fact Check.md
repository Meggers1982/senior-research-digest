### Study 2: Sedatives Are Finally Fading After Hip Fractures — But Opioid Prescribing Has Barely Budged
**PMID:** 42852585 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Survivorship exclusion misstated** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Analysis excluded people who died within roughly three months of discharge."
- **Abstract says:** "The cohort included individuals aged 66 years and older... and who survived for at least 275 days in the following year."
- **Problem:** The cohort required survival of at least 275 days (about nine months), so the exclusion is substantially broader than 'roughly three months,' which understates the selection restriction and potential survivorship bias.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change to: 'Analysis included only people who survived at least 275 days (roughly nine months) in the year after admission, excluding those who died sooner.'"

**Issue B. 'Barely budged' versus reported decline** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Opioid dispensing, however, peaked at 55.8% in 2015 and still stood at 44.6% in 2024"
- **Abstract says:** "opioids dispensing peaked in 2015 at 55.8% and decreased to 44.6% in 2024... opioids dispensing remained consistently high."
- **Problem:** The headline phrase 'barely budged' implies little change, whereas dispensing fell about 11 percentage points from its peak; the abstract characterizes it as 'remained consistently high' rather than unchanged.
- **Suggested fix:** "Rephrase the headline to 'But Opioid Prescribing Remains High' to match the abstract's wording."

### Study 3: Hearing, Vision and Mood — Not Just Knees — Drive Fall Risk in People With Knee Arthritis
**PMID:** 42759165 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Causal verb in headline for a prediction model** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Hearing, Vision and Mood — Not Just Knees — Drive Fall Risk in People With Knee Arthritis"
- **Abstract says:** "Sensory impairments (hearing and vision problems) and depressive symptoms were the most influential predictors of falls."
- **Problem:** 'Drive' suggests these factors cause falls, while the study only identified them as the most influential predictors in a machine learning model built on observational cohort data.
- **Suggested fix:** "Use 'Flag' or 'Predict' instead of 'Drive' (e.g., 'Hearing, Vision and Mood — Not Just Knees — Predict Fall Risk')."

### Study 5: Bed and Chair Alarms Don't Prevent Falls — So Why Are Hospitals Still Using Them?
**PMID:** 42833806 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Categorical efficacy claim in headline** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Bed and Chair Alarms Don't Prevent Falls — So Why Are Hospitals Still Using Them?"
- **Abstract says:** "The review covers the evidence for fall prevention alarms and other fall prevention practices, factors that contribute to overuse of fall prevention alarms, and strategies that are being tested to de-implement fall prevention alarms."
- **Problem:** The abstract describes a review of the evidence and of overuse; it does not state a flat conclusion that alarms do not prevent falls. The headline asserts more than the abstract supports.
- **Suggested fix:** "Soften to 'Bed and Chair Alarms Have Weak Evidence Behind Them — So Why Are Hospitals Still Using Them?'"

**Issue B. Harms of alarms not in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Alarms are noisy, disrupt sleep, can discourage patients from moving, and contribute to alarm fatigue among staff"
- **Abstract says:** "The abstract mentions only overuse, low-value care and de-implementation science; no specific harms are listed."
- **Problem:** Unverifiable from abstract only — the specific harms attributed to alarms do not appear in the abstract text.
- **Suggested fix:** "Attribute these harms generally ('commonly cited concerns about alarms include...') or cite the full text."

**Issue C. Comparative evidence claim for alternatives** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "supervised walking, a toileting schedule, a medication review, good lighting, and proper footwear have better support than an alarm does"
- **Abstract says:** "The review covers the evidence for fall prevention alarms and other fall prevention practices."
- **Problem:** Unverifiable from abstract only — the abstract does not name alternative practices or rank their evidence relative to alarms.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say the review also examines other fall prevention practices, without specifying which have stronger evidence, unless sourced from the full text."

### Study 6: How Fast You Walk in Your 50s May Predict Falls Decades Later

### Study 9: Uneven Ground Is Where Walking Gets Risky — and Brain Scans Show Why
**PMID:** 42679689 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Screening recommendation beyond study scope** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "supports using a simple in-office physical performance test to identify who needs extra caution on uneven ground"
- **Abstract says:** "Older adults were classified as higher- or lower-functioning based on Short Physical Performance Battery scores... Lower-functioning older adults showed greater baseline gait variability and steeper increases in variability with increasing terrain unevenness."
- **Problem:** The SPPB was used to stratify the sample, not evaluated as a screening tool for terrain-related instability or falls; the abstract reports no predictive or clinical-utility analysis.
- **Suggested fix:** "Rephrase to note that people with lower scores on a standard physical performance test showed more instability on uneven terrain in this lab study, without implying validated screening use."

### Study 11: Visual Hallucinations From Sight Loss Can Cause Falls — and Few Clinicians Are Looking for Them
**PMID:** 42833804 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline states causation the review only suggests** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Visual Hallucinations From Sight Loss Can Cause Falls — and Few Clinicians Are Looking for Them"
- **Abstract says:** "recent work has highlighted that CBS may also lead to physical outcomes by causing and increasing susceptibility to falls"
- **Problem:** The abstract hedges with 'may also lead to'; the headline presents the causal link as established, even though the piece is a narrative review with no new data.
- **Suggested fix:** "Soften to 'Visual Hallucinations From Sight Loss May Contribute to Falls — and Few Clinicians Are Looking for Them'."

**Issue B. 'Intact cognition' not in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "people with declining vision experience visual hallucinations — seeing patterns, figures or objects that aren't there — despite intact cognition"
- **Abstract says:** "Charles Bonnet Syndrome (CBS) refers to visual hallucinations associated with a decline in vision."
- **Problem:** The abstract does not state that cognition is intact in CBS; this is clinically conventional background but unverifiable from the abstract alone.
- **Suggested fix:** "Attribute the detail to general CBS definitions or drop 'despite intact cognition'."

**Issue C. Unverified claim about patient silence/dementia fear** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Older adults with macular degeneration, glaucoma or other vision loss often stay silent about hallucinations for fear of being labeled as having dementia"
- **Abstract says:** "As awareness of CBS among sight loss and falls prevention specialists is low, its role in the sight loss and falls mechanism may be missed."
- **Problem:** The abstract attributes under-recognition to low specialist awareness, not to patients withholding symptoms for fear of a dementia label. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Rephrase to reflect the abstract's point about low clinician awareness, or label the patient-silence point as background."

### Study 12: Hospital Fall Risk Changes by the Day — and a One-Time Admission Check May Miss It
**PMID:** 42842205 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Ambiguous timing of acute functional decline finding** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "a sudden functional decline predicted falls by day 7"
- **Abstract says:** "Acute functional decline was associated with higher fall risk at LM7 but not LM3."
- **Problem:** The abstract means the association was present in the day-7 landmark analysis (falls within 30 days after day 7), not that falls occurred 'by day 7'. As written, readers may misread the follow-up window.
- **Suggested fix:** "Rewrite as 'a sudden functional decline was linked to later falls in the day-7 assessment, but not at day 3'."

### Study 15: Persistent Depression in Later Life Predicts Faster Decline Into Frailty
**PMID:** 42822543 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Causal framing in 'Why it matters'** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "chronically untreated depressive symptoms — not a single bad patch — are a measurable driver of that slide"
- **Abstract says:** "Persistent high burden thus represents a reproducible risk marker of accelerated frailty accumulation"
- **Problem:** The authors explicitly call it a risk marker, not a cause, and treatment status was not measured. 'Untreated ... driver' implies causation and an intervention effect the data cannot support, and conflicts with the digest's own caveat.
- **Suggested fix:** "Rephrase to 'persistently high depressive symptoms are a reproducible warning sign of faster frailty accumulation'."

**Issue B. Cohort heterogeneity omitted** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "The persistent high burden group ... accumulated frailty significantly faster over subsequent years and had elevated odds of becoming frail (odds ratios 1.29 to 2.75)."
- **Abstract says:** "consistently predicted faster non-cognitive frailty accumulation in the three cohorts amenable to landmark analysis (HRS, ELSA, SHARE) ... with a positive but concurrent longitudinal association in CHARLS ... significantly elevated odds of incident frailty (FI ≥ 0.25) in all three cohorts (OR 1.29-2.75)"
- **Problem:** The prospective (landmark) result came from three of the four cohorts; in CHARLS the association was concurrent, not temporal. The outcome was also a non-cognitive frailty index.
- **Suggested fix:** "Note that the faster-accumulation finding came from HRS, ELSA and SHARE, with only a concurrent association in CHARLS."

### Study 16: A New National Blueprint Says Falls Prevention Can't Be Left to Doctors Alone

This article presents the plan's strategies with recommendations for incorporation into clinical practice and partnership with community organizations."

- **Suggested fix:** "Use the sectors named in the abstract and say the plan recommends clinical-community partnerships rather than 'specific referral pathways'."

### Study 20: A Subtle Change in Stride Flags Stroke Survivors Who Are About to Stumble
**PMID:** 42696957 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline implies prediction of future stumbles** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "A Subtle Change in Stride Flags Stroke Survivors Who Are About to Stumble"
- **Abstract says:** "157 subacute stroke survivors were included, comprising 82 participants with near-falls during gait analysis and 75 without near-falls ... Reduced TLA and greater trunk rotational excursion were independently associated with near-falls during walking"
- **Problem:** Near-falls were observed during the same gait testing session, so the design is cross-sectional discrimination, not prediction of future events as 'about to stumble' suggests.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to 'A Subtle Change in Stride Distinguishes Stroke Survivors Who Nearly Fall'."

### Study 21: Newer Sleep Drugs Don't Appear to Raise Fall Risk — But the Evidence Is Still Thin
**PMID:** 42799573 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Causal framing of background claim** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Traditional sleeping pills such as benzodiazepines and 'Z-drugs' are a well-documented cause of falls and hip fractures in older adults"
- **Abstract says:** "Falls are the leading cause of injury in older adults. Sedative-hypnotic use is a known risk factor, but their use for insomnia continues to be common."
- **Problem:** The abstract characterizes sedative-hypnotics as a known risk factor, not an established cause; the digest upgrades observational risk association to causation for the comparator drug classes.
- **Suggested fix:** "Rephrase as "Traditional sleeping pills such as benzodiazepines and 'Z-drugs' are a well-documented risk factor for falls and hip fractures in older adults"."
