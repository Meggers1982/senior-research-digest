### Study 1: Hearing Loss Plus Multiple Chronic Conditions Doubles Death Risk in Adults Over 70
**PMID:** 42728658 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Borderline significance of hearing-loss-only estimate** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "hearing loss alone raised mortality risk (adjusted HR 1.4)"
- **Abstract says:** "Risk of mortality increased independently with hearing loss (aHR = 1.4 95% CI = 1.0-2.0"
- **Problem:** The confidence interval touches 1.0, so the hearing-loss-only association is at best marginal; the digest presents it as an established increase and the caveat only mentions the comorbidity-only estimate crossing 1.0.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add the interval, e.g. "hearing loss alone was associated with a modest, borderline increase in mortality risk (adjusted HR 1.4, 95% CI 1.0-2.0)", and extend the caveat to cover this estimate too."

**Issue B. Causal verb in headline** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Hearing Loss Plus Multiple Chronic Conditions Doubles Death Risk in Adults Over 70"
- **Abstract says:** "audiometric hearing loss modified the association between comorbidity and mortality"
- **Problem:** "Doubles" implies the combination causes the extra deaths; this is a cohort association, and the abstract explicitly calls for more research on whether treating hearing loss improves survival.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to something like "Hearing Loss Plus Multiple Chronic Conditions Linked to More Than Double the Death Risk in Adults Over 70"."

### Study 2: In a 16,000-Person Cohort, People Who Said Their Hearing Was Severely Impaired Were Far More Likely to Have Dementia
**PMID:** 42744782 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Design label not stated in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Cross-sectional, observational analysis"
- **Abstract says:** "This study evaluated hearing loss (HL) as a factor associated with dementia in a large population-based cohort. We analyzed 16,270 participants from the All of Us database, including 1224 (7.5%) with dementia, matched to controls by U.S. census demographics."
- **Problem:** The abstract describes a matched population-based cohort/case-control analysis and never says cross-sectional; the design cannot be confirmed from the abstract alone.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say "observational analysis of matched cases and controls; the abstract does not specify whether exposures preceded dementia diagnosis" rather than asserting a cross-sectional design."

### Study 3: Turning Hearing Aids Down in Noisy Rooms Made Listening More Comfortable Without Hurting Speech Understanding
**PMID:** 42760273 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Unsupported background claim** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Noisy restaurants, dining rooms and family gatherings are the single most common reason people abandon their hearing aids"
- **Abstract says:** "(no data on reasons for hearing aid non-use or abandonment)"
- **Problem:** Presented as fact but not verifiable from this abstract, which reports only gain preferences, comfort and speech perception.
- **Suggested fix:** "Attribute the claim to outside literature or soften to "difficulty in noisy settings is a common complaint among hearing aid users.""

### Study 5: Review of 61 Studies Finds Hearing Aids Support Thinking Skills and Brain-Level Sound Processing
**PMID:** 42447643 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Language restriction overstated** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "English-language search only."
- **Abstract says:** "Databases ... were searched (2005-2025) using English keywords on hearing aid effects on neurocognition and auditory processing"
- **Problem:** The abstract reports English search keywords, not a restriction to English-language publications; these are different limitations.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change to "search conducted with English-language keywords; no publication-language restriction is reported.""

**Issue B. Action advice from a low-detail review** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the accumulated evidence favors acting sooner rather than later — the benefit described here is tied to early rehabilitation"
- **Abstract says:** "The present literature review emphasized the importance of early rehabilitation through hearing aid use ... Existing literature showed heterogeneous outcomes among studies"
- **Problem:** The review reports no effect sizes, quality grading or meta-analysis, so "the accumulated evidence favors acting" reads stronger than a narrative synthesis with heterogeneous outcomes supports.
- **Suggested fix:** "Soften to "the review's authors emphasize early rehabilitation, though the underlying studies varied widely in quality and results.""

### Study 8: Free Community Hearing Screenings on the Texas Border Found Over Half of Attendees Failed
**PMID:** 42446280 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Inferred accuracy of self-suspected hearing loss** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "if you've already suspected your hearing is slipping, that suspicion was right for most people in this study"
- **Abstract says:** "Among the 305 participants, 57% failed the hearing screening. A majority (64.4%) reported suspected hearing loss."
- **Problem:** The abstract gives the two percentages separately and never cross-tabulates them, so it does not show that most who suspected loss actually failed; readers could take this as validated self-assessment accuracy.
- **Suggested fix:** "Rewrite as "about two thirds of people screened already suspected they had hearing loss, and 57% failed the screening — the study did not report how well suspicion matched results.""

### Study 9: Audiologists Say Testing Your Hearing in Background Noise Is Valuable — but Half Don't Do It Routinely
**PMID:** 42735379 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Bias direction stated more firmly than the authors do** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the authors explicitly note self-selection and social desirability bias, meaning the true rate of speech-in-noise testing is likely lower than reported"
- **Abstract says:** "However, biases including self-selection bias and social desirability bias cannot be ruled out."
- **Problem:** The authors say such biases cannot be ruled out; they do not state that the true testing rate is lower. The digest converts a possibility into a likelihood.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change to "the authors note that self-selection and social desirability bias cannot be ruled out, so reported testing rates may be higher than actual practice.""

### Study 10: Nearly Everyone Says They'd Wear a Hearing Aid — What Actually Predicts It Is Knowing How They Work
**PMID:** 42447317 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Most trusted" versus "preferred" source** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Organized sessions with a medical professional — the source respondents most trusted"
- **Abstract says:** "They preferred to obtain it through "medical staff" (61.02%) or the "Internet" (54.96%)."
- **Problem:** The survey measured preferred channel for health education, not trust; the digest attributes a construct the study did not assess.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change "the source respondents most trusted" to "the source respondents most often preferred.""

### Study 11: Hospitals own simple hearing amplifiers that could transform patient conversations — but staff rarely use them
**PMID:** 42677253 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline overstatement** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Hospitals own simple hearing amplifiers that could transform patient conversations — but staff rarely use them"
- **Abstract says:** "Thematic analysis revealed that regardless of profession, PHAs were perceived as valuable yet underutilized... These findings lay the groundwork to test future interventions to promote the increased uptake of PHA devices"
- **Problem:** The study measured provider perceptions only; no patient communication outcomes were assessed, so "could transform patient conversations" is promotional language the data cannot support.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to something like "Hospital staff call simple hearing amplifiers valuable — but say they're rarely used"."

### Study 12: How hard you feel you're working to listen predicts how well you actually hear
**PMID:** 42348455 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Predictive language in headline** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "How hard you feel you're working to listen predicts how well you actually hear"
- **Abstract says:** "In general, lower levels of self-reported workload were associated with better speech recognition scores... self-reported workload may serve as a complementary tool"
- **Problem:** "Predicts" implies a directional/temporal relationship that a cross-sectional analysis cannot establish; the body text and caveats correctly describe it as an association.
- **Suggested fix:** "Use "tracks with" or "is linked to" instead of "predicts" in the headline."

### Study 13: Big Japanese study finds fish and omega-3s don't protect hearing — and turns up a surprise about saturated fat
**PMID:** 42697173 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline asserts absence of protection from cross-sectional null** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Big Japanese study finds fish and omega-3s don't protect hearing"
- **Abstract says:** "In this population with high seafood consumption, seafood and N-3 PUFA intakes were not associated with hearing thresholds."
- **Problem:** A cross-sectional null association is not the same as demonstrating lack of a protective effect; the study cannot rule out protection over time.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reframe as "finds no link between fish or omega-3 intake and measured hearing"."

### Study 14: Shop teachers spend hours a day in damaging noise — and most skip ear protection
**PMID:** 42334971 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Added qualifier "regular" exposure** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "for 22 of 27 tools listed, over 70% of teachers reported regular exposure"
- **Abstract says:** "For 22/27 tools included in the survey, over 70% of teachers reported exposure."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports exposure, not "regular" exposure; the added word implies a frequency the abstract does not specify.
- **Suggested fix:** "Drop "regular" and say "over 70% of teachers reported exposure"."

### Study 18: Hearing Loss Linked to Faster Cognitive Decline in 6,650 Chinese Elders Tracked for a Decade
**PMID:** 42753934 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Interaction generalized beyond moderate-decline group** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Notably, starting with a strong cognitive score was less protective among those with hearing loss."
- **Abstract says:** "The MMSE-by-hearing interaction was significant for moderate decline (RRR 1.252, 95% CI 1.079-1.453), indicating a weaker inverse association between baseline MMSE and moderate-decline membership among participants with hearing loss."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports a significant interaction only for the moderate-decline trajectory, not for rapid decline; the digest presents it as a general effect, and the "Why it matters" line repeats the broader version.
- **Suggested fix:** "Specify that the weaker protective effect of a high baseline MMSE was seen for moderate-decline membership."

### Study 21: Hearing Aids That Count Your Steps: What Older Users Actually Think
**PMID:** 42138544 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Motivation to monitor framed as behavior change** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "for people doing lighter exercise, this study suggests the feature can be a genuine nudge, but only if you know it exists"
- **Abstract says:** "Participants who engaged in light-intensity physical activity were more likely to be motivated to monitor daily step count using the hearing aids."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports self-described motivation to monitor steps, not any demonstrated increase in activity; 'a genuine nudge' implies a behavioral effect the study did not measure — and the digest's own caveat notes there was 'no measurement of whether step counting actually increased activity.'
- **Suggested fix:** "Rephrase to, e.g., 'participants doing lighter exercise were the most interested in tracking their steps this way, though the study did not test whether it increased their activity.'"
