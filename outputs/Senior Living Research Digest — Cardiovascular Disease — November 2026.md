### 1. Which exercise helps your heart most? It depends on the number you're trying to move

**Journal:** *Arch Gerontol Geriatr* | **Published:** 2027 Jan;152:106384
**PMID:** 42822168 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.archger.2026.106384

**The study:** Researchers pooled 57 randomized controlled trials involving 3,655 adults aged 60 and older in a network meta-analysis comparing five types of exercise: aerobic, resistance, combined, high-intensity interval training, and traditional Chinese training such as Tai Chi, Baduanjin, Wuqinxi, and Yijinjing. Each modality topped the rankings for different cardiometabolic measures: traditional Chinese training for total and LDL cholesterol, aerobic for HDL and diastolic blood pressure, combined training for systolic blood pressure and fasting glucose, resistance for triglycerides and BMI, and HIIT for HbA1c. The authors stressed that ranking position alone doesn't prove superiority, and confidence in the evidence was low or very low for several outcomes.

**Why it matters:** Older adults are routinely told to "exercise more" without guidance on which kind; this analysis suggests the choice can be matched to the specific risk factor a person is trying to improve, including gentler options like Tai Chi that performed well for cholesterol.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're working on a specific number — blood pressure, cholesterol, blood sugar, weight — it's worth asking your doctor whether your exercise routine is matched to that goal. Notably, slower practices like Tai Chi and qigong ranked at the top for cholesterol measures, so low-impact movement is not a consolation prize.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For activity directors and wellness staff in senior living, the findings support offering a varied menu rather than a single default class, and give a rationale for keeping Tai Chi and qigong programming alongside strength and walking groups.

**Caveats:** Confidence ratings were low or very low for several outcomes and some head-to-head comparisons were sparse, so rankings are provisional; the included trials varied in design, duration, and population, and results may not generalize to the frailest older adults.

### 2. Hidden plaque in the arteries is far more common than expected — and often nowhere near the heart

**Journal:** *N Engl J Med* | **Published:** 2026 Nov 5;395(18):1818-1829
**PMID:** 42670946 | **DOI:** 10.1056/NEJMoa2609059

**The study:** The REACT study enrolled 16,808 adults aged 18 to 70 in Denmark and Spain with no known cardiovascular disease and imaged their carotid, femoral, and coronary arteries using 3D vascular ultrasound and coronary CT angiography. Silent atherosclerosis was found in 57.1% of participants overall, appearing in roughly 7-9% of those aged 18-29 and rising steeply with age, with a later but sharper midlife increase in women. In younger adults, plaque was usually peripheral and limited to one artery territory; with age, plaque volume rose exponentially and multiple territories became involved. Plaque confined to the coronary arteries alone was uncommon at every age.

**Why it matters:** It shows that by later life, silent arterial plaque is the norm rather than the exception, and that the legs and neck arteries — not just the heart — are where disease typically shows up first, which has implications for how screening is done.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Feeling fine is not evidence that your arteries are clear. This is a reason to take blood pressure, cholesterol, and lifestyle conversations seriously at any age, and to ask your clinician how your overall cardiovascular risk is being estimated — but it is not, on its own, a reason to demand imaging scans.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families, the finding that disease appears decades before symptoms reframes heart health as a multigenerational conversation; adult children of residents may benefit from acting on their own risk factors now rather than waiting for symptoms.

**Caveats:** Observational cohort with no outcome follow-up reported here — prevalence of plaque is not the same as who will have a heart attack; participants were from Denmark and Spain and may not represent other populations; funded by the Novo Nordisk Foundation, with co-authors employed by Philips Healthcare.

### 3. A history of depression tracks with higher heart and metabolic disease risk 15 years later

**Journal:** *J Affect Disord* | **Published:** 2027 Jan 1;416:122580
**PMID:** 42826766 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jad.2026.122580

**The study:** Using the Netherlands Study of Depression and Anxiety, researchers followed 1,858 adults with lifetime major depressive disorder or no depression, all free of cardiometabolic disease at baseline, for 15 years. Those with major depressive disorder had a 65% higher rate of new cardiometabolic disease than controls (HR 1.65, 95% CI 1.30-2.11). Specific symptom profiles — atypical energy-related symptoms versus anhedonia versus typical depressive symptoms — showed broadly similar effect sizes, suggesting a general depression burden rather than one risky symptom pattern, though only atypical energy-related symptoms were linked to new diabetes.

**Why it matters:** It supports treating a history of depression as a standing reason for ongoing blood pressure, cholesterol, and glucose monitoring, rather than as a purely mental health issue handled separately from cardiac care.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have had depression at any point in your life, consider mentioning it when your heart and metabolic risk is being reviewed, and ask whether your blood pressure, cholesterol, and blood sugar are being tracked regularly. The link is an association, not proof that depression causes heart disease.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For care teams and families, this argues against siloing mental health history away from medical monitoring — a resident's depression record is relevant information for cardiometabolic surveillance planning.

**Caveats:** Observational design cannot establish causation; cardiometabolic outcomes were partly based on self-report and medication use; Dutch cohort that included middle-aged as well as older adults, so findings may not generalize to the oldest old; several authors report consultancy or speaker fees from pharmaceutical companies including Novo Nordisk and Johnson & Johnson.

### 4. Blood markers of cellular aging may sharpen risk estimates before heart surgery in older patients

**Journal:** *Geroscience* | **Published:** 2026 Nov 6
**PMID:** 42993449 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11357-026-02626-9

**The study:** Researchers at a single German center prospectively enrolled 131 patients aged 65 and older undergoing elective cardiac surgery and measured 19 preoperative blood markers linked to cellular senescence (the senescence-associated secretory phenotype). Two markers, IGFBP-1 and IGFBP-4, independently predicted a composite adverse outcome, performing comparably to the established EuroSCORE II risk score and improving reclassification of patients beyond both EuroSCORE II and the STS Score. However, no individual marker survived correction for multiple comparisons.

**Why it matters:** Standard surgical risk calculators are based on chronological age and clinical history; this is early evidence that a blood test reflecting biological aging could help identify which older patients are genuinely more vulnerable to a rough recovery.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you are weighing elective heart surgery, it is reasonable to ask your surgical team how your individual risk was calculated and whether anything beyond the standard score — frailty, biological aging, multimorbidity — was factored in. These particular blood tests are research tools, not something to request yet.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families helping an older relative decide about elective cardiac surgery, this highlights that published risk scores are averages; pressing for a frailty-informed, individualized discussion of recovery expectations is reasonable.

**Caveats:** Small sample (N=131), single-center, and no marker survived false discovery rate correction; the authors themselves call for external validation in larger cohorts; findings are prognostic associations, not a validated clinical test.

### 5. Osteoporosis and clogged arteries may share a biological root

**Journal:** *Bone* | **Published:** 2027 Jan;214:118097
**PMID:** 42749103 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.bone.2026.118097

**The study:** A scoping review screened 6,285 papers published between 1990 and 2025, identifying 742 relevant studies and 64 with mechanistic evidence, then built a conceptual model linking osteoporosis and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease. The proposed pathway runs from estrogen deficiency to hyperlipidemia, inflammation and oxidative stress, with calcium and phosphate released from resorbing bone circulating and depositing in arteries; the authors also describe the reverse direction, in which vascular calcification contributes to bone loss. They argue this supports screening people with osteoporosis for early cardiovascular disease, and vice versa.

**Why it matters:** Bone health and heart health are usually managed by different specialists on different schedules; if the two conditions share mechanisms, a diagnosis in one area may be a signal to look harder at the other.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have been diagnosed with osteoporosis — or with artery disease — it's worth asking your doctor whether the other condition has been assessed. This is a hypothesis built from existing research rather than a trial result, so treat it as a question to raise, not a change to make on your own.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For care coordinators, this is an argument for flagging the overlap between fracture-risk and cardiovascular-risk reviews so residents aren't screened for one while the other goes unexamined.

**Caveats:** Scoping review and conceptual model, not new primary data; draws on animal and in vitro studies alongside human research; the authors state more work is needed to define the pathways and their relative importance; no evidence presented that acting on the model improves outcomes.

### 6. Inflammation plus insulin resistance: a two-marker combination flags doubled cardiovascular risk in midlife and older adults

**Journal:** *Maturitas* | **Published:** 2026 Nov 5;215:109181
**PMID:** 42994151 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.maturitas.2026.109181

**The study:** Researchers analyzed 4,883 participants aged 45 and older from the Chinese CHARLS cohort who had cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome stages 0-3, using blood measurements taken in 2011 and 2015 to group people by inflammatory-metabolic burden (a C-reactive protein-triglyceride-glucose index) and insulin sensitivity (estimated glucose disposal rate). Over a median 5 years of follow-up, 1,195 developed cardiovascular disease. Compared with the low-inflammation, high-insulin-sensitivity group, those with intermediate inflammation and low insulin sensitivity had more than double the risk (HR 2.12, 95% CI 1.86-2.42), and the high-inflammation group had a 25% higher risk.

**Why it matters:** It suggests that routinely available blood results — inflammation, triglycerides, glucose, waist and blood pressure data — considered together and tracked over time may say more about cardiovascular risk than any single value.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Rather than fixating on one lab number, consider asking your doctor to look at the pattern across your inflammation, blood sugar, and lipid results over several years. Insulin resistance in particular carried the strongest signal here, and it is modifiable through weight, activity, and medication.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and professionals tracking a resident's chronic disease trajectory, this supports keeping a longitudinal view of lab trends rather than reacting to isolated abnormal results.

**Caveats:** Retrospective analysis of an observational cohort — cannot establish causation; Chinese population aged 45 and older, so findings may not generalize elsewhere or to the oldest old; cluster separation between phenotypes was described as modest; relatively short 5-year post-landmark follow-up.

### 7. Getting stronger, feeling better: frailty and mood changes track with heart risk in people with high blood pressure

**Journal:** *J Nutr Health Aging* | **Published:** 2026 Nov
**PMID:** 42679503 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jnha.2026.100965

**The study:** Researchers pooled data from three large aging cohorts in China, the United States and Mexico (CHARLS, HRS, MHAS), following 12,230 adults aged 50 and older who had hypertension but no cardiovascular disease at the start. Frailty and depressive symptoms were measured twice, about two years apart, and participants were tracked for a median of eight years, during which 2,996 developed heart disease or stroke. People who became frail had a 53% higher risk of cardiovascular disease, while those who recovered from frailty had 30% lower risk; a similar pattern held for depressive symptoms (27% higher risk with onset, 18% lower with remission).

**Why it matters:** It suggests frailty and low mood are not fixed states in later life — and that changes in either direction line up with meaningful differences in heart and stroke risk among the very large group of older adults living with high blood pressure.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have high blood pressure, your heart risk picture may not be captured by your numbers alone. Consider asking your clinician whether your walking speed, strength, fatigue and mood are being tracked over time, not just once — and whether exercise, nutrition or mental health support could be part of your blood pressure plan.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and senior living staff, this is a case for treating a resident's drift toward frailty or withdrawal as a potential cardiovascular signal worth reporting, and for valuing the strength and engagement programs that may help reverse that drift.

**Caveats:** Observational design — cannot establish that reversing frailty or depression causes lower heart risk; depressive symptoms were measured by self-report scales; frailty and mood assessed only twice; cohorts differ across countries, so findings may not generalize evenly.

### 8. Depression raises heart and death risk in people with type 2 diabetes — and blood sugar and cholesterol don't explain it

**Journal:** *J Affect Disord* | **Published:** 2026 Dec 15
**PMID:** 42624218 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jad.2026.122401

**The study:** Researchers analyzed records from 571,129 Chinese adults in Hong Kong with type 2 diabetes (mean age 60.4 years, 66% women) who were free of cardiovascular and end-stage kidney disease at baseline, tracked between 2000 and 2024. The 7.5% with depression had more hospital admissions and longer stays, and faced 21% higher risk of new cardiovascular disease and 30% higher risk of death. Mediation analysis showed that conventional cardiometabolic risk factors such as HbA1c, blood pressure and lipids explained only a small share of the excess risk.

**Why it matters:** For the many older adults living with type 2 diabetes, this suggests untreated depression is a heart risk in its own right — not simply a cause of poorer diet, exercise or medication adherence.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have diabetes and have been feeling persistently low, flat or hopeless, that is worth raising at your next diabetes appointment — mood screening belongs alongside your A1c and blood pressure checks, and this study suggests depression tracks with heart risk even when your numbers look controlled.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Care teams and senior living providers may want to look at whether diabetes management and mental health care actually talk to each other in their setting, since treating the metabolic numbers alone appears to leave a large part of the risk untouched.

**Caveats:** Observational database study — cannot establish causation; depression identified from diagnostic codes and antidepressant prescriptions, which likely misses undiagnosed cases; single health system in Hong Kong, so findings may not generalize; several authors report extensive pharmaceutical industry honoraria and research grants (including AstraZeneca, Boehringer Ingelheim, Novo Nordisk and others).

### 9. Getting up repeatedly at night may flag higher heart risk in men with sleep apnea

**Journal:** *Sleep Med* | **Published:** 2026 Dec
**PMID:** 42600420 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.sleep.2026.109188

**The study:** Using the Sydney Sleep Biobank, researchers examined 603 adults aged 30-74 with untreated obstructive sleep apnea (67.5% male, mean age 54) and compared cardiovascular risk scores between those who reported nocturia — waking to urinate and having sleep disturbed three or more times a week — and those who did not. Just over half reported significant nocturia, and after adjusting for confounders, nocturia was linked to 72% higher odds of being in the high cardiovascular risk category. The association held strongly in men but was not seen in women.

**Why it matters:** Night-time urination is extremely common with age and often dismissed as a nuisance or prostate issue, but in people with sleep apnea it may be a visible, no-cost clue to underlying heart and vascular risk.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you snore heavily or have been diagnosed with sleep apnea and you are up several times a night to use the bathroom, mention both symptoms together to your doctor and ask whether your overall cardiovascular risk has been assessed — don't assume the night-time trips are only a bladder problem.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For caregivers and night staff, frequent bathroom trips are usually logged as a fall risk; this study suggests the pattern is also worth passing to the medical team as a possible marker of untreated sleep apnea and heart risk.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional and observational — cannot show that nocturia causes or predicts events; nocturia was self-reported via a questionnaire item; participants were sleep-clinic patients aged 30-74, younger and more male than a typical senior population; risk measured by Framingham score rather than actual cardiovascular events.

### 10. Financial strain and discrimination show up in the blood — but mainly in older Black adults who already have heart disease

**Journal:** *Exp Gerontol* | **Published:** 2026 Nov
**PMID:** 42843435 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.exger.2026.113350

**The study:** Researchers measured 92 inflammatory proteins in blood samples from 642 urban-dwelling older Black adults (mean age 75; 80% with hypertension, 25% with type 2 diabetes) and tested how these related to reported financial burden, perceived stress and experiences of discrimination. Psychosocial stress by itself showed no significant association with inflammation. Instead, the links appeared only in combination with cardiovascular burden — for example, discrimination interacted with heart disease across 20 proteins and financial burden across 11.

**Why it matters:** It suggests that for older adults already living with heart disease, chronic social and financial stress may carry a measurable biological cost, strengthening the case for treating stress and economic hardship as part of cardiac care rather than as background noise.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you are managing heart disease while also dealing with money worries or the stress of unfair treatment, these pressures may matter to your physical health, not just your mood — it is reasonable to ask your care team about benefits counseling, social work referrals or stress-management support as part of your heart care.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living and community health professionals, the findings support screening residents with cardiac conditions for financial strain and experiences of discrimination, and building practical supports — not just clinical ones — into care plans.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional and exploratory — cannot establish timing or causation; stressors were self-reported; findings require replication and the clinical meaning of the protein changes is unknown; sample was urban-dwelling, highly educated (mean 15 years) and 80% women, limiting generalizability.

### 11. Texting and Portal Messages Nudged Thousands Back onto Their Statins

**Journal:** *JAMA* | **Published:** 2026 Nov 3;336(17):1518-1528
**PMID:** 42667613 | **DOI:** 10.1001/jama.2026.16372

**The study:** Kaiser Permanente Northern California ran a pragmatic randomized trial in 20,604 adults (mean age 54.8 years; 15.3% with established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease) who had a statin prescription but had recently stopped refilling it. Participants were randomly assigned to a secure portal message, a text message, a non-secure email, or usual communication, and non-responders were randomized a second time to repeat, switch, or stop outreach. Any digital outreach raised 14-day refill rates from 12.0% to 14.4%, a second nudge added further gains among non-responders, and switching the type of message offered no extra benefit over simply repeating it.

**Why it matters:** Statin nonadherence is one of the most common and most fixable causes of preventable heart attacks and strokes, and this trial shows that a simple, cheap, repeated reminder from a health system measurably moves the needle.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have been prescribed a statin and have let refills lapse, know that you are far from alone — and that a single reminder works for some people but a second one works for more. Consider turning on pharmacy refill alerts in your patient portal or by text, and if cost, side effects or confusion about why you are taking the drug is the real barrier, raise that explicitly at your next visit rather than quietly stopping.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living operators and health system population-health teams, this is a template worth copying: automated, sequential outreach beat usual communication, and the gains came from persistence rather than cleverness in choosing the channel. Families managing a parent's medications can apply the same lesson — one reminder is rarely enough.

**Caveats:** Outcome was short-term pharmacy refill, not actual pill-taking or cardiovascular events; follow-up was only 28 days; absolute improvements were modest (about 2-3 percentage points); mean age was mid-50s, so results may not fully generalize to the oldest adults; conducted in a single integrated health system where all participants already had digital access; several authors report extensive industry consulting and research funding, and the trial was supported in part by The Permanente Medical Group and the Garfield Memorial Fund.

### 12. Standard Heart Risk Calculators Perform Poorly in Older Women with Kidney Disease

**Journal:** *J Gen Intern Med* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 26 (online ahead of print)
**PMID:** 42934356 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11606-026-10946-x

**The study:** Researchers tested five widely used cardiovascular risk calculators — Framingham, ATP III, the ACC/AHA Pooled Cohort Equations, QRISK3, and the newer PREVENT model — in 21,548 postmenopausal women aged 50-79 from the Women's Health Initiative, including 1,095 with chronic kidney disease. Discrimination was mediocre overall and worse in the kidney disease group (C-statistics 0.57-0.67 versus 0.61-0.71 in women without CKD). Calibration was generally poor, with most models underestimating actual risk in women with CKD, and the PREVENT model underpredicting risk in women both with and without kidney disease.

**Why it matters:** These calculators drive real decisions about whether an older woman is offered a statin or blood pressure treatment, so systematic underestimation means some women at genuine risk may be told they are fine.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you are a woman past menopause — especially if you have reduced kidney function — treat the percentage your doctor's risk calculator spits out as a starting point for conversation rather than a verdict. It is reasonable to ask which model was used, whether your kidney numbers were factored in, and whether your estimate might be on the low side.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Clinicians and care coordinators should be alert that risk-score-driven protocols may systematically under-treat women with chronic kidney disease, a group that is common in senior living settings; kidney function deserves weight in prevention decisions beyond what these equations capture.

**Caveats:** Observational secondary analysis of a cohort study, so it cannot establish causation; the CKD subgroup was relatively small (1,095 women with 93-145 events); CKD was defined by a single eGFR threshold without albuminuria data; the Women's Health Initiative cohort enrolled decades ago and may not reflect current treatment patterns or population diversity; women over 79 were not represented.

### 13. Modeling Study: Newer Diabetes and Obesity Drugs Could Prevent Half a Million Heart Failure Cases

**Journal:** *JACC Heart Fail* | **Published:** 2026 Nov 2 (online ahead of print)
**PMID:** 42975393 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jchf.2026.103475

**The study:** Using a nationally representative U.S. sample of adults aged 30-79 without existing cardiovascular disease and the PREVENT risk equations, researchers projected how many heart failure cases could be averted by preventive use of SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists, and nonsteroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists. About 5.5 million Americans in that age band are projected to develop heart failure over 10 years. Targeting combination therapy to the roughly 6.8 million people with a 10-year heart failure risk of 10% or higher would prevent an estimated 498,000 cases, with 14 people needing treatment to prevent one case.

**Why it matters:** Heart failure is a leading driver of hospitalization, disability and transitions into higher levels of care among older adults, and this analysis quantifies how much of that future burden might be preventable with drugs already on pharmacy shelves.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have diabetes, obesity, or chronic kidney disease, it is worth asking your clinician what your estimated heart failure risk is and whether one of these newer medication classes is indicated for you — not as a guarantee, but as a conversation about prevention rather than waiting for symptoms.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living and health system planners, the projection cuts both ways: a large future heart failure burden is coming, and risk-based prescribing could blunt it — but only if cost, access, and monitoring capacity are addressed. Expect more residents arriving on these medications and more need for staff familiar with their side effects.

**Caveats:** This is a statistical modeling and projection study, not a trial — no one was actually treated, and benefits were extrapolated from trial effect estimates applied to population risk; the PREVENT equations themselves have been shown elsewhere to underpredict risk in some groups; the model does not account for cost, adherence, tolerability, or competing risks in the oldest adults; the senior author reports consulting relationships with dozens of pharmaceutical companies including manufacturers of the drug classes analyzed.

### 14. A Blood-Sugar-and-Fat Score Tracks Heart Risk in Older Adults with Weak Grip Strength

**Journal:** *Geriatr Gerontol Int* | **Published:** 2026 Nov;26(11):e70901
**PMID:** 42988750 | **DOI:** 10.1111/ggi.70901

**The study:** Drawing on the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study, researchers followed 607 middle-aged and older adults who had low handgrip strength (a marker of sarcopenia) between 2011 and 2015, calculating a cumulative triglyceride-glucose index and five obesity-related variants from two time points. Over follow-up, 130 participants developed cardiovascular disease. Each standard-deviation increase in the cumulative metabolic burden score was linked to a 22%-40% higher hazard of cardiovascular disease, and adding these scores to traditional risk factors modestly improved prediction (C-index 0.623-0.646 versus 0.606).

**Why it matters:** It suggests that in older adults who are already physically weakening, repeated measures of blood sugar and blood fats over time may tell more about heart risk than a single snapshot — and that muscle weakness and metabolic trouble together are a meaningful combination.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If your grip has gotten noticeably weaker — trouble opening jars, carrying groceries, or turning a key — that is worth mentioning to your doctor alongside your usual cholesterol and blood sugar results. Sustained metabolic problems over years, not just one high reading, appear to be what tracks with heart risk.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For care professionals, this reinforces the value of routine grip-strength screening as a cheap, quick flag that should prompt a closer look at metabolic and cardiovascular risk, not just a referral for strength training.

**Caveats:** Observational cohort — cannot establish that the metabolic burden causes cardiovascular disease; small sample (N=607) with only 130 events; subgroup findings in women and older strata were not supported by significant interaction tests; the improvement in prediction was modest and not externally validated; Chinese population using Asian sarcopenia cutoffs may not generalize to Western older adults; incident cardiovascular disease was identified from survey waves.

### 15. Cardiologists Make the Case for Palliative Care in Advanced Heart Disease

**Journal:** *Eur Heart J* | **Published:** 2026 Nov 6;47(41):6127-6144
**PMID:** 41879158 | **DOI:** 10.1093/eurheartj/ehag219

**The study:** A large multinational panel of cardiologists, intensivists, cardiac surgeons, and palliative medicine specialists produced a state-of-the-art review synthesizing the evidence for palliative care in advanced cardiovascular disease. They summarize evidence that early palliative care improves symptom control and quality of life, reduces psychological distress, and decreases high-intensity, low-value treatment near the end of life, and they lay out referral triggers, delivery models, and a practical algorithm covering heart failure, valve disease, pulmonary hypertension, arrhythmias, and congenital heart disease. The review also addresses advance directives and the ethics of deactivating implanted cardiac devices.

**Why it matters:** Many people now live for years with advanced heart disease carrying a heavy symptom burden, and this review makes clear that palliative care is meant to run alongside cardiac treatment — not replace it or signal giving up.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you are living with advanced heart failure or another serious heart condition, you can ask your cardiologist for a palliative care referral at any stage to help with breathlessness, fatigue, pain and planning — it does not mean stopping your heart medications. If you have a pacemaker or defibrillator, ask now what deactivation would involve and record your wishes in an advance directive.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Families and senior living staff should know that device deactivation and goals-of-care conversations are explicitly within scope and are best raised early, not during a crisis; the review notes that palliative care integration remains patchy outside heart failure clinics, so advocacy may be needed to get a referral.

**Caveats:** This is a narrative state-of-the-art review and expert synthesis rather than new primary research, so it carries no new patient data; the authors note substantial gaps in evidence outside heart failure populations and across differing health systems and cultures; recommendations are European-authored and may not map exactly onto U.S. hospice and insurance structures.

### 16. Your heart risk score may also say something about your brain

**Journal:** *Front Aging Neurosci* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 5
**PMID:** 42898366 | **DOI:** 10.3389/fnagi.2026.1874472

**The study:** Researchers analyzed brain scans and cognitive tests from 1,626 older adults in the Health and Aging Brain Study-Health Disparities (HABS-HD), a racially and ethnically diverse cohort of people who had neither dementia nor diagnosed cardiovascular disease. Each person's standard 10-year atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) risk score was compared with amyloid and tau PET imaging, cortical thickness, hippocampal volume, and white matter damage. Higher 10-year heart risk was linked to more amyloid and tau signal, thinner cortex, smaller hippocampus, more white matter damage, and lower scores in memory, executive function, processing speed, and language — even after accounting for APOE ε4 genetic status and amyloid burden.

**Why it matters:** The same number your doctor uses to decide about blood pressure or cholesterol treatment appears to track with brain changes and thinking performance, suggesting cardiovascular health and brain health are measured together rather than separately.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** The next time your clinician calculates your 10-year heart risk score, you can ask what it might also signal about your brain health — and use it as one more reason to take blood pressure, cholesterol, smoking, and diabetes management seriously. This study cannot prove that lowering the score protects memory, but it supports treating heart and brain health as one conversation.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living and care teams, this strengthens the case for integrating routine cardiovascular metrics into cognitive wellness programming, and for including historically underrepresented residents in brain-health screening — the findings come from a deliberately diverse cohort, unlike much dementia imaging research.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design — it captures one moment in time and cannot establish causation or which came first; participants were free of diagnosed cardiovascular disease and dementia, so findings may not generalize to people already diagnosed.

### 17. A five-minute artery stiffness test predicted who died over the next 13 years

**Journal:** *Geriatr Gerontol Int* | **Published:** 2026 Nov
**PMID:** 42988562 | **DOI:** 10.1111/ggi.70875

**The study:** Japanese researchers followed 5,994 community-dwelling adults aged 35–69 for an average of 12.7 years, measuring arterial stiffness at baseline with the cardio-ankle vascular index (CAVI), a blood-pressure-independent test done with cuffs on the arms and ankles. Among the 295 people who died, those with the stiffest arteries (CAVI ≥ 9.0) had a 38% higher risk of death from any cause compared with those below 8.0. The authors estimated that roughly 23% of cardiovascular deaths in this population might be avoided if everyone kept CAVI below 9.0.

**Why it matters:** Arterial stiffness is a non-invasive, inexpensive measure that adds prognostic information beyond blood pressure alone, and it responds to the same lifestyle and medication strategies that protect the heart.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have hypertension, diabetes, or a family history of heart disease, it is reasonable to ask whether an arterial stiffness measurement (CAVI or a similar pulse wave velocity test) is available to you — it is painless, takes minutes, and gives a picture of vessel health that a blood pressure cuff alone does not.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For nursing and wellness staff, the authors specifically point to CAVI as a tool for primary prevention and nursing management — a concrete, trackable number that can anchor conversations about exercise, smoking, and medication adherence with residents and clients.

**Caveats:** Observational cohort — cannot establish that lowering CAVI prevents deaths; the cardiovascular and non-cardiovascular mortality hazard ratios had confidence intervals crossing 1.0; participants were Japanese adults aged 35–69 at enrollment, so results may not generalize to other populations or to the oldest old.

### 18. Blood sugar swings that standard tests miss were tied to higher heart risk in people without diabetes

**Journal:** *Diabetes Care* | **Published:** 2026 Nov 1
**PMID:** 42752803 | **DOI:** 10.2337/dc26-1197

**The study:** Framingham Heart Study researchers had 1,356 participants without diabetes or cardiovascular disease (mean age 59) wear a blinded continuous glucose monitor for up to 10 days, then compared the glucose patterns with estimated 10-year cardiovascular risk and individual risk factors. Higher glucose variability and more time above 140 mg/dL were associated with higher estimated cardiovascular risk and 21–26% higher odds of hypertension and dyslipidemia — even after adjusting for fasting glucose. Notably, many people flagged as higher-risk by their CGM patterns were not flagged by conventional fasting glucose or HbA1c testing.

**Why it matters:** It suggests that routine fasting glucose and A1c may miss meaningful metabolic trouble in older adults who look normal on paper, and that continuous glucose data could eventually sharpen cardiovascular risk assessment.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If your fasting glucose and A1c are 'normal' but you have hypertension, high cholesterol, or extra weight around the middle, this is a reason to keep asking about metabolic health rather than assuming you are in the clear — though continuous glucose monitoring is not yet a recommended screening tool for people without diabetes.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and care professionals, the practical takeaway is skepticism toward a single reassuring lab value; this research adds to the evidence that post-meal glucose patterns matter, and may foreshadow wider CGM use in preventive care for older adults.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional analysis using estimated rather than observed cardiovascular events; 92.6% of participants were non-Hispanic White, limiting generalizability; several authors report consulting or research funding from Abbott, Novo Nordisk, Dexcom, and other device and pharmaceutical companies, and one author owns a vascular device company.

### 19. A specific breathing pattern during sleep apnea flagged higher heart disease risk — especially in men

**Journal:** *Sleep* | **Published:** 2026 Nov 2
**PMID:** 42969922 | **DOI:** 10.1093/sleep/zsag286

**The study:** Investigators re-analyzed sleep study data from two large US cohorts — the Sleep Heart Health Study and the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis — to test whether an unstable breathing-control pattern during sleep (measured as respiratory 'self-similarity,' a marker of high chemoreflex loop gain) predicted later heart disease. People in the highest quartile of this measure had a higher incidence of new cardiovascular disease in both cohorts, and the measure was also associated with cardiovascular death. Associations were consistently stronger in men, and weakened when age was handled as a continuous variable.

**Why it matters:** It indicates that not all sleep apnea is the same — the pattern and underlying mechanism of disordered breathing may matter for heart risk, beyond the familiar apnea-hypopnea count.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have been diagnosed with sleep apnea, it is worth asking your sleep physician whether your pattern includes central or unstable-breathing features, since these may carry different cardiovascular implications than obstructive events alone — and worth taking treatment adherence seriously either way.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For caregivers and senior living staff, this is a reminder that untreated or poorly characterized sleep apnea in residents, particularly older men, is a cardiovascular issue and not just a snoring nuisance; unused CPAP machines deserve follow-up.

**Caveats:** Secondary analysis of observational cohort data — cannot establish causation; results for categorical high-exposure and cardiovascular mortality were inconsistent across the two cohorts, and associations were substantially attenuated when age was modeled continuously, which weakens confidence in the finding.

### 20. Why men die earlier — and what the data say is actually driving it

**Journal:** *Lancet* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 31
**PMID:** 42956774 | **DOI:** 10.1016/S0140-6736(26)01490-X

**The study:** A multinational team analyzed mortality and disability data from the Global Burden of Diseases studies spanning 1990–2023 to map why males have shorter life expectancies and greater disease burden than females worldwide. Cardiovascular disease and cancer accounted for the largest share of men's excess mortality, alongside substance use, suicide, injuries, and respiratory infections. The authors argue that much of the gap reflects modifiable health behaviors and structural conditions — not biology alone — and is worst among marginalized groups including men who are incarcerated, homeless, low income, or living with mental illness or substance use disorders.

**Why it matters:** The largest single contributor to men's shorter lives is cardiovascular disease, a condition that is substantially preventable with screening and treatment that many men avoid or delay.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you are an older man who has drifted away from regular checkups, this analysis makes the case plainly: the conditions shortening men's lives most are heart disease and cancer, both of which are far more manageable when found early. Booking the blood pressure, cholesterol, and cancer screenings you have been putting off is the single most evidence-aligned response.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For health systems and senior living providers, the findings point to outreach design — men in marginalized circumstances are the least likely to be reached by conventional preventive services, so where and how services are offered may matter as much as what is offered.

**Caveats:** This is an analytical overview of modeled global burden-of-disease estimates rather than a primary study of individuals; GBD estimates carry uncertainty, particularly in regions with sparse vital registration data; several authors report extensive industry consulting and foundation funding (detailed in the paper's declaration of interests).

### 21. Popular Diabetes and Heart Failure Pills Nudge Blood Counts Up — and Quietly Shift Iron Levels

**Journal:** *JAMA* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 24
**PMID:** 42925557 | **DOI:** 10.1001/jama.2026.19921

**The study:** Researchers pooled 85 randomized, double-masked trials covering 70,543 participants (mean age 62.4 years) with heart disease, diabetes, heart failure, or chronic kidney disease who took an SGLT2 inhibitor (drugs such as empagliflozin and dapagliflozin) or a placebo/comparator for a median of 24 weeks. Hemoglobin rose by about 0.60 g/dL and hematocrit by about 2.36 percentage points more in the SGLT2 inhibitor groups than in controls. Erythropoietin, the hormone that drives red blood cell production, also went up, while transferrin saturation — a marker of available iron — went down slightly. The authors say the clinical significance of these modest changes is still unclear.

**Why it matters:** SGLT2 inhibitors are now prescribed widely to older adults with heart failure, diabetes, or kidney disease, and this clarifies that a rise in blood counts on routine labs is an expected drug effect rather than a red flag — while also signaling that iron stores may drift downward.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you take an SGLT2 inhibitor, know that a bump in your hemoglobin or hematocrit on routine bloodwork is a well-documented effect of the medication, not necessarily a new problem; it is reasonable to ask your clinician whether your iron markers are also being checked, and never stop the drug on your own based on a lab number.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For care teams and family members tracking lab reports across multiple specialists, this helps prevent unnecessary alarm, duplicate workups, or referrals when hemoglobin climbs after an SGLT2 inhibitor is started — and flags iron metabolism as something worth watching over longer treatment.

**Caveats:** Median trial duration was only 24 weeks, so long-term effects on iron stores are unknown; the clinical meaning of the hemoglobin change was not established; many authors report extensive consulting, speaking, or research funding from pharmaceutical companies including AstraZeneca, Boehringer Ingelheim, Novo Nordisk, and others.

### 22. A Blood Marker of Inflammation Flagged Future Heart Failure Years Before Symptoms — and Weight Loss Lowered It

**Journal:** *JACC Heart Fail* | **Published:** 2026 Nov
**PMID:** 42687462 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jchf.2026.103296

**The study:** In an ancillary analysis of the Look AHEAD trial, researchers measured two inflammation markers in adults with type 2 diabetes and overweight or obesity — high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP, n=1,902) and interleukin-6 (IL-6, n=1,507) — and followed them for cardiovascular events. After adjusting for standard risk factors and statin use, hs-CRP predicted nothing, but higher IL-6 was tied to a 43% higher risk of new heart failure per standard-deviation increase, rising to 92% higher among people with no prior cardiovascular disease. Participants randomized to an intensive lifestyle intervention aimed at weight loss cut their IL-6 by 28.7% in one year, versus 11.8% in the diabetes support and education group.

**Why it matters:** Heart failure is one of the leading reasons older adults with diabetes are hospitalized, and this suggests a measurable inflammatory pathway that responds to diet and activity changes — even in people who have never had a heart event.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have type 2 diabetes and carry extra weight, this is evidence that a structured weight-loss and activity program does more than move the scale — it measurably cooled the inflammation linked to future heart failure; ask your clinician what lifestyle programs your plan covers, rather than asking for an IL-6 test, which is not yet a routine clinical tool.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living and wellness staff, the finding strengthens the case for funding sustained, structured lifestyle programming for residents with diabetes — the benefit in this trial came from an intensive, supported intervention, not generic advice.

**Caveats:** Observational for the biomarker-outcome link (cannot establish causation); IL-6 testing is not standard clinical practice; participants all had type 2 diabetes with overweight/obesity, so findings may not generalize; the lifestyle comparison covered only 1 year of IL-6 change; funded by the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute, with numerous author disclosures from pharmaceutical and diagnostics companies.

### 23. People Over 80 Are Dying Less of Heart Disease — and Living Longer With Disability

**Journal:** *Arch Gerontol Geriatr* | **Published:** 2026 Nov
**PMID:** 42594758 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.archger.2026.106374

**The study:** Analyzing Global Burden of Disease 2023 data from 1990 to 2023 across 10 selected countries, researchers tracked how health loss among adults aged 80 and older splits between years of life lost to early death and years lived with disability. The population aged 80+ in these countries more than tripled, from 31.8 million to 103.8 million. Death rates fell in all 10 countries (median -31.3%), while disability rates rose in six, pushing the disabling share of total health burden from 19.0% to 25.9%. Cardiovascular disease's share of the burden dropped sharply from 42.3% to 28.6%, while cancers and neurological conditions grew.

**Why it matters:** The decades-long win against fatal heart disease in the oldest-old has shifted the challenge toward preserving function, mobility, and independence — which is exactly what long-term care and geriatric services are built around, and what they are often underfunded to deliver.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Surviving a heart condition into your 80s and 90s is increasingly the norm — so it is worth shifting some of your own attention from survival alone to function: ask your doctor about strength, balance, vision, hearing, and memory assessments, not just blood pressure and cholesterol numbers.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families planning care and for senior living operators, this quantifies the demand shift: more residents living longer with cumulative disability, which argues for investment in rehabilitation, cognitive support, and function-preserving services rather than acute-crisis capacity alone.

**Caveats:** Population-level modeled estimates, not individual patient data; a purposively selected sample of 10 countries that may not generalize; the authors explicitly state the data cannot establish whether morbidity is expanding or compressing at the individual level; GBD estimates carry substantial uncertainty for the oldest age groups.

### 24. Fewer Than 2% of Heart Disease Guidelines Even Mention LGBTQ+ Patients

**Journal:** *J Gen Intern Med* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 13
**PMID:** 42870187 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11606-026-10857-x

**The study:** Researchers systematically searched 313 cardiology clinical guidelines published by American, European, and international societies for any LGBTQIA+-related terms. Only six guidelines — 1.9% — contained such terms, and most mentions were brief: the 2023 atrial fibrillation guideline notes stroke-prevention therapy should be offered equitably regardless of sex and gender diversity, the 2023 chronic coronary disease guideline names sexual orientation as a social determinant of health, and a 2020 obesity guideline calls for more research. The authors note this community makes up roughly 7% of the US population and faces elevated cardiovascular risk from psychosocial stress, smoking, obesity, and gender-affirming hormone therapy.

**Why it matters:** The documents that shape how cardiologists screen, prescribe, and counsel are nearly silent on a sizeable patient group — including a growing cohort of LGBTQ+ older adults whose heart risk factors and hormone regimens may not be addressed by standard guidance.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you are an LGBTQ+ older adult, do not assume your cardiologist has guideline-based direction tailored to you — be explicit about hormone therapy, chronic stress, and smoking history at your visits, and ask directly how those factor into your personal heart risk estimate.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living staff and family caregivers, this is a prompt to make intake conversations and medication reconciliation genuinely inclusive — and to recognize that residents may have had decades of care in which their risk factors went unasked about and undocumented.

**Caveats:** A document review, not a study of patients or outcomes; it cannot show that the guideline gap causes worse health; the risk claims about the LGBTQIA+ population are cited from prior literature rather than generated here; no age-specific analysis, so implications for older adults specifically are inferred.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**
- **Mood keeps landing in the cardiovascular column.** Two studies carry straight over — "A history of depression tracks with higher heart and metabolic disease risk 15 years later" (PMID 42826766) and "Depression raises heart and death risk in people with type 2 diabetes — and blood sugar and cholesterol don't explain it" (PMID 42624218) — and the new "Getting stronger, feeling better: frailty and mood changes track with heart risk in people with high blood pressure" (PMID 42679503) adds a third population and, importantly, shows the association moving in both directions.
- **Social and economic strain as measurable biology.** "Financial strain and discrimination show up in the blood — but mainly in older Black adults who already have heart disease" (PMID 42843435) recurs, and is now joined by two studies about whole groups the system misses: "Why men die earlier — and what the data say is actually driving it" (PMID 42956774) and "Fewer Than 2% of Heart Disease Guidelines Even Mention LGBTQ+ Patients" (PMID 42870187).
- **Heart numbers and brain health.** "Your heart risk score may also say something about your brain" (PMID 42898366) recurs, continuing the thread the previous digest built with "Heart Risk Scores May Also Forecast How Fast Memory Slips" (PMID 42561811).
- **Surviving heart disease into deep old age.** "People Over 80 Are Dying Less of Heart Disease — and Living Longer With Disability" (PMID 42594758) recurs and now has a clinical companion in "Cardiologists Make the Case for Palliative Care in Advanced Heart Disease" (PMID 41879158): more years lived with serious cardiac disease means more years needing symptom control and planning.

**Advances**
- **From legs-deserve-attention to legs-come-first.** The previous digest's peripheral arterial disease series (PMID 42772316) argued leg artery disease is underdiagnosed; the new REACT imaging study, "Hidden plaque in the arteries is far more common than expected — and often nowhere near the heart" (PMID 42670946), shows why that matters mechanically — plaque shows up in the femoral and carotid arteries years before the coronaries, and coronary-only disease was uncommon at every age.
- **From a risky condition cluster to a proposed mechanism.** "Which Combinations of Chronic Conditions Carry the Highest Risk?" (PMID 42821655) flagged frailty, depression and osteoporosis together as a high-mortality grouping. Now "Osteoporosis and clogged arteries may share a biological root" (PMID 42749103) proposes a shared pathway for the bone–artery half of that cluster, and PMID 42679503 supplies the frailty-and-mood half with hard event data.
- **From nocturia as a clue to breathing patterns as a clue.** "Getting up repeatedly at night may flag higher heart risk in men with sleep apnea" (PMID 42600420) recurs; "A specific breathing pattern during sleep apnea flagged higher heart disease risk — especially in men" (PMID 42969922) pushes into the mechanism, and both find the signal mainly in men — though the newer study's results were inconsistent across cohorts.

**Counterpoints**
- **Risk calculators lost some shine.** The previous digest treated PREVENT as a reference standard, testing whether calcium scans improved on it (PMID 42647022) and using it to predict cognitive decline (PMID 42561811). This batch pushes back hard: "Standard Heart Risk Calculators Perform Poorly in Older Women with Kidney Disease" (PMID 42934356) found mediocre discrimination across five models and underprediction by PREVENT in women with and without kidney disease — while "Modeling Study: Newer Diabetes and Obesity Drugs Could Prevent Half a Million Heart Failure Cases" (PMID 42975393) builds a half-million-case projection on those same equations.
- **A single lab value looks weaker than a trend.** Against the previous digest's snapshot measures, three new studies argue for tracking patterns over years: PMID 42994151 (inflammation plus insulin resistance), PMID 42988750 (cumulative blood sugar and fats in people with weak grip), and PMID 42752803, which found that continuous glucose monitoring flagged people whose fasting glucose and HbA1c looked fine.

**New themes**
- **Making treatment actually happen** — "Texting and Portal Messages Nudged Thousands Back onto Their Statins" (PMID 42667613) and PMID 42975393 shift the frame from prediction to delivery, which the previous digest barely touched.
- **Matching the exercise to the number** — "Which exercise helps your heart most? It depends on the number you're trying to move" (PMID 42822168).
- **Palliative care inside cardiology** — PMID 41879158.
- **Reading routine labs correctly** — "Popular Diabetes and Heart Failure Pills Nudge Blood Counts Up — and Quietly Shift Iron Levels" (PMID 42925557).

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Several studies in this batch show that things long treated as the fixed scenery of old age move — and that heart risk moves with them. People who recovered from frailty had 30% lower cardiovascular risk and those whose depression lifted had 18% lower risk in "Getting stronger, feeling better" (PMID 42679503); a structured weight-loss and activity program cut the inflammatory marker tied to future heart failure by 28.7% in "A Blood Marker of Inflammation Flagged Future Heart Failure Years Before Symptoms — and Weight Loss Lowered It" (PMID 42687462); different kinds of exercise, including Tai Chi, topped different cardiometabolic measures in PMID 42822168; and a second text message got thousands of lapsed patients back on their statins in PMID 42667613.
**Why pitch this now:** Most heart coverage aimed at older adults is about prediction — scores, scans, biomarkers — while this cluster is about what still changes after 65, which is the question readers and families actually ask.
**Angle:** A reported feature for older adults and the people who care for them on the comeback evidence: what frailty recovery, mood treatment, matched exercise and a restarted prescription were each worth in heart-risk terms, and what it takes — honestly — to get any of them.
**Potential headlines:**
- Frailty Lifted, Mood Lifted, Heart Risk Dropped
- Tai Chi Outranked the Treadmill for Cholesterol
- The Heart Risk You Can Still Move at 75
**Potential outlets:**
- **Next Avenue** — the piece ends with concrete steps a reader can take this month, which matches how they handle aging-and-health evidence.
- **AARP** — a large audience already being told to "exercise more" with no guidance on which kind, and this feature answers that specifically.
- **McKnight's Senior Living** — strength, Tai Chi and mood programming get framed as cardiovascular interventions with numbers attached, which is a budget argument operators can use.
- **Skilled Nursing News** — frailty reversal and rehabilitation as measurable clinical value is directly relevant to therapy programming and outcome reporting.
