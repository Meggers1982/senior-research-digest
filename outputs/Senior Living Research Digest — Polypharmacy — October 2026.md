### 1. Frail patients with atrial fibrillation are less likely to get stroke-preventing blood thinners — and the gap is widest in Asia

**Journal:** *Geroscience* | **Published:** 2026 Oct;48(5):6871-6885
**PMID:** 41345290 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11357-025-02026-5

**The study:** Researchers compared two large prospective registries of patients with atrial fibrillation — one European (EORP AF Long-Term Registry), one Asian (APHRS AF registry) — measuring multimorbidity, polypharmacy (number of medicines at baseline) and frailty using a 40-item frailty index. European patients carried more multimorbidity, frailty and polypharmacy, but Asian patients with those features were markedly less likely to be prescribed oral anticoagulants, especially if frail (odds ratio 0.34 vs 0.47 in Europe). All three domains predicted higher risk of death and major cardiovascular events, and that excess risk was greater in Asian patients.

**Why it matters:** Frailty and a long medication list appear to make doctors hesitant to prescribe the anticoagulants that prevent AF-related strokes — yet those same patients are at the highest risk of bad outcomes.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have atrial fibrillation and are taking many medicines or have been described as frail, it is worth asking your doctor whether you are on a stroke-prevention anticoagulant and, if not, what specifically is driving that decision — frailty alone is not an automatic reason to withhold it.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For care teams and family advocates: frailty assessments can inadvertently become a reason to under-treat. When reviewing a resident's medication list, check whether anticoagulation was ever started, stopped, or quietly never initiated, and whether that decision was documented and revisited.

**Caveats:** Observational registry data — cannot establish causation; prescribing patterns reflect era and regional practice and may not generalize to current US care; several authors report consultancy and speaker fees from anticoagulant manufacturers (BMS/Pfizer, Boehringer Ingelheim, Daiichi-Sankyo, Bayer, Anthos) and EU Horizon program funding.

### 2. Four years of tracking older adults shows frailty rarely reverses — and certain drug classes track with decline

**Journal:** *Arch Gerontol Geriatr* | **Published:** 2026 Dec;151:106388
**PMID:** 42669283 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.archger.2026.106388

**The study:** Researchers followed 973 community-dwelling Mexican adults aged 60 and older for four years, measuring frailty phenotype annually and modeling transitions between states. They identified four distinct frailty trajectories; movement between states was asymmetric, with improvement the least likely annual outcome. Greater age, female sex, multimorbidity and use of antihypertensive, psycholeptic/psychoanaleptic (sedative and antidepressant-type), antiparkinsonian or antivertiginous drugs were each associated with a higher probability of moving toward more severe frailty, while more education and having a partner were protective.

**Why it matters:** The window to act is at the prefrailty stage, and the drug classes flagged here are extremely common in older adults — making an honest medication review part of frailty prevention rather than an afterthought.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have been told you are "a bit slower" or prefrail, treat that as your window: ask for a full medication review focused on sedatives, antidepressants, blood pressure drugs and dizziness medicines, and ask whether any are still earning their place — while also pushing on exercise and nutrition.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and senior living staff: frailty trajectories in this cohort mostly went one way. Annual, structured reviews of psychoactive and blood-pressure medicines — paired with attention to social connection and partnership, which were protective — belong in routine care planning rather than waiting for a fall.

**Caveats:** Observational secondary analysis — the drug associations may reflect the underlying conditions being treated (confounding by indication) rather than harm from the drugs themselves; single Mexican cohort of social-security beneficiaries, so may not generalize; no intervention tested.

### 3. Why stopping unnecessary medicines near the end of life is so hard — and what actually makes it work

**Journal:** *Palliat Med* | **Published:** 2026 Oct;40(9):1270-1288
**PMID:** 42533730 | **DOI:** 10.1177/02692163261465769

**The study:** A qualitative systematic review synthesized 23 studies on deprescribing for community-dwelling adults with palliative care needs, drawing on the views of patients, caregivers and primary care practitioners. Four themes emerged: people's initial stance toward stopping medicines (shaped by values and beliefs about prescribing), the quality of the deprescribing conversation itself (individualized deliberation, shared decision-making, tailored communication), organizational factors such as interprofessional collaboration and systematic work processes, and broader system-level prerequisites.

**Why it matters:** Potentially inappropriate medicines persist at the end of life largely because the conversation never happens — not because patients refuse; knowing what makes that conversation go well gives families and clinicians a concrete script.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or someone you love is in the last phase of a serious illness, you can start the conversation yourself: ask your GP or pharmacist which of your medicines are meant to prevent problems years from now versus which help you feel better today, and whether the preventive ones are still worth the pills and side effects.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For caregivers and care-home teams: this review suggests deprescribing succeeds when it is built into routine work processes and shared across the team — a scheduled medication review with the pharmacist, GP and nurse present beats hoping someone raises it at a crisis visit. Reassure families that stopping a drug is not "giving up."

**Caveats:** Qualitative evidence synthesis, not a trial — describes influences on practice, not outcomes; three review authors co-authored papers included in the review; findings are drawn largely from European and other high-income primary care systems.

### 4. Hospice nurses are often the ones who spot the pills that no longer help

**Journal:** *J Hosp Palliat Nurs* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 1;28(5):E210-E216
**PMID:** 42519959 | **DOI:** 10.1097/NJH.0000000000001254

**The study:** A rapid review screened 494 records and included 37 studies published from 2020 through 2025 on deprescribing in adults with serious illness, covering medication burden, deprescribing patterns, tools such as STOPPFrail and OncPal, and the nursing role. The evidence indicates that long-term preventive drugs near the end of life — statins, antihypertensives, diabetes medicines, antithrombotics and vitamins — offer limited short-term benefit while adding side effects and treatment burden, yet deprescribing remains inconsistent and usually reactive rather than planned.

**Why it matters:** It identifies the specific medication categories most likely to be safely reducible in late-stage illness and positions nurses — often the clinician families see most — as the practical starting point for that conversation.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you are on hospice or palliative care, ask directly about statins, blood pressure pills, diabetes drugs and vitamins: these are the classes most often found to add burden without adding comfort at this stage. Nothing should be stopped without your care team's involvement, but you are entitled to ask why each one is still there.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For hospice and senior living nurses: structured tools like STOPPFrail and OncPal exist and are underused. Building medication reconciliation and post-withdrawal monitoring into admission and regular review — rather than reacting to a swallowing problem or an adverse event — is where this review says the gains are.

**Caveats:** Rapid review rather than a full systematic review or meta-analysis; included studies are heterogeneous (qualitative, observational, framework evaluations) with no pooled effect estimates; no new patient-level outcome data.

### 5. Pelvic fractures after 50 look a lot like hip fractures — and may deserve the same seriousness

**Journal:** *Osteoporos Int* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 5 (online ahead of print)
**PMID:** 42832039 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s00198-026-08221-0

**The study:** Using national Dutch claims and population data, researchers analyzed all 7,120 people aged 50 and older diagnosed with a pelvic (n=6,091) or acetabular (n=1,029) fracture in 2019. Pelvic fracture patients were older (mean 77 vs 74), overwhelmingly female (79% vs 36%), and had higher pre-injury rates of osteoporosis, connective tissue disease and polypharmacy, while acetabular fractures led to more hospital admission, surgery and complication-related readmission. Despite those differences, one-year mortality was similar and high in both groups (15% vs 17%), as were rates of new major fractures.

**Why it matters:** A pelvic fracture is often treated as a minor, non-surgical injury, yet one in six of these patients died within a year — a profile comparable to hip fracture and a signal that bone health and medication review should follow.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you break your pelvis — even if you are told it will heal without surgery and you are sent home — ask for an osteoporosis evaluation, a fall-risk check and a review of medicines that can cause dizziness or unsteadiness. This is not a trivial injury, and it predicts further fractures.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and senior living professionals: a resident with a "conservatively managed" pelvic fracture warrants the same post-fracture pathway you would trigger after a hip fracture — bone density assessment, polypharmacy review, and intensified fall prevention — not just pain control and time.

**Caveats:** Retrospective administrative claims analysis — observational, cannot establish causation, and relies on coding accuracy; single national health system (Netherlands) with care patterns that may not match other countries; no data on functional recovery or pre-fracture frailty measures beyond diagnoses.

### 6. Botox for depression: pooled trial data hint at a drug-free alternative for people already on many medicines

**Journal:** *J Affect Disord* | **Published:** 2026 Dec 15;415:122443
**PMID:** 42669382 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jad.2026.122443

**The study:** A systematic review and meta-analysis pooled 8 randomized controlled trials with 636 participants comparing facial botulinum toxin A injections against placebo or the antidepressant sertraline. BoNT/A was associated with a significant reduction in depressive symptoms versus controls (SMD −0.81), was superior to placebo (SMD −1.04), and performed comparably to sertraline (SMD −0.23). Standard glabellar (frown-line) injection protocols worked; high-dose pan-facial protocols did not separate from controls. Overall adverse event rates matched controls, with only transient local reactions and none of the systemic side effects seen with oral antidepressants.

**Why it matters:** For people whose medication lists are already long, a treatment that does not add another daily systemic drug — and does not interact with existing ones — is a meaningful option to know about and discuss.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If depression is part of your picture and you are already juggling many pills or have had bad reactions to antidepressants, this is a reasonable question to raise with your doctor — while recognizing that the evidence base is still small and not specific to older adults, and that nothing here means stopping a medication that is working.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For clinicians and care teams managing polypharmacy: this adds a non-systemic candidate to the conversation about treatment burden in depression, but access, cost, insurance coverage and the limited elder-specific data make it a discussion point rather than a care-plan change.

**Caveats:** Meta-analysis of only 8 trials totaling 636 participants; trial populations were not older adults specifically, so generalizability to seniors is unproven; blinding is inherently difficult with an injection that visibly changes facial expression; short follow-up in most trials; outcomes based on depression rating scales.

### 7. More Than Half of Older Adults on Acid-Reflux Pills Stay on Them Long Term, Finnish Data Show

**Journal:** *Drugs Aging* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 30
**PMID:** 42814388 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40266-026-01335-4

**The study:** Researchers at Finland's Social Insurance Institution analyzed nationwide prescription records from 2017 to 2024, comparing proton pump inhibitor (PPI) use in adults aged 65 and older with use in adults aged 18-64. The number of PPI users rose 12% and dispensations rose 37% over the period, with the steepest climb among older adults. By 2024, nearly half of all PPI users were 65 or older, and 56.3% of them were long-term users (at least three dispensations a year) versus 34.1% of working-age adults. Among older PPI users, 47% were also taking more than ten other prescription medicines and 39% were on anticoagulants.

**Why it matters:** PPIs are often started for a short, specific reason and then quietly continued for years, adding to an already heavy medication load and to potential long-term risks in people who may no longer need them.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have been taking omeprazole, pantoprazole or a similar acid-reducing pill for months or years, it is worth asking your prescriber at your next visit why you are still on it, whether the original reason still applies, and whether a planned taper or on-demand use would be reasonable for you. Do not stop on your own, since some people need these drugs long term and abrupt stopping can cause rebound symptoms.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For family caregivers and senior living staff, PPIs are a prime candidate for inclusion in routine medication-review checklists: flag any resident who has been on one continuously for more than a year and ask the prescriber to document the ongoing indication, particularly when the resident is also on ten-plus other medicines or an anticoagulant.

**Caveats:** Observational register study of dispensing data, so it cannot establish whether long-term use caused harm; data reflect dispensed prescriptions rather than confirmed use or clinical indication; Finnish population and prescribing patterns may not generalize elsewhere; study conducted by the national insurance institution that administers the reimbursement data.

### 8. Counting Only Pills Undercounts Your Medications: Creams, Patches and Eye Drops Add Up

**Journal:** *Int J Clin Pharm* | **Published:** 2026 Oct
**PMID:** 42536289 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11096-026-02194-0

**The study:** Australian researchers used linked medication dispensing records for adults over 65 in Western Australia from 2012/13 to 2018/19, comparing the conventional way of measuring polypharmacy (oral tablets and capsules only) with a broader count that also included creams, ointments, patches, and eye and ear drops. Adding non-oral products raised the measured medicine burden in both community-dwelling older adults and residential aged care residents, with a larger relative increase in aged care. The share of people in the five-or-more-medicines category rose by about 3 to 4 percentage points, and dry-eye treatments emerged as the seventh most common medicine class in aged care, while opioids split into separate oral and patch rankings.

**Why it matters:** The true complexity of an older person's medication regimen, including the daily work of applying patches, creams and drops, is routinely invisible in the records clinicians and researchers rely on to spot risky polypharmacy.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** When you bring a medication list to an appointment, include everything that is not a pill: pain patches, hormone or nitrate patches, steroid creams, eye drops, ear drops and inhalers. These count toward your total drug exposure and toward how much daily medication management you are juggling, and leaving them off can make your regimen look simpler and safer than it is.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living and aged care operators, this is an argument for brown-bag reviews and medication administration records that capture topical and transdermal products explicitly, and for recognizing the staff time and error risk attached to drops, creams and patches that never appear in standard polypharmacy counts.

**Caveats:** Observational analysis of administrative dispensing data, which cannot confirm that medicines were actually used; single Australian state; data end in 2018/19; does not measure clinical outcomes such as adverse events or hospitalization.

### 9. Tools to Safely Cut Back Medicines Near the End of Life Show Promise, but the Evidence Is Still Thin

**Journal:** *Palliat Med* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 26
**PMID:** 42799555 | **DOI:** 10.1177/02692163261483456

**The study:** Australian researchers screened 4,108 papers and reviewed 34 studies covering 18 different deprescribing tools designed for adults approaching the end of life. Eight studies showed the tools reduced the number of medicines taken, and there was some supportive evidence for lower mortality, fewer acute care referrals and lower medication costs. However, most comparisons against usual care were not statistically significant, definitions of "end of life" varied widely between studies, and risk of bias was rated moderate to high throughout.

**Why it matters:** For people in their last months of life, continuing preventive drugs such as statins or bone medicines offers little benefit while adding side effects, cost and pill burden, and this review maps which structured tools clinicians can use to have that conversation.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or someone you love is living with advanced illness, it is reasonable to ask the treating team to run a structured medication review and to ask of each drug: what is this doing for me now, and how long would it take to help? Stopping preventive medicines that no longer serve your goals is a legitimate part of good care, not giving up.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Hospice, palliative care and senior living clinicians should note that validated, standardized deprescribing tools exist but that no single instrument has strong outcome evidence behind it; pick one that fits your setting, document the rationale, and expect to defend decisions on clinical judgment rather than trial data.

**Caveats:** Systematic review without meta-analysis; included studies had moderate to high risk of bias; inconsistent definitions of end of life limit comparability; most clinical outcome comparisons were non-significant.

### 10. In Hospitalized Seniors, Combined Memory and Mobility Problems Multiply the Risk of Falls, Pressure Injuries and Other Complications

**Journal:** *Geriatr Nurs* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 23
**PMID:** 42777277 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.gerinurse.2026.104364

**The study:** Researchers at a tertiary hospital in Wuhan, China, assessed 459 inpatients aged 60 and older for intrinsic capacity (cognition, mobility, vitality, sensory function and psychological health) and for five nursing risks: falls, pressure injuries, aspiration, deep vein thrombosis and unplanned tube removal. Nearly three-quarters (73.2%) had at least one of these risks. Overall intrinsic capacity impairment was strongly associated with both having a risk at all and having multiple stacked risks, and patients with both cognitive and mobility impairment had by far the highest odds of escalating risk. The authors also pointed to pain and polypharmacy as modifiable contributors.

**Why it matters:** It identifies a simple, high-yield combination — trouble thinking clearly plus trouble walking — that should trigger intensified safety precautions the moment an older person is admitted to the hospital.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you are admitted to the hospital and you already have memory changes along with unsteady walking, ask the nursing team what specific precautions are in place for falls, skin breakdown, swallowing and blood clots, and ask whether your pain control and your medication list have been reviewed for drugs that make confusion or unsteadiness worse.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For nursing leadership and senior living staff accompanying residents through hospitalizations, the practical takeaway is to screen for intrinsic capacity domains on admission rather than relying on single-risk checklists, and to treat co-occurring cognitive plus mobility impairment as a flag for bundled, not piecemeal, prevention.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design cannot establish causation or direction of effect; single center with convenience sampling; Chinese tertiary hospital inpatients may not generalize to other systems; very wide confidence intervals on the main odds ratios suggest unstable estimates.

### 11. Facing Surgery in Your 80s or 90s? What the Evidence Says Should Happen Before, During and After

**Journal:** *Eur Geriatr Med* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 1
**PMID:** 42821180 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s41999-026-01617-5

**The study:** Polish intensive care and geriatrics specialists reviewed the available evidence on perioperative care for very old surgical patients. They concluded that preoperative assessment should go beyond heart and lung risk to screen for frailty, sarcopenia, malnutrition and cognitive decline, with positive findings triggering interdisciplinary intervention. They emphasized caution with medications because of frequent polypharmacy and altered drug handling in older bodies, structured prevention and management of postoperative delirium, and coordinated rehabilitation and post-discharge care to counter functional decline.

**Why it matters:** Surgery is a common turning point where an independent older adult can lose function permanently, and the review lays out which assessments and precautions families can reasonably expect to be in place.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Before a planned operation, ask your surgical team whether you will have a frailty, nutrition and memory assessment, whether your full medication list (including sedatives and anticholinergics) will be reviewed for the perioperative period, what the delirium prevention plan is, and what rehabilitation is arranged for after discharge.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living and care-coordination staff can use these domains as a pre-admission checklist and should plan early for the post-discharge window, when functional decline and medication changes most often go unmanaged after an older resident returns from surgery.

**Caveats:** Non-systematic narrative review rather than new data; no formal quality appraisal or quantitative synthesis; some recommendations extrapolated from broader older populations rather than the very old specifically.

### 12. Sedating and Anticholinergic Drug Loads Rarely Change in the Hospital — and That Shapes Where Patients Go Next

**Journal:** *J Am Geriatr Soc* | **Published:** 2026 Sep
**PMID:** 42530030 | **DOI:** 10.1111/jgs.70549

**The study:** Researchers at three academic health system hospitals used electronic health records to automatically calculate the Drug Burden Index — a measure of cumulative exposure to sedating and anticholinergic medications — for 3,450 patients aged 65 and older who were prescribed central nervous system–active drugs around a hospital stay (2018–2020). In 80% of patients, the drug burden did not change at all during hospitalization. Any change in burden, up or down, was linked to much lower odds of being discharged home, and higher burden at admission or discharge was linked to more outpatient visits in the next 90 days; no link was found with 30-day readmission.

**Why it matters:** A hospital stay is one of the few moments when someone reviews a whole medication list at once, yet for four out of five older patients on brain-active drugs, the sedative and anticholinergic load left the hospital exactly as it arrived.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you take sleep aids, strong painkillers, antidepressants, bladder medications, or antihistamines, a hospital admission is a reasonable time to ask the team: has anyone reviewed my full medication list for sedating or anticholinergic effects, and did anything change? Ask for the reasoning behind any new drug added during the stay and when it should be reassessed.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For discharge planners and senior living staff, this suggests flagging patients whose brain-active drug burden shifted during hospitalization — in either direction — as a group needing more intensive post-discharge follow-up, since they are far less likely to go straight home and tend to need more clinic visits.

**Caveats:** Retrospective observational study of health records — cannot establish causation; limited to three academic hospitals and to patients already on CNS-active medications, so findings may not generalize; drug burden was derived from prescribing records rather than confirmed intake.

### 13. National Snapshot: How Much Older Americans Mix Prescriptions, Over-the-Counter Drugs and Supplements

**Journal:** *JAMA* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 24
**PMID:** 42783349 | **DOI:** 10.1001/jama.2026.17268

**The study:** Using a nationally representative US sample, researchers compared medication use among older adults between 2015–2016 and 2021–2023, counting not only prescription drugs but also over-the-counter products and dietary supplements. The analysis tracked concurrent use of multiple products and the frequency of combinations flagged as potentially major drug-drug interactions.

**Why it matters:** Pill counts that ignore drugstore products and supplements understate real exposure, and some of the riskiest combinations in older adults involve something that was never prescribed by a doctor.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Bring everything — prescriptions, aspirin, heartburn pills, fish oil, herbal and vitamin supplements — to your next appointment or to a pharmacist for a 'brown bag' review, and specifically ask whether any two items on your list interact. Do not stop a prescribed medication on your own.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Families and senior living staff often track prescriptions carefully while bedside-drawer supplements and OTC purchases go unrecorded; building those into the medication list is a concrete, low-cost safety step.

**Caveats:** Only a plain-language summary was available in the abstract record, so effect sizes and specific interaction rates are not reported here; cross-sectional survey design cannot establish causation; medication use in national surveys relies in part on respondent report. Authors report consulting and grant funding including NIH, Arnold Ventures, and the National Community Pharmacists Association.

### 14. Pharmacist Medication Reviews for Patients on Five-Plus Drugs Paid for Themselves — About €181 Per Patient

**Journal:** *Int J Clin Pharm* | **Published:** 2026 Oct
**PMID:** 42467355 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11096-026-02190-4

**The study:** Over 19 months at a Dutch academic hospital, junior pharmacists supervised by clinical pharmacists performed 271 medication reviews for patients taking five or more medications who had at least one additional risk factor, producing 1,689 recommendations. A cost model found the reviews cost about €104 per patient but generated roughly €46 in medication savings and €239 in avoided medication-related readmissions, for a net €181 per patient — around €688,000 if scaled across the hospital. No patient characteristic — age, kidney function, medication count, or ward — predicted who would yield the most savings.

**Why it matters:** It supports offering structured medication reviews broadly to anyone on multiple drugs rather than rationing them to the patients who look highest-risk on paper, since the analysis could not identify a subgroup where the reviews paid off more.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you take five or more medications, ask whether a pharmacist — not only your physician — can conduct a formal medication review during a hospital stay or at your pharmacy, and ask for the written list of recommendations so you can discuss them with your regular doctor.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For administrators and care organizations, this is an economic argument for staffing pharmacist review programs, including supervised junior pharmacists, rather than treating them as a discretionary extra.

**Caveats:** Modeled cost analysis, not a trial measuring actual readmissions avoided; relatively small sample (271 reviews); single-center in the Netherlands, so costs and savings may not translate to other health systems; savings depended on an assumed rate of preventable medication-related admissions.

### 15. Fear of Falling Runs Deepest in Older Adults With More Falls, More Hospital Visits and Longer Medication Lists

**Journal:** *J Am Med Dir Assoc* | **Published:** 2026 Oct
**PMID:** 42628444 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jamda.2026.106418

**The study:** Researchers assessed 391 adults aged 65 and older (average age 78.6; 60% women) attending a geriatric clinic in northeastern Mexico who had fallen within the past two years, measuring concern about falling with a validated Spanish-language scale. Severe concern was independently associated with more frequent falls, hospitalizations, emergency visits, a higher medication count, and female sex; moderate concern was linked to falls, hospitalizations, diabetes, gait disorders, and frailty.

**Why it matters:** Fear of falling is not just an emotional state — it clusters with frailty, polypharmacy, and heavy health care use, and it drives people to restrict activity in ways that make future falls more likely.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If worry about falling has started shaping what you do — skipping errands, avoiding stairs, going out less — say so at your next visit, and ask for a fall-risk assessment that includes a review of your medication list alongside gait and balance testing.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and senior living staff, a resident's expressed fear of falling is a useful screening signal worth documenting; it may flag someone who also warrants a medication review, gait assessment, and a structured activity plan before the next fall happens.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional observational design — cannot determine whether medication burden or health care use causes fear of falling or the reverse; single semiprivate tertiary clinic in one region of Mexico, so results may not generalize; some data drawn from caregiver reports.

### 16. Pharmacist-led medication reviews cut risky prescriptions in half for older cancer patients

**Journal:** *J Geriatr Oncol* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 12
**PMID:** 42731364 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jgo.2026.104120

**The study:** Researchers pooled eight studies involving 1,018 adults aged 65 and older who had cancer and were taking medications flagged as potentially inappropriate. Every program involved a clinical pharmacist, usually working within a multidisciplinary team, reviewing medication lists and recommending stopping or tapering drugs. Across four studies reporting rates, 62.5% to 73% of flagged medications were successfully deprescribed, and a meta-analysis of three studies found the share of patients taking at least one inappropriate medication dropped by roughly half (pooled relative risk 0.49).

**Why it matters:** Older adults in cancer treatment often accumulate medications from multiple prescribers, raising the risk of interactions with chemotherapy and other side effects — and this review shows a structured pharmacist review can safely trim that list.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or a loved one is being treated for cancer, you can ask whether a clinical pharmacist has reviewed your full medication list — including over-the-counter products and supplements — alongside your oncology plan, and whether any long-standing drugs are still earning their place.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For oncology and senior living teams: the evidence supports embedding a pharmacist in the geriatric oncology workflow, but the review also exposes how rarely anyone measures whether deprescribing actually improves falls, hospitalizations or quality of life — a gap worth tracking locally.

**Caveats:** Systematic review of mostly small, uncontrolled before-and-after service evaluations (range 26–392 participants) with serious risk of bias; only three studies contributed to the meta-analysis (n=189); patient-centered outcomes such as symptoms, function and survival were reported only narratively.

### 17. One in five prescriptions for older Chinese outpatients contained a potentially risky medication — and polypharmacy was the biggest red flag

**Journal:** *J Am Med Dir Assoc* | **Published:** 2026 Oct
**PMID:** 42594508 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jamda.2026.106423

**The study:** Researchers analyzed 130,243 geriatric outpatient prescriptions from 59 hospitals across six Chinese cities for patients aged 65 and older (median age 76). Depending on which of eight screening tools was applied, between 13.3% and 36.5% of prescriptions included a potentially inappropriate medication, with psychiatric drugs and NSAIDs the leading culprits. Taking five or more medications was the strongest predictor of exposure (adjusted odds ratio 3.40–5.58), followed by psychiatric comorbidity and advanced age.

**Why it matters:** It quantifies, at a national scale, how quickly the risk of an inappropriate prescription climbs once a medication list passes five drugs — and pinpoints sedatives, antidepressants and anti-inflammatories as the usual suspects.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Count your medications. If you're at five or more — especially if any are sleeping pills, anxiety or depression medications, or regular anti-inflammatory painkillers — that's a reasonable prompt to request a full medication review at your next appointment rather than a reason to stop anything on your own.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For care teams and family caregivers: the study found that different screening criteria flag very different drugs, so a medication reviewed as 'fine' under one tool may be flagged under another — worth asking which criteria your clinician or pharmacy uses.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional prescription-data analysis — it identifies potentially inappropriate prescribing, not actual harm, and cannot establish causation; conducted in Chinese tertiary and secondary hospital geriatric clinics, so prescribing patterns may not generalize to other health systems; no clinical outcomes measured.

### 18. Older hospital patients with depression get more sedatives and antipsychotics than younger ones, 16-year survey finds

**Journal:** *J Affect Disord* | **Published:** 2026 Dec 1
**PMID:** 42503320 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jad.2026.122322

**The study:** Using a long-running drug surveillance program covering psychiatric hospitals in Germany and Austria from 2007 to 2023, researchers compared prescribing for 25,881 inpatients with major depressive disorder, of whom 5,297 were over 64. Older patients were far more likely to have psychotic depression (16.9% vs. 9.1%) and to be on multiple psychotropic drugs at once. They received antipsychotics more often (57.4% vs. 45.9%) along with more tranquilizers and sleeping medications, while SSRIs and tricyclics were more common in younger patients.

**Why it matters:** Antipsychotics, tranquilizers and hypnotics carry heightened risks of falls, confusion and sedation in later life, so knowing that these are disproportionately used in hospitalized older adults with depression gives patients and families a concrete reason to ask about the plan for tapering them.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're hospitalized for depression, ask directly what each psychiatric medication is for, how long it's meant to continue, and specifically whether any sedative or antipsychotic is intended as a short-term measure — and make sure that plan follows you home to your regular doctor.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and senior living staff receiving a resident back from a psychiatric admission: the discharge medication list may include drugs started for acute agitation or sleep that were never meant to be permanent. Flagging these for review at the first follow-up visit is a practical safeguard.

**Caveats:** Observational cross-sectional surveys of prescribing patterns — no outcome data on effectiveness or harm, and no causal conclusions possible; limited to inpatient psychiatric settings in Germany and Austria; the AMSP program has long received unrestricted educational and research grants from numerous pharmaceutical companies, and several authors report industry honoraria and advisory roles.

### 19. Polypharmacy raises the odds of being admitted when older adults land in the emergency department

**Journal:** *BMC Geriatr* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 5
**PMID:** 42701177 | **DOI:** 10.1186/s12877-026-08158-3

**The study:** This analysis of the EGERS study tracked 2,794 patients aged 65 and older (mean age 77.4) with a single non-traumatic complaint across 36 emergency departments in nine European countries during a seven-week window in late 2020. Shortness of breath, abdominal pain, chest pain, weakness/fatigue, and extremity pain accounted for 59% of visits. Being 85 or older, having geriatric risk factors (dementia, home help, prior falls), taking multiple medications, and presenting with shortness of breath all increased the odds of hospital admission; shortness of breath also doubled the odds of in-hospital death.

**Why it matters:** It confirms that the complaint an older person walks in with tells emergency staff little about how sick they are — and that a long medication list is itself a marker of needing closer scrutiny.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Keep an up-to-date medication list (including doses and over-the-counter items) somewhere you can hand over at the emergency department — along with notes on recent falls and any memory problems — because those details, more than your presenting symptom, shape how carefully you'll be assessed.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living operators and family caregivers: a vague complaint like 'weakness and fatigue' in a resident with polypharmacy deserves the same urgency as a dramatic one. The authors argue for geriatric-specific emergency care pathways and staff training rather than triage by symptom alone.

**Caveats:** Observational cross-sectional secondary analysis — cannot establish causation; data were collected during the Covid-19 pandemic, which likely altered who came to emergency departments and who was admitted; patients with more than one documented chief complaint were excluded; European emergency systems may not generalize elsewhere.

### 20. Hospitals are testing ways to catch bad prescriptions at the front door — but almost no one is measuring whether patients do better

**Journal:** *Int J Clin Pharm* | **Published:** 2026 Oct
**PMID:** 42313336 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11096-026-02170-8

**The study:** Researchers searched the literature from 1991 to 2025 for programs aimed at reducing potentially inappropriate prescribing in adults aged 65 and over arriving at emergency departments or acute geriatric units. They found 21 studies, only four of which were randomized controlled trials, describing 18 interventions: pharmacist-led medication reviews, computerized clinical decision support, prescriber education and academic detailing, or combinations. Nearly all measured whether prescribing changed; very few looked at whether patients were readmitted, fell less, or felt better.

**Why it matters:** Acute hospital visits are a prime moment to fix a bloated medication list, and this review maps which approaches hospitals are actually using — while flagging that the field still can't say which ones improve the outcomes patients care about.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** A hospital or emergency visit is a natural checkpoint for your medications — it's reasonable to ask whether a pharmacist will review your list before you're discharged and to request that any changes be explained in writing for your regular doctor.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living and post-acute professionals: the review identifies pharmacist-led review, decision support software and prescriber education as the main models, and highlights unanswered questions about prescriber follow-through and communication back to community providers — an obvious handoff point where residents' medication changes get lost.

**Caveats:** Scoping review, not a meta-analysis — it maps the evidence rather than quantifying effectiveness; the underlying literature is heterogeneous, mostly non-randomized, short-term, and focused on prescribing metrics rather than clinical outcomes or patient experience.

### 21. Assisted Living Has Become a High-Medication Setting With Thin Medical Oversight, Review Finds

**Journal:** *Geriatrics (Basel)* | **Published:** 2026 Aug 18
**PMID:** 42645013 | **DOI:** 10.3390/geriatrics11040109

**The study:** Researchers screened the published literature from 2010 to 2025 for studies dealing specifically with medication safety and care coordination in assisted living (as distinct from nursing homes or general long-term care). Only 22 studies met their strict definition, drawn from just four countries — the United States (13), Finland (6), Canada (2) and the UK (1) — and every randomized trial came from a single Finnish research program. The authors conclude that assisted living now houses an older, frailer, more heavily medicated population than ever, yet is typically regulated as a social rather than a clinical setting, with no requirement for on-site nursing and no clear point of clinical accountability for medications.

**Why it matters:** Residents and families often assume that moving into assisted living means someone is actively reviewing the medication list — this review suggests that in many places no one formally owns that job, and rigorous trials of deprescribing in this setting barely exist.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you live in or are considering assisted living, ask directly who reviews your full medication list, how often, and whether that person talks to your prescribing doctors and pharmacy — don't assume the community handles it as part of the package.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For operators and administrators: this is a structural accountability gap, not a clinical one. Consider documenting who is responsible for medication reconciliation, building a standing relationship with a consultant pharmacist, and making that arrangement explicit in marketing and admission materials — families are increasingly asking.

**Caveats:** Scoping review of a sparse literature (22 sources) from only four high-income countries; the evidence base is dominated by one Finnish research program, so findings may not generalize to all assisted living models or regulatory environments.

### 22. Adding Geriatricians to Hip Fracture Care Changed Prescribing — And Deaths in Hospital Fell by More Than Half

**Journal:** *Osteoporos Int* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 28
**PMID:** 42806170 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s00198-026-08244-7

**The study:** A Portuguese hospital compared 872 consecutively admitted patients aged 65 and older with hip and other proximal femoral fractures before and after launching an orthogeriatric co-management unit, where geriatricians work alongside orthopedic surgeons. After implementation, prescribing of bone-protective and supportive treatments rose sharply (vitamin D 2.5% to 56.7%, calcium 2.7% to 51.5%, zoledronic acid 1.4% to 12.7%, iron infusion 1.4% to 20.6%, laxatives 8.1% to 74.5%), while injected haloperidol — a sedating antipsychotic often used for delirium — dropped from 21.7% to 10.9%. Unadjusted in-hospital mortality fell from 7.7% to 2.9%.

**Why it matters:** It shows that who manages an older patient after a fracture changes what drugs they receive — more prevention of the next fracture, fewer sedatives — and is associated with markedly better survival during the hospital stay.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or your spouse break a hip, ask whether the hospital has an orthogeriatric or co-management service, and before discharge ask specifically whether osteoporosis treatment, vitamin D, calcium, anemia correction and a bowel plan have been started — these are the items that were routinely missed before geriatricians were involved.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and care staff: the sharp fall in injected haloperidol is worth noting. Delirium after hip fracture is common, and this model suggests much of it can be managed without reflexive sedation — a reasonable thing to raise with the hospital team when a parent becomes confused post-surgery.

**Caveats:** Retrospective, observational before-and-after study at a single center over 23 months; mortality figures were unadjusted, so other changes in care over the period could contribute. Causation cannot be established.

### 23. Nearly All Advanced Lung Cancer Patients Were Flagged for a Questionable Medication — And Most Said They'd Happily Stop One

**Journal:** *Int J Clin Pharm* | **Published:** 2026 Oct
**PMID:** 42126766 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11096-026-02158-4

**The study:** Dutch researchers screened the medication records of 817 patients with stage IV non-small cell lung cancer using the OncoSTRIP checklist and surveyed 37 patients, 15 caregivers, 24 clinicians and 24 pharmacists about deprescribing. Potentially inappropriate medications were flagged in 97.1% of patients; 40.8% of flagged drugs were confirmed problematic on chart review, most often because of adverse-event risk (47.3%) or because the therapy was simply unnecessary (30.0%). Nearly 9 in 10 patients (89.2%) and 73.3% of caregivers said they would be willing to stop a medication if their doctor recommended it.

**Why it matters:** When life expectancy is limited, drugs aimed at preventing problems years down the road can cause harm without delivering benefit — and this study suggests patients are far more open to stopping them than clinicians assume.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're living with advanced cancer or another serious illness, it is reasonable to ask your oncologist or pharmacist: which of my pills are still working for me today, and which were started for a benefit that is years away? Most patients in this study welcomed that conversation.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For caregivers and care teams: the bottleneck was not patient resistance but professional hesitancy — doctors cited poor collaboration and uncertainty, pharmacists cited competence and patient-related concerns. Prompting a formal medication review, and making clear the family is open to it, can break that stalemate.

**Caveats:** Observational chart review plus small survey samples (37 patients, 15 caregivers); 59.2% of flagged medications could not be confirmed as inappropriate because of missing record data, likely inflating the headline prevalence. Single hospital system in the Netherlands; attitudes were self-reported and hypothetical, not actual deprescribing outcomes.

### 24. Expert Panel Sets Out How Opioids Should — and Shouldn't — Be Used for Chronic Pain in Older Adults

**Journal:** *Drugs Aging* | **Published:** 2026 Sep
**PMID:** 42627454 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40266-026-01329-2

**The study:** A multidisciplinary Portuguese panel spanning palliative care, anesthesiology, rheumatology, orthopedics, rehabilitation and family medicine reviewed the evidence and reached consensus recommendations on opioid use for chronic pain in older adults. They emphasize that aging reduces kidney and liver clearance, lowers lean body mass and alters protein binding, so doses and dosing intervals must be adjusted — "start low, go slow" — with preference for opioids that have inactive metabolites or predictable clearance. They draw a sharp distinction between cancer pain, where potent opioids (including transdermal and rapid-acting formulations) are often appropriate, and chronic non-cancer pain, where the emphasis should be on treating the underlying pain mechanism, formal risk-benefit assessment, and time-limited opioid use.

**Why it matters:** Opioids interact with the many other drugs older adults take and raise the risk of constipation, confusion and falls, yet undertreated pain is also harmful — this consensus gives a framework for getting the balance right.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're prescribed an opioid, ask what the plan is: what dose you're starting at, when it will be reassessed, how long it's intended to last, and what's being done up front about constipation and fall risk. For non-cancer pain, a time limit and an exit plan should be part of the conversation from day one.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and senior living staff: new or increased opioids warrant extra fall-prevention attention and bowel monitoring in the following days. A sudden change in alertness, balance or continence after a dose change is a reason to call the prescriber rather than to wait it out.

**Caveats:** Consensus review based on expert opinion and narrative literature synthesis, not new patient data or a systematic review; recommendations reflect one national panel and may not match local prescribing guidance or drug availability.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**
- **Frailty as an unspoken reason to withhold beneficial treatment.** The previous digest's German heart failure analysis (PMID 42446811) found older patients less likely to receive aldosterone antagonists and beta-blockers. The new digest extends the same pattern to stroke prevention: *Frail patients with atrial fibrillation are less likely to get stroke-preventing blood thinners* (PMID 41345290) shows frailty and a long medication list predicting lower anticoagulant prescribing in the very patients at highest risk of death.
- **Psychoactive and sedating drugs keep surfacing as the problem classes.** The previous run flagged benzodiazepines, anticholinergics and antiepileptics in Danish fallers (PMID 42584792) and opioid-benzodiazepine pairs in NHANES (PMID 42285234). The new digest adds *Four years of tracking older adults shows frailty rarely reverses* (PMID 42669283), where sedative and antidepressant-type drugs tracked with worsening frailty; *One in five prescriptions for older Chinese outpatients contained a potentially risky medication* (PMID 42594508), where psychiatric drugs and NSAIDs led the list; and *Older hospital patients with depression get more sedatives and antipsychotics than younger ones* (PMID 42503320).
- **Pharmacist-led review continues to deliver.** The Korean readmission program (PMID 42570168) and Dutch pharmacy trial (PMID 42472621) from the previous digest are now joined by *Pharmacist-led medication reviews cut risky prescriptions in half for older cancer patients* (PMID 42731364) and *Pharmacist Medication Reviews for Patients on Five-Plus Drugs Paid for Themselves* (PMID 42467355).
- **Deprescribing is doable; proof that patients feel better still isn't there.** The previous umbrella review on focused deprescribing (PMID 42607278) made this point. Three new reviews repeat it almost word for word: *Tools to Safely Cut Back Medicines Near the End of Life* (PMID 42799555), *Hospitals are testing ways to catch bad prescriptions at the front door* (PMID 42313336) and PMID 42731364.
- **Duplicate carryover:** the Portuguese opioid consensus (PMID 42627454) appears in both digests with the same "start low, go slow" framing.

**Advances**
- From "does review work" to "what does it pay and who should get it": PMID 42467355 puts a number on it — about €181 net per patient — and, notably, found no subgroup where reviews paid off more, which argues against rationing them to the patients who look riskiest on paper.
- From counting pills to counting everything: *Counting Only Pills Undercounts Your Medications* (PMID 42536289) and the *National Snapshot* of prescriptions, over-the-counter drugs and supplements (PMID 42783349) push past the oral-tablet counts that underpinned nearly every study in the previous digest.
- From "stop the drug" to "have the conversation": *Why stopping unnecessary medicines near the end of life is so hard* (PMID 42533730) and *Nearly All Advanced Lung Cancer Patients Were Flagged for a Questionable Medication* (PMID 42126766) move the field toward the human bottleneck rather than the pharmacology.

**Counterpoints**
- The previous digest's prediction modeling (PMID 42296796) concluded polypharmacy counts added little beyond hip strength and diuretic use for forecasting injurious falls. Several new studies push back on writing off the raw count: medication number was independently tied to severe fear of falling (PMID 42628444), to hospital admission from the emergency department (PMID 42701177), and to a three- to fivefold rise in potentially inappropriate prescribing once a list passed five drugs (PMID 42594508). The count may be a poor fall predictor but a useful trigger.

**New themes**
- **End-of-life and serious-illness deprescribing** arrives as a cluster with no counterpart in the previous digest: PMIDs 42533730, 42519959, 42799555 and 42126766.
- **Setting-level accountability**: *Assisted Living Has Become a High-Medication Setting With Thin Medical Oversight* (PMID 42645013) is the first study in either run to ask who formally owns the medication list in a residential setting.
- **The hospital stay as a missed checkpoint**: PMID 42530030 (drug burden unchanged in 80% of patients), PMID 42806170 (orthogeriatric co-management) and PMID 42821180 (perioperative care in the very old).

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Four studies in this batch converge on the same uncomfortable finding about medicines in advanced illness — the obstacle is not the patient. *Nearly All Advanced Lung Cancer Patients Were Flagged for a Questionable Medication* (PMID 42126766) found 89% of patients and 73% of caregivers willing to stop a drug if the doctor suggested it, while clinicians cited hesitancy and poor collaboration; *Why stopping unnecessary medicines near the end of life is so hard* (PMID 42533730) traces the failure to a conversation that never gets scheduled; *Hospice nurses are often the ones who spot the pills that no longer help* (PMID 42519959) names the specific classes — statins, blood pressure pills, diabetes drugs, vitamins — that keep going; and *Tools to Safely Cut Back Medicines Near the End of Life* (PMID 42799555) finds structured tools exist and reduce pill counts, but that almost nobody has measured whether patients end up feeling better.

**Why pitch this now:** Four separate research teams on three continents published within weeks of each other, all landing on the same conclusion — that families are more ready for this conversation than the health system is — and that reframes a topic usually written up as "doctors must deprescribe" into something a reader can actually start.

**Angle:** A practical, non-morbid piece for older adults with a serious diagnosis and the family members beside them: what preventive medicines are still doing for someone in the last phase of illness, how to open the subject without it sounding like giving up, and which questions reliably get a real answer. Secondary value for hospice, palliative and assisted living staff, who can see where the handoffs break.

**Potential headlines:**
- Most Patients Say Yes to Stopping a Pill — Their Doctors Just Rarely Ask
- The Statin That Outlives the Reason You Started It
- Four Questions Worth Asking About Every Medicine on the List When Time Is Short

**Potential outlets:**
- **Next Avenue** — regularly runs serious-illness and caregiving service journalism and would take a piece built around a conversation script rather than a scare.
- **SeniorLiving.org** — the "what to ask your prescriber" framing fits their consumer guidance format, and the medication-oversight gap ties directly to their assisted living audience.
- **McKnight's Senior Living** — the operational half of this story (who schedules the review, who documents it, who talks to the pharmacy) is squarely their beat.
- **McKnight's Long-Term Care News** — hospice and skilled nursing teams are the ones holding the STOPPFrail and OncPal tools described in PMIDs 42519959 and 42799555, and the review-versus-reactive distinction is a clinical-operations story.
