### 1. Stopping Antipsychotics Safely in People With Dementia: Tapering Reduces Falls and Confusion Risk

**Journal:** *Lancet Healthy Longevity* | **Published:** May 2026
**PMID:** 42302797 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.lanhl.2026.100852

**The study:** Using UK health records from over 134,000 people with dementia who started antipsychotic medications, researchers tracked outcomes for those who continued, tapered, or abruptly stopped their medications after 12 or 24 weeks. Among 24,822 patients who reached the 12-week mark (mean age 84, 67% women), both tapering and abrupt discontinuation reduced the risk of delirium by 2.5% and fractures by 2.8% over two years compared to continuing the medication. Importantly, stopping antipsychotics did not increase risks of death, stroke, or pneumonia.

**Why it matters:** Antipsychotics are commonly prescribed for behavioral symptoms in dementia but carry serious risks. This large study provides reassurance that these medications can be safely stopped when treatment has exceeded recommended durations, potentially preventing thousands of falls and episodes of confusion.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or a family member with dementia has been taking an antipsychotic medication for more than 12 weeks, this may be a good time to ask your doctor whether it's still needed. Research suggests stopping safely—whether gradually or all at once—can reduce your risk of falls and confusion without increasing other health risks.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Care facilities and families managing dementia should work with prescribers to regularly review antipsychotic medications. Guidelines recommend short-term use only, yet many residents remain on these drugs indefinitely. This study supports implementing systematic medication reviews and deprescribing protocols in memory care settings.

**Caveats:** Observational design using electronic health records (cannot establish causation); UK population may not generalize to all settings; some outcomes relied on hospital coding.

### 2. One in Five Older Adults in Taiwan Reports Feeling Lonely—Simple Screening Questions Work Well

**Journal:** *Clinical Interventions in Aging* | **Published:** June 10, 2026
**PMID:** 42299339 | **DOI:** 10.2147/CIA.S604568

**The study:** Researchers surveyed 186 community-dwelling adults aged 65 and older in Southern Taiwan using three different loneliness measures. Between 18% and 27% reported significant loneliness depending on the measure used. Depression symptoms were the strongest predictor of loneliness. Brief screening tools—including a simple single question about feeling lonely—performed nearly as well as longer questionnaires in identifying those experiencing loneliness.

**Why it matters:** Loneliness is a serious but often overlooked health concern that increases risk for cognitive decline, heart disease, and early death. Finding that a single direct question can effectively screen for loneliness makes it practical for healthcare providers and community programs to routinely check in with older adults.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If someone asks whether you feel lonely, being honest can open doors to helpful resources. Loneliness is common and nothing to be ashamed of—about one in five older adults experiences it. Speaking up is the first step toward connection and support.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior centers, primary care practices, and home health agencies should consider adding a simple loneliness question to routine assessments. This study shows brief screening works well, and identifying lonely individuals early allows for timely intervention through social programs, volunteer visitors, or mental health support.

**Caveats:** Single-center study in Taiwan; modest sample size (N=186); cross-sectional design; findings may not generalize to other cultural contexts.

### 3. Poor Sleep Quality Linked to Unsteady Walking in Middle-Aged and Older Adults

**Journal:** *JMIR Aging* | **Published:** June 16, 2026
**PMID:** 42302301 | **DOI:** 10.2196/81630

**The study:** Seventy-two adults (mean age 71, 51% female) wore sleep monitoring rings for at least two nights and then completed walking tests with motion sensors. Those with more fragmented sleep and lower sleep stability showed greater side-to-side variability in their walking—a pattern associated with balance problems and fall risk. The relationship held even after accounting for age, BMI, and usual walking speed.

**Why it matters:** Falls are a leading cause of injury and loss of independence in older adults. This study suggests that improving sleep quality might help maintain steadier walking, offering a potentially modifiable way to reduce fall risk.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** How well you sleep may affect how steadily you walk. If you frequently wake during the night or feel your sleep is restless, mention it to your doctor—addressing sleep problems could help protect you from falls.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Fall prevention programs should consider incorporating sleep quality assessments. For residents in senior living who experience frequent falls, evaluating and addressing sleep disturbances may be an underutilized intervention strategy worth exploring alongside traditional balance exercises.

**Caveats:** Small sample (N=72); cross-sectional design (cannot determine if poor sleep causes gait problems); 90% non-Hispanic White participants; observational only.

### 4. Machine Learning Model Predicts Kidney Injury Risk in Older Adults After Heart Surgery

**Journal:** *Clinical Interventions in Aging* | **Published:** June 11, 2026
**PMID:** 42306297 | **DOI:** 10.2147/CIA.S612843

**The study:** Researchers developed an AI-based prediction model using data from 733 older adults who underwent cardiac surgery across three Chinese hospitals. Acute kidney injury occurred in 24% of patients within the first week after surgery. The best-performing model identified high-risk patients with 78% accuracy, with key predictors including lactate levels, surgery duration, and blood clotting times.

**Why it matters:** Kidney injury after heart surgery is common in older adults and significantly worsens outcomes. Early identification of high-risk patients could allow surgical teams to take preventive measures before and during surgery.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're facing heart surgery, ask your care team about your kidney health and what steps they take to protect kidney function during the procedure. Knowing your risk factors can help you have informed conversations with your doctors.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Surgical teams and hospitals caring for older cardiac surgery patients may benefit from risk stratification tools that flag individuals needing extra kidney-protective measures. This study demonstrates that routinely collected clinical data can power such predictions.

**Caveats:** Retrospective design; Chinese population (may not generalize globally); external validation sample was small (N=110); clinical implementation not yet tested.

### 5. Walking Ability Emerges as Central to Healthy Aging in Adults Over 80

**Journal:** *GeroScience* | **Published:** June 16, 2026
**PMID:** 42303956 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11357-026-02370-0

**The study:** In a study of 319 community-dwelling Italian adults aged 80 and older (mean age 85, 67% women), researchers examined how different functional abilities—walking, cognition, vitality, mood, hearing, and vision—connect to each other and predict survival. Using network analysis, locomotion (walking ability) emerged as the central hub linking sensory function to cognition. Each 10-point improvement in overall functional capacity was associated with 32-33% lower mortality risk over 4-7 years.

**Why it matters:** For the very old, maintaining the ability to walk may be the single most important factor connecting all other aspects of health. This suggests that interventions to preserve mobility should be prioritized in the oldest adults.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Your ability to get up and move may be the most important thing you can do for your overall health as you age. Even gentle walking, standing exercises, or physical therapy to maintain mobility can have far-reaching benefits for your mind, senses, and longevity.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Care plans for the oldest-old should prioritize preserving locomotion above all else. This research suggests that walking ability acts as a gateway function—when it declines, other domains follow. Senior living communities should consider mobility preservation as a foundational care goal.

**Caveats:** Single cohort from rural Italy; observational design; functional measures derived from routine assessments rather than gold-standard tests.

### 6. Older Adults and Caregivers See Promise in Voice AI Assistants—But Want Ethical Safeguards

**Journal:** *JMIR Aging* | **Published:** June 16, 2026
**PMID:** 42305013 | **DOI:** 10.2196/79740

**The study:** Nine older adults and family caregivers participated in eight workshop sessions over four months, exploring how voice-enabled AI assistants like Alexa or Google Home might support informal caregiving. Participants envisioned AI helping with daily health tasks, providing companionship, and supporting mental health. However, they raised significant concerns about privacy, transparency, trust, affordability, and preserving human autonomy in care decisions.

**Why it matters:** As AI technology becomes more prevalent in home care, understanding what older adults and caregivers actually want—and fear—from these tools is essential for developing products that people will actually use and benefit from.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Voice assistants could help with medication reminders, health monitoring, and even companionship—but you have the right to understand how they work, what data they collect, and who can access it. Your comfort and privacy should guide any decisions about bringing this technology into your home.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Technology companies and senior living providers developing AI-assisted care tools should prioritize transparency, privacy protections, and affordability. This study reveals that older adults and caregivers are cautiously optimistic but will reject technology that feels intrusive or that undermines their control over care decisions.

**Caveats:** Very small sample (N=9); qualitative design; participants from one geographic area; speculative rather than evaluating actual technology use.

### 7. Home Heart Failure Monitoring: Technology Helps, But Human Connection Matters Most

**Journal:** *JMIR Aging* | **Published:** June 16, 2026
**PMID:** 42302267 | **DOI:** 10.2196/79797

**The study:** Researchers followed 34 heart failure patients, 28 caregivers, and 20 nurses across three primary care organizations in Quebec, Canada, during a 6-month home monitoring program. Patients used smartwatches, Bluetooth scales, voice-activated tablets, and smart pill dispensers while nurses monitored data remotely. Many participants felt more confident and supported, but some experienced frustration when technology disrupted routines or when feedback from clinicians felt limited.

**Why it matters:** Remote monitoring can extend the reach of healthcare into homes, but this study shows that technology alone isn't enough. The quality of the human relationship with care providers determines whether patients stay engaged or disengage from monitoring programs.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Home monitoring devices can help you and your care team catch heart failure warning signs early—but the technology works best when paired with regular, meaningful communication with a nurse or doctor who knows you. Don't hesitate to ask questions or share concerns about the devices.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Healthcare organizations implementing remote monitoring should invest equally in the technology and the clinical relationships that support it. Nurses need adequate time to provide personalized feedback and emotional support, not just review dashboards.

**Caveats:** Canadian healthcare context; qualitative design limits generalizability; 6-month follow-up; specific technology platform studied.

### 8. Predicting Which Older Hip Surgery Patients Will Become Frail: New Tool Shows Promise

**Journal:** *JMIR Aging* | **Published:** June 16, 2026
**PMID:** 42302293 | **DOI:** 10.2196/90705

**The study:** Following 209 older adults who underwent hip surgery at a Chinese hospital, researchers identified three distinct recovery patterns over six months: 26% maintained low frailty, 39% improved from high frailty, and 35% worsened. Using 12 predictors spanning individual characteristics, social networks, and living environment, a machine learning model accurately predicted which trajectory patients would follow. A web-based calculator was developed for clinical use.

**Why it matters:** Hip surgery is common in older adults, and frailty afterward significantly impacts recovery and independence. Identifying high-risk patients early could allow targeted interventions—like enhanced nutrition, physical therapy, or social support—before decline sets in.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or a loved one is facing hip surgery, ask about your frailty risk and what can be done before and after surgery to support the best possible recovery. Factors like your social support network and living situation matter, not just your physical health.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Orthopedic and rehabilitation teams should consider frailty trajectory prediction as part of surgical planning for older patients. This study provides a practical tool and highlights that social and environmental factors—not just medical ones—influence recovery.

**Caveats:** Single-center study in China; requires external validation; 35% of patients worsened despite prediction tool being available.

### 9. CT Scans Reveal Muscle Quality Linked to Frailty, Cognitive Decline, and Pressure Injuries

**Journal:** *GeroScience* | **Published:** June 16, 2026
**PMID:** 42303955 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11357-026-02279-8

**The study:** Among 121 older adults (aged 65+) who had abdominal CT scans, researchers measured muscle density and composition. Better muscle quality—not just muscle size—was associated with better daily functioning, less frailty, less cognitive impairment, fewer pressure injuries, and higher quality of life. The ratio of healthy muscle to fatty muscle was particularly strongly linked to frailty severity.

**Why it matters:** This study suggests that the quality of our muscles matters as much as the quantity. CT scans that older adults may already receive for other reasons could potentially be used to assess muscle health and predict who is at risk for multiple geriatric problems.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Keeping your muscles healthy through protein-rich nutrition and regular strength exercises may protect not just your physical abilities but also your thinking and skin health as you age. Ask your doctor about ways to maintain muscle quality, not just muscle mass.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Radiologists and geriatricians could collaborate to extract muscle quality information from CT scans already being performed for other indications. This opportunistic screening could identify at-risk individuals for comprehensive geriatric assessment and intervention.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design (cannot determine causation); relatively small sample (N=121); single medical center in Taiwan; correlation does not prove muscle quality causes these outcomes.

### 10. Behavioral Variability and Brain Activity Patterns May Signal Early Cognitive Decline

**Journal:** *GeroScience* | **Published:** June 17, 2026
**PMID:** 42303957 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11357-026-02369-7

**The study:** Ninety-two older adults (ages 65-89) completed a reaction-time task while their brain activity was recorded via EEG. Those scoring in the mild cognitive impairment range showed greater inconsistency in their response times and reduced "preparatory" brain activity before responding—but their average speed was no different from cognitively normal participants. These measures partially explained the relationship between age and cognitive performance.

**Why it matters:** Standard cognitive tests may miss early signs of decline. This study suggests that measuring consistency of performance and preparatory brain activity could provide more sensitive markers of cognitive vulnerability, potentially enabling earlier intervention.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you notice you're becoming more inconsistent—sometimes sharp, sometimes struggling—rather than uniformly slower, this could be worth mentioning to your doctor. Early detection of cognitive changes opens the door to interventions that may help preserve your mental abilities.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Clinicians and researchers developing cognitive screening programs might consider incorporating measures of response variability alongside traditional accuracy-based tests. Inconsistency may be an earlier warning sign than average performance decline.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design; relatively small sample (N=92); cognitive status based on screening tool, not clinical diagnosis; findings need replication in longitudinal studies.

### 11. Food Insecurity in Ghana and South Africa Linked to Disability in Older Adults—Childhood Poverty Plays a Role

**Journal:** *Journal of Nutrition, Health and Aging* | **Published:** June 15, 2026
**PMID:** 42296619 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jnha.2026.100884

**The study:** Tracking over 11,000 adults aged 60+ across Ghana and South Africa over 10 years (2007-2019), researchers found that food insecurity nearly doubled the odds of functional disability in South Africa and increased odds by 20% in Ghana. Importantly, the pathway wasn't just about current food access—parents' employment during childhood and the person's own education level partially explained the connection, suggesting lifelong effects of poverty.

**Why it matters:** This study reveals that disability in later life is shaped by circumstances spanning the entire life course, from childhood economic conditions through current food security. Addressing disability requires both immediate food assistance and long-term investments in economic opportunity.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're struggling to afford enough food, this can affect your physical abilities and independence. Programs that provide food assistance exist in most communities—reaching out for help is a way of protecting your health and mobility.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Policymakers and aging services organizations should recognize that functional disability has roots in lifelong economic circumstances. Interventions must address both immediate food insecurity through assistance programs and upstream factors through education and economic development policies.

**Caveats:** Observational longitudinal design; limited to Ghana and South Africa; self-reported outcomes; mediation analysis suggests pathways but cannot prove causation.

### 12. Framing Matters: Emphasizing What You Could Lose Makes People More Likely to Plan for End-of-Life Care

**Journal:** *Journal of Gerontology: Psychological Sciences* | **Published:** June 16, 2026
**PMID:** 42301908 | **DOI:** 10.1093/geronb/gbag106

**The study:** Five hundred U.S. adults ages 22-88 were randomly assigned to read about advance care planning framed as either what they could gain, what they could lose by not planning, or neutral information. Those who read the loss-framed message showed greater intention to engage in advance care planning. Interestingly, older adults were more likely to avoid seeking additional information when shown the gain-framed message, though this pattern appeared only in their actual behavior, not their self-reports.

**Why it matters:** Despite its importance, many people avoid planning for end-of-life care. Understanding how to frame these conversations effectively could help more families have crucial discussions before a health crisis forces difficult decisions.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Thinking about what could go wrong if you don't plan for future medical decisions—like having treatments you wouldn't want or burdening family members with difficult choices—might motivate you more than thinking about the benefits of planning. Consider what matters most to you and start those conversations.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Healthcare providers and senior living staff initiating advance care planning conversations might consider framing the discussion around what could be lost without a plan—loss of control over decisions, family conflict, unwanted treatments—rather than only emphasizing positive benefits of planning.

**Caveats:** Online survey-based experimental design; participants were not followed to see if intentions translated to actual planning behavior; U.S. sample only.

### 13. Infection Prevention in Nursing Homes: Multi-Strategy Approaches Work Best

**Journal:** *Lancet Healthy Longevity* | **Published:** June 16, 2026
**PMID:** 42302810 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.lanhl.2026.100859

**The study:** Researchers systematically reviewed 25 studies examining infection prevention strategies in long-term care facilities for older adults. Multifaceted interventions using WHO's multimodal improvement strategy (combining system change, education, monitoring, feedback, and safety culture) showed effectiveness in six of eight studies. Vaccination programs also showed positive results. Evidence for single interventions like education alone, hand hygiene improvement alone, or air quality measures was inconclusive.

**Why it matters:** Infections in nursing homes cause significant illness and death among residents. This review confirms that comprehensive, multi-pronged approaches to infection control are more effective than relying on any single strategy.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** When choosing a care facility, ask about their infection prevention practices. Facilities using comprehensive approaches—not just hand sanitizer stations—are following the best available evidence. Staying current on vaccinations is also one of the most effective protections available.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Long-term care administrators should implement infection prevention programs that combine multiple strategies simultaneously: system changes, staff education, monitoring and feedback, and building a culture of safety. Single-intervention approaches like annual hand hygiene training alone are insufficient.

**Caveats:** High risk of bias in most included studies (19 of 25); significant heterogeneity across studies; evidence predominantly from high-income countries; may not generalize to resource-limited settings.

### 14. Roadmap Published for Delivering Mental Health Care to Rural Older Veterans via Telehealth

**Journal:** *JMIR Aging* | **Published:** June 16, 2026
**PMID:** 42302264 | **DOI:** 10.2196/95286

**The study:** Over three years, researchers interviewed 25 clinicians from all eight VA tele-geriatric mental health teams at multiple time points. They identified three key phases for successful implementation: pre-implementation (engaging leadership, securing funding, defining services), scale-up (advertising, addressing challenges, refining approaches), and sustainment (ongoing maintenance). Barriers included unfamiliarity with local aging resources; facilitators included adapting strategies to local contexts and engaging referring clinicians.

**Why it matters:** Rural older adults often lack access to specialized mental health care. This study provides a practical blueprint for healthcare systems seeking to extend geriatric mental health services to underserved areas through telehealth.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you live in a rural area and need mental health support, ask whether telehealth options are available. Many health systems are expanding virtual access to specialists who can help with depression, anxiety, memory concerns, and other issues—you shouldn't have to travel hours for quality care.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Healthcare organizations looking to extend geriatric mental health services to rural populations can use this roadmap to guide implementation. Success requires sustained effort across multiple phases, strong leadership engagement, and adaptation to local contexts and resources.

**Caveats:** Qualitative study of VA system only; implementation experiences may differ in non-VA healthcare settings; rural barriers vary by region.

### 15. Cognitive Training Games Show Promise for Older Adults With Hearing Loss

**Journal:** *JMIR Aging* | **Published:** June 16, 2026
**PMID:** 42302249 | **DOI:** 10.2196/84083

**The study:** Sixty older adults (mean age 68, 75% male) with mild-to-moderate age-related hearing loss were randomized to either a web-based auditory-cognitive training program or a waitlist control. After the intervention, the training group showed significant improvements in attention measures and some cognitive domains (naming, visual cognition), as well as reduced emotional impact of hearing difficulties. Participants found the gamified training engaging and reported increased confidence.

**Why it matters:** Hearing loss affects quality of life and is linked to cognitive decline. This culturally adapted Chinese-language training program shows that combining hearing and cognitive exercises in an engaging, game-like format may help address both challenges simultaneously.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have hearing difficulties, training programs that exercise both your hearing and thinking skills might offer benefits beyond hearing aids alone. Ask your audiologist or doctor about cognitive-auditory training options that might be available in your language.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior centers and audiology practices might consider offering structured auditory-cognitive training programs as a complement to traditional hearing rehabilitation. The gamified, web-based approach makes it accessible for home use and may improve engagement.

**Caveats:** Pilot study with small sample (N=60); predominantly male participants; waitlist control (not active comparison); Chinese-language program may not generalize to other languages; short-term follow-up only.

### 16. Community Eye Screenings Uncover Major Unmet Vision Needs in Older Adults

**Journal:** *Aging and Disease* | **Published:** June 10, 2026
**PMID:** 42295093 | **DOI:** 10.14336/AD.2026.10331

**The study:** In a Chinese community screening program, 3,459 adults aged 60+ (91% participation rate) received comprehensive eye exams integrated into routine health check-ups. Nearly half (48.5%) needed advanced eye care. Cataracts affected 43.5%, age-related macular degeneration 37.5%, and glaucoma suspicion 12.4%. After two years, younger participants (60-69) showed vision improvements, while those 70-74 showed progressive decline despite screening.

**Why it matters:** Many older adults have treatable eye conditions that go undetected. Integrating eye screenings into existing community health check-ups dramatically increases access and reveals substantial unmet needs that, if addressed, could preserve vision and quality of life.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Regular eye exams can catch problems early when treatment is most effective. If your community offers vision screenings during health check-ups, take advantage of them—and follow through with any recommended specialist visits. Protecting your eyesight helps you stay independent.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Primary care and public health programs should consider integrating basic eye screenings into routine health assessments for older adults. This study demonstrates high feasibility and substantial unmet need. The challenge is ensuring those identified with problems complete referrals to eye specialists.

**Caveats:** Chinese population may not generalize globally; low referral completion rates noted as a limitation; two-year follow-up showed mixed results by age group.

### 17. Cervical Cancer Screening Remains Important for Older Women—But Requires Adjusted Approaches

**Journal:** *Current Aging Science* | **Published:** June 11, 2026
**PMID:** 42304742 | **DOI:** 10.2174/0118746098435866260603051645

**The study:** This narrative review synthesized evidence on cervical cancer screening challenges in elderly women. Menopause-related changes cause tissue atrophy and make the transformation zone harder to visualize, leading to more unsatisfactory Pap smears and potentially missed lesions. Although HPV prevalence decreases with age, persistent high-risk HPV infections in older women are strongly associated with significant cervical abnormalities. Newer approaches including HPV testing and short-term topical estrogen show promise for improving detection.

**Why it matters:** Many older women mistakenly believe cervical cancer screening is no longer necessary after menopause or hysterectomy. This review emphasizes that risk persists and that screening approaches may need to be individualized based on hormone status and prior screening history.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Don't assume you no longer need cervical cancer screening just because you've gone through menopause. If you've never had regular screenings or had abnormal results in the past, talk to your doctor about whether continued screening—possibly with HPV testing—is right for you.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Healthcare providers should individualize cervical screening recommendations for older women rather than applying one-size-fits-all age cutoffs. HPV testing may be more reliable than traditional Pap smears in postmenopausal women, and short-term topical estrogen can improve sample quality when needed.

**Caveats:** Narrative review (not systematic); no original data; recommendations based on synthesis of existing literature and guidelines.

### 18. Blood Proteins May Reveal Who Will Experience Both Physical and Cognitive Decline

**Journal:** *GeroScience* | **Published:** June 16, 2026
**PMID:** 42301613 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11357-026-02356-y

**The study:** Following 774 Italian adults aged 60+ for up to 15 years, researchers identified three groups: those with no decline, physical decline only, or dual decline in both walking speed and cognition. Blood analysis of over 1,300 proteins revealed that those with dual decline showed patterns indicating cellular stress, inflammation, and reduced growth factor availability. Specific proteins like GDF15 and TFF3 were elevated in dual decliners.

**Why it matters:** Understanding the biological signatures of dual decline could eventually lead to blood tests that identify at-risk individuals before symptoms become obvious, enabling earlier interventions.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Scientists are working to understand the biological changes that predict who might experience declines in both thinking and physical abilities as they age. While blood tests for this aren't yet available clinically, staying active and managing inflammation through healthy lifestyle choices may help protect both your mind and body.
- **About older adults and seniors:** This research moves toward precision medicine approaches for aging. Future clinical applications might include blood-based biomarker panels to stratify risk and guide preventive interventions. The convergence on inflammation-related proteins supports anti-inflammatory strategies as potentially protective.

**Caveats:** Observational design; Italian population; proteins measured at baseline only (no longitudinal proteomics); findings need replication; no immediate clinical applications.
