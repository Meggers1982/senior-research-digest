### 1. Weak bones raise the odds of implant failure after elbow replacement

**Journal:** *Geriatr Orthop Surg Rehabil* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 12
**PMID:** 42733408 | **DOI:** 10.1177/21514593261490230

**The study:** Researchers reviewed records from 1,410 adults who had total elbow replacement surgery and were followed at least two years, matching patients with osteoporosis to similar patients without it on demographics, other conditions, surgical reason, and lab markers. Those with osteoporosis had roughly three times the rate of fracture around the implant (4.4% vs. 1.4%) and more than double the rate of implant loosening (5.0% vs. 2.3%). Rates of revision surgery, infection, nerve injury, and instability were similar between groups. Among the 520 osteoporosis patients, those taking bone medication had a lower fracture rate, but the difference fell just short of statistical significance.

**Why it matters:** Elbow replacement is often done in older adults with rheumatoid arthritis or fractures, and this suggests bone quality is a meaningful driver of whether the implant holds up over the following two years.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If elbow replacement surgery has been raised as an option for you, it is reasonable to ask your surgeon whether your bone density has been checked and whether bone health should be addressed before the operation is scheduled.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For care teams and family members coordinating orthopedic surgery, this is a prompt to make sure osteoporosis evaluation is part of the pre-surgical workup rather than an afterthought, and to anticipate closer monitoring of the implant afterward.

**Caveats:** Retrospective and observational, so it cannot prove osteoporosis causes implant failure; database-matched design may miss unmeasured differences; the medication analysis was secondary and underpowered; several authors report extensive industry consulting and royalty relationships with orthopedic device manufacturers.

### 2. Five years of repeat romosozumab courses: one clinic's real-world bone density results

**Journal:** *JBMR Plus* | **Published:** 2026 Aug 27
**PMID:** 42769300 | **DOI:** 10.1093/jbmrpl/ziag149

**The study:** A single osteoporosis clinic in Tokyo tracked 68 high-risk patients who began romosozumab between 2020 and 2026, with 49 completing five years of follow-up. Patients took romosozumab for 12 months, then monthly oral ibandronate for 3 months, then were reassessed for another course, all while taking a daily vitamin D analog. Spine bone density rose by about 19.6 percentage points of the young-adult mean and hip density by 9.9 points over five years, pain scores dropped sharply, and about 72% of patients were still on treatment at five years. One recurrent spinal fracture occurred and no cardiovascular events, jaw osteonecrosis, or atypical femur fractures were recorded.

**Why it matters:** Romosozumab is normally given as a single 12-month course, so real-world data on repeating it matters for people with severe osteoporosis who run out of options after the first year.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have severe osteoporosis and have already completed a course of romosozumab, you can ask your bone specialist what the evidence says about repeat courses and what follow-up treatment plan protects the gains you made — but note this is early, uncontrolled data, not a basis for changing treatment on its own.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living staff and family caregivers, the standout practical finding is persistence: nearly three in ten patients dropped off treatment over five years, a reminder that injection schedules and reassessment appointments need active tracking.

**Caveats:** Small sample (N=68, 49 analyzed), single-center, retrospective with no control group, and baseline data were missing for the 19 patients who discontinued; pain was self-reported; the authors themselves describe the findings as descriptive and hypothesis-generating; Japanese clinic population may not generalize.

### 3. Which older adults keep returning to the emergency department — and what makes the visit go badly

**Journal:** *Arch Gerontol Geriatr* | **Published:** 2026 Dec
**PMID:** 42762629 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.archger.2026.106414

**The study:** Australian researchers linked hospital and mortality records for 336,283 emergency department visits by adults aged 65 and older in New South Wales in 2018, following them for five years, and paired this with 47,461 patient experience surveys. About 10.6% were frequent presenters (four or more visits), most commonly those aged 75–84; multiple chronic conditions, living in rural areas, and living in aged care raised the odds. Negative care experiences were driven most strongly by inadequate information about treatment (more than five times the odds), along with waits of an hour or more and complications.

**Why it matters:** It identifies exactly which older adults cycle through emergency care and shows that communication, not just wait times, determines whether the experience feels good or bad.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you find yourself in the emergency department, the single factor most tied to a bad experience was not being given enough information about your treatment — so ask directly what is being done, why, and what happens next, and bring someone who can ask with you.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For aged care operators and family caregivers: residents of aged care facilities and people with multiple chronic conditions were among the most frequent presenters, which strengthens the case for better in-house escalation plans, advance care documentation, and after-hours clinical support to head off avoidable transfers.

**Caveats:** Observational design cannot establish causation; patient experience outcomes were self-reported and surveys came from a subset of patients; single Australian state health system may not generalize; interestingly, osteoporosis and frailty were associated with lower odds of frequent presentation, which may reflect coding patterns rather than real protection.

### 4. The Mediterranean diet and your bones: what the evidence actually shows

**Journal:** *Clin Nutr ESPEN* | **Published:** 2026 Oct
**PMID:** 42641790 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.clnesp.2026.105057

**The study:** This narrative review synthesized research on how closely following a Mediterranean eating pattern — fruits, vegetables, whole grains, legumes, nuts, olive oil, moderate fish, little red meat — relates to osteoporosis and fracture risk. The authors report that observational studies consistently find lower fracture risk with higher adherence, while results for bone mineral density itself are mixed. Proposed mechanisms include reduced oxidative stress and chronic inflammation, gut microbiome effects, and better nutrient availability from polyphenols, omega-3s, vitamins, and minerals.

**Why it matters:** Diet is one of the few bone-protective levers older adults control entirely themselves, and this suggests the overall eating pattern may matter more for fractures than any single nutrient.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** You don't need to overhaul everything at once: shifting toward more vegetables, legumes, nuts, olive oil, and fish is a low-risk change associated with fewer fractures — just treat it as a complement to, not a replacement for, calcium, vitamin D, and any prescribed bone medication.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For dining directors and family members doing the cooking, this is a practical menu-planning argument: Mediterranean-style meals are affordable, familiar, and support bone health alongside the cardiovascular benefits already well established in older populations.

**Caveats:** Narrative review, not a systematic review or new data; the supporting evidence is largely observational and cannot establish causation; findings on bone mineral density are inconsistent; the authors note few long-term randomized trials exist.

### 5. Women with epilepsy face added bone and heart risks at menopause — and little research to guide care

**Journal:** *Maturitas* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 22
**PMID:** 42810186 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.maturitas.2026.109134

**The study:** A joint expert panel from the European Menopause and Andropause Society and the Centre for Global Epilepsy reviewed the literature and reached consensus on managing menopause in women with epilepsy. They conclude these women may reach menopause earlier and are more likely than other women to develop cardiovascular disease, osteoporosis, and cognitive impairment. The panel notes a striking evidence gap: women with epilepsy are routinely excluded from trials of menopause and osteoporosis treatments, so management must be individualized, with transdermal estrogen often preferred and careful attention to interactions between hormone therapy and antiseizure medications.

**Why it matters:** Long-term antiseizure medication use is a known contributor to bone loss, and this flags a sizable group of older women whose fracture risk may be underappreciated by both their neurologist and their primary care doctor.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have epilepsy and are at or past menopause, it's worth asking whether your bone density has ever been measured and whether your specific antiseizure medications affect bone — and if hormone therapy is discussed, ask specifically about drug interactions with your seizure medication.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and care staff supporting an older woman with epilepsy, the takeaway is coordination: neurology and women's health or primary care need to be talking to each other, since neither specialty alone typically owns fracture prevention in this group.

**Caveats:** This is an expert consensus clinical guide, not new primary research; the authors explicitly note the underlying evidence base is thin because women with epilepsy are commonly excluded from trials; recommendations reflect expert opinion where data are absent.

### 6. Big vitamin D doses didn't prevent broken bones in a 5-year trial of older adults

**Journal:** *Bone* | **Published:** 2026 Dec;213:118081. Epub 2026 Sep 7
**PMID:** 42704968 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.bone.2026.118081

**The study:** The Finnish Vitamin D Trial randomized 2,495 older adults (men 60 and over, women 65 and over) to 1,600 IU/day of vitamin D3, 3,200 IU/day, or placebo for five years, then tracked fractures through national health registries for an average of 7.3 years. Neither dose reduced fractures; in fact, the 1,600 IU group recorded more fractures than placebo (HR 1.40 over extended follow-up), while the 3,200 IU group showed no significant difference. Participants started out largely vitamin D-sufficient, with a mean baseline blood level of about 75 nmol/L, and supplementation pushed levels to roughly 100 and 120 nmol/L. Age, sex, BMI, prior fracture or fall, osteoporosis diagnosis, and calcium use did not change the results.

**Why it matters:** Millions of older adults take high-dose vitamin D specifically hoping to protect their bones; this large, long randomized trial suggests that if you already have adequate vitamin D, extra doses are not buying you fracture protection.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're taking high-dose vitamin D mainly for bone protection, this is a good reason to ask your doctor what your actual blood level is and whether the dose still makes sense — the benefit in this trial appeared only in theory, not in the fracture counts. Vitamin D still matters for people who are genuinely deficient, so the question to raise is 'am I deficient?' rather than 'should I just take more?'
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living and care staff who manage medication lists, this trial argues for reviewing routine high-dose vitamin D supplementation as part of deprescribing conversations, and for redirecting fracture-prevention energy toward proven measures like strength and balance training, fall-hazard removal, and screening for osteoporosis treatment eligibility.

**Caveats:** Participants were mostly vitamin D-sufficient at baseline and largely Finnish, so results may not generalize to deficient or institutionalized populations; the apparent excess of fractures in the 1,600 IU arm but not the higher-dose arm has no dose-response logic and may be a chance finding; several authors report grants and travel support from foundations and industry (including Abiogen Pharma SpA and Mars Edge, outside the submitted work).

### 7. One in six adults over 40 had a spinal fracture they didn't know about

**Journal:** *Bone* | **Published:** 2026 Dec;213:118054. Epub 2026 Aug 20
**PMID:** 42624233 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.bone.2026.118054

**The study:** Spanish researchers brought a mobile bone-density scanner to 15 primary care centers across six provinces and used Vertebral Fracture Assessment to image the spines of 1,082 adults aged 40 to 80. They found 244 vertebral fractures in 171 people — 15.9% of the sample — most of them mild (grade 1), affecting the front of the vertebra, and clustered at the thoracolumbar junction. Fractures were more common in men (19.3% vs 12.5% in women), in older participants, and in those with lower hip bone density, prior fragility fractures, or an osteoporosis diagnosis; about 4.8% had true clinical fragility fractures, which skewed toward older women.

**Why it matters:** Vertebral fractures are the most common osteoporotic fracture and a strong predictor of future breaks, yet most cause no memorable pain and go undetected — meaning a large group of older adults who would qualify for bone-protective treatment never get offered it.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you've lost height, developed a stooped posture, or had any fragility fracture, ask whether your bone density scan can include a vertebral fracture assessment — it's a low-radiation add-on done on the same machine and can reveal a silent spinal fracture that changes your treatment plan. Men, take note: in this study they had the higher rate, even though osteoporosis is often framed as a women's disease.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and care professionals, the practical takeaway is that unexplained height loss, a new hunch in the upper back, or a seemingly minor fall followed by vague back pain are worth flagging to a physician rather than writing off as normal aging.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional observational design — it describes prevalence and associations but cannot establish cause or predict individual outcomes; participants were volunteers recruited through primary care in Spain and may not represent other populations; the lead author reports financial support from UCB Spain and Stada, and equipment/support from Hologic, which manufactured the DXA unit used.

### 8. Older adults scored under 30% on a basic osteoporosis quiz — and men did worst

**Journal:** *Bone* | **Published:** 2026 Dec;213:118042. Epub 2026 Aug 14
**PMID:** 42600719 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.bone.2026.118042

**The study:** Researchers surveyed 1,432 Australian volunteers aged 50 and older (mean age 64) using a validated online questionnaire covering osteoporosis risk factors, attitudes, and intentions across six topics. The average knowledge score was 29.5 out of 100. The weakest area by far was knowing which exercises improve balance (11.1 out of 100); the strongest was identifying food sources of calcium (35.0). Being male more than doubled the odds of landing in the lowest knowledge tertile, and older age, lower education, and having no family history of osteoporosis also predicted poor knowledge.

**Why it matters:** People can't act on prevention advice they've never received, and the biggest gap — balance exercise — is precisely the behavior most directly tied to avoiding the falls that cause fractures.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you couldn't name a balance exercise right now, you're in the majority — ask your doctor or a physiotherapist for a specific, named balance and weight-bearing routine rather than general advice to 'stay active.' Knowing calcium sources isn't enough; the exercise side of bone protection is where most people in this survey came up empty.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living activity directors and family caregivers can use this as a prompt to audit their own programming: are balance-specific sessions offered and explained as bone protection, and are men being reached, given that they scored substantially worse and are often left out of osteoporosis messaging entirely?

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional survey of self-selected healthy online volunteers in Australia, so results may not generalize to frailer or less digitally connected older adults; all measures are self-reported knowledge and intentions, not actual behavior or fracture outcomes; no control group or follow-up.

### 9. 162 Trials, One Message: Exercise Builds Bone — But the Payoff Depends on Where You Measure

**Journal:** *Bone* | **Published:** 2026 Dec (Epub 2026 Aug 10)
**PMID:** 42575297 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.bone.2026.118044

**The study:** Researchers pooled 162 randomized controlled trials covering 10,475 adults aged 18 to 81.6 years to compare how different exercise types affect bone mineral density at six skeletal sites. Exercise produced small-to-moderate but statistically significant gains at five of six sites, with the strongest effect at the femoral neck (Hedges' g = 0.28), followed by total body, Ward's triangle, lumbar spine, and the trochanter; total hip evidence was rated very low certainty. An exploratory dose analysis pointed to a peak benefit around 1,100 MET-minutes per week for lumbar spine density, though the authors stress that modality rankings and dose estimates are hypothesis-generating, not prescriptions.

**Why it matters:** This is the largest synthesis to date confirming that structured exercise measurably improves bone density in adults, including at the femoral neck — the hip site most relevant to devastating fragility fractures.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have been told your bone density is low, this analysis supports exercise as a real contributor — not a substitute for medication, but a meaningful addition. Roughly 1,100 MET-minutes a week is a lot of activity (think brisk walking plus resistance work most days), so ask your doctor or a physical therapist what intensity and loading your skeleton and joints can safely handle, and whether combined aerobic plus resistance training makes sense for you.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living activity directors and rehab staff: the site-specific findings argue against generic 'movement' programming. Programs that include progressive resistance and weight-bearing loading target the hip and spine; chair-based or purely aerobic classes are unlikely to shift bone density, even if they help with balance and mood.

**Caveats:** Meta-analysis of trials with wide variation in protocols; network comparisons had wide credible intervals and low certainty from imprecision, heterogeneity, and sparse direct evidence. Dose-response findings were explicitly labeled exploratory and boundary-sensitive. Age range spans young adults through 81, so results are not specific to older adults.

### 10. Broken Wrist, Missed Diagnosis: Nationwide Data Show Osteoporosis Goes Unrecorded After Many Fragility Fractures

**Journal:** *Bone* | **Published:** 2026 Nov (Epub 2026 Jul 14)
**PMID:** 42448012 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.bone.2026.118014

**The study:** Austrian researchers analyzed nationwide inpatient insurance data on 1,705,684 hospitalized patients aged 50-79 between 2003 and 2014, mapping which diagnoses cluster around osteoporosis and which fractures get coded alongside it. Hip fractures showed strong co-occurrence with an osteoporosis diagnosis, especially in women aged 60 and older — but forearm, wrist/hand, and humerus fractures showed no significant osteoporosis co-diagnosis in any age group, despite being classic early warning fragility fractures. Women had far broader osteoporosis-linked comorbidity networks than men (330 versus 164 linked diagnoses at ages 70-79), and under-recognition appeared worst in younger patients and in men.

**Why it matters:** A wrist or upper-arm break after a minor fall is often the first signal of fragile bones, and this analysis suggests those signals are routinely not acted on — leaving people untreated until a hip fracture happens.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you are over 50 and have broken a wrist, forearm, or upper arm from a fall from standing height, do not let it be filed away as bad luck. Ask directly: 'Should I have a bone density scan and a fracture risk assessment?' Men in particular should ask, because this data suggests men's osteoporosis is the most likely to go unrecorded.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and senior living clinical staff: build a 'first fracture' trigger into your follow-up routine. When a resident returns from the emergency department with any low-trauma fracture — not just a hip — flag it for bone health review at the next primary care visit, since the hospital record may never mention osteoporosis at all.

**Caveats:** Retrospective analysis of administrative billing data, which reflects coding practice rather than confirmed clinical status; cannot establish causation. Data are from Austria and 2003-2014, so coding patterns may differ by country and may have improved since. Limited to hospitalized patients aged 50-79.

### 11. When Your Spine Scan and Your MRI Disagree: Metabolic Clues to Hidden Bone Weakness

**Journal:** *Bone* | **Published:** 2026 Nov (Epub 2026 Jul 23)
**PMID:** 42492717 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.bone.2026.118029

**The study:** Researchers in China retrospectively reviewed 266 adults who had both a lumbar MRI and a DXA bone density scan within six months before spine surgery, comparing an MRI-derived vertebral bone quality (VBQ) score against DXA T-scores. VBQ correlated strongly with age (r = 0.706) but only moderately with DXA scores, and it detected DXA-defined osteoporosis with modest accuracy (AUC 0.728). Patients whose DXA looked normal but whose VBQ looked abnormal tended to share a metabolic signature: higher triglycerides, VLDL cholesterol, glycated albumin, and conjugated bilirubin.

**Why it matters:** DXA scans of the lumbar spine can be falsely reassuring in older adults because arthritis and degenerative changes inflate the reading — this study suggests an MRI many spine patients already receive may carry extra information about bone quality.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you are heading toward spine surgery and already having an MRI, it is reasonable to ask your surgeon whether the images say anything about your bone quality, and whether your spine DXA result might be inflated by arthritis. Metabolic markers like triglycerides and blood sugar control came up in this analysis — worth discussing as part of overall bone health, though this study cannot show that treating them protects bone.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For care teams: a 'normal' lumbar DXA in an older adult with significant spinal degeneration should not close the book on osteoporosis risk. Hip-site measurements and clinical fracture history remain essential context when advising families about fall precautions.

**Caveats:** Retrospective, single-center, observational design in surgical patients — cannot establish causation and may not generalize to the general older population. Moderate sample (N=266) and modest diagnostic accuracy (positive predictive value only 30.1%). No fracture outcomes were measured.

### 12. Can What You Eat Speed Up Fracture Healing? A Review Finds Promise but Thin Proof

**Journal:** *Bone* | **Published:** 2026 Nov (Epub 2026 Jul 20)
**PMID:** 42476484 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.bone.2026.118017

**The study:** Dutch researchers screened 7,709 publications and included 59 in vivo studies (human and animal) testing whether oral nutritional supplements improve fracture healing or bone-forming cell activity. Supplements fell into seven categories — proteins and amino acids, plant-derived nutrients and polyphenols, minerals, vitamins, fats, and others — and about two-thirds of studies reported promising effects, with citrulline, protein supplements, fruit and vegetable extracts, and flavonoids singled out for further research. The authors concluded that small sample sizes and wide variation between studies prevent any definitive recommendations.

**Why it matters:** Nutrition is one of the few fracture-recovery levers older adults and their families can actually control, but this review shows the evidence is still too preliminary to justify buying specific supplements.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you are recovering from a fracture, focus first on what the evidence does support — adequate overall protein and calories — and talk to your doctor or a dietitian before spending money on citrulline, flavonoid, or polyphenol products marketed for bone healing. The research behind those is early and mostly not from large human trials.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For caregivers and senior living dining teams: the practical takeaway is less about novel supplements and more about screening for undernutrition during fracture recovery, when appetite often drops exactly when protein needs rise. Be prepared for residents and families to ask about supplements they have seen advertised — the honest answer is that the evidence is not there yet.

**Caveats:** Scoping review, not a meta-analysis; includes animal studies alongside human ones and excluded osteoporosis-specific research. Included studies had small sample sizes and substantial heterogeneity, and no pooled effect estimate was calculated.

### 13. A Bigger Fracture-Risk Calculator: Falls and Recent Breaks Push Many Patients Into Treatment Territory

**Journal:** *JBMR Plus* | **Published:** 2026 Jul 28 (print 2026 Oct)
**PMID:** 42689245 | **DOI:** 10.1093/jbmrpl/ziag120

**The study:** Researchers applied the newer FRAXplus tool — which adds factors such as recent falls, lumbar spine bone density, trabecular bone score, glucocorticoid dose, and how recently and where a prior fracture occurred — to 28,708 people in the Swiss Osteoporosis Registry (88% women, average age 65.8). Median 10-year risk of a major osteoporotic fracture rose from 16% with standard FRAX to 18% with FRAXplus, with 72% of people getting a higher estimate (average increase of 4.8 percentage points) and 27% a lower one. Having at least one fall in the past year and a recent spine or hip fracture produced the biggest individual changes, and 16% of people were reclassified upward into a higher-risk category under Swiss treatment thresholds.

**Why it matters:** The same patient can land on either side of the "treat or don't treat" line depending on which calculator the clinician uses, and details such as a fall last year or a fracture six months ago carry real weight.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you've fallen in the past year or broken a bone recently, make sure your doctor knows the details and dates — those specifics can meaningfully raise your calculated fracture risk and may change whether osteoporosis treatment is recommended. It's reasonable to ask whether your risk score accounted for falls, recent fractures, and spine bone density, not just a hip scan.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For care staff and family: documenting falls accurately — including unwitnessed ones and near-misses — now has direct consequences for a resident's fracture risk classification and medication decisions, making fall logs a clinical tool rather than an administrative chore.

**Caveats:** Registry-based analysis of risk estimates, not actual fracture outcomes; hip axis length and diabetes duration data were unavailable; thresholds are Swiss-specific and may not translate to other countries; many authors report honoraria or consultancy fees from Amgen, UCB, Eli Lilly and others, and one author is a FRAX design team member and director of Osteoporosis Research Ltd.

### 14. A Blood Test May Show Who Can Safely Wait Longer Between Osteoporosis Infusions

**Journal:** *Bone* | **Published:** 2026 Nov (Epub 2026 Jul 3)
**PMID:** 42398845 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.bone.2026.118003

**The study:** Fifty treatment-naive postmenopausal Indian women with DXA-confirmed osteoporosis received a single 5 mg intravenous zoledronic acid infusion. At 12 months, those whose bone turnover marker β-CTX remained below 300 pg/mL had their next dose deferred and were monitored every 12 weeks; 25 women ended up with delayed redosing at a median of 18.5 months. Lower baseline β-CTX and lower BMI each independently predicted eligibility for delay, and bone density gains were comparable between groups with no new vertebral fractures over two years.

**Why it matters:** Annual infusions are the default, but this suggests some women may be able to stretch the interval based on a blood marker — fewer infusions, fewer side effects, lower cost — though only with monitoring.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you receive yearly zoledronic acid infusions, you can ask your doctor whether bone turnover marker testing (β-CTX) is appropriate for you and what it might show about your dosing schedule — but don't skip or delay a scheduled infusion on your own; the women in this study were monitored every three months.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and care coordinators: a biomarker-guided schedule means more lab visits, not fewer appointments overall, so weigh transportation and monitoring burden against the appeal of fewer infusions when discussing options with the prescriber.

**Caveats:** Small sample (N=50), single-center, observational with no randomized comparison group, short two-year follow-up with fracture outcomes too few to assess, and findings in postmenopausal Indian women may not generalize to other populations.

### 15. When an Osteoporosis Pill Burns: A Nursing Home Case Highlights a Swallowing Danger

**Journal:** *Geriatrics (Basel)* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 8
**PMID:** 42776717 | **DOI:** 10.3390/geriatrics11050128

**The study:** Clinicians in Cork, Ireland describe a 73-year-old nursing home resident with advanced vascular dementia who developed sudden oral pain, reduced food intake, lethargy and facial swelling. She had been taking monthly ibandronic acid for two years despite documented difficulty swallowing tablets and no formal swallowing assessment; examination showed ulceration of the lower lip and tongue. The cause was chemical mucosal injury from the bisphosphonate tablet being retained in the mouth rather than swallowed; after stopping the drug and using topical treatment, the ulcers healed in three weeks and she was switched to injectable denosumab.

**Why it matters:** Oral bisphosphonates require sitting upright and swallowing with a full glass of water — instructions that people with dementia or swallowing problems may not be able to follow, turning a routine bone drug into a painful injury.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or someone you care for takes a bisphosphonate tablet and has any trouble swallowing pills, raise it with the prescriber — a swallowing assessment or a switch to an injectable bone medication may be safer. New mouth pain, ulcers or facial swelling after a dose deserve prompt medical attention and a medication review.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living and nursing staff: this is a direct prompt to audit which residents with cognitive impairment or dysphagia are still on oral bisphosphonates, and to verify that administration protocols (upright posture, full water, observed swallow) are actually achievable for each of them. Current guidelines don't explicitly address dysphagia or cognition, so local protocols matter.

**Caveats:** Single case report (N=1) from one hospital — it illustrates a recognized risk and cannot quantify how often it occurs or prove the drug was the cause beyond clinical judgment.

### 16. Postmenopausal Women Warm to a Talking Speaker That Coaches Them Through Osteoporosis Care

**Journal:** *Bone* | **Published:** 2026 Nov (Epub 2026 Jul 13)
**PMID:** 42442524 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.bone.2026.118011

**The study:** Australian researchers interviewed 22 of 25 postmenopausal women who had spent 12 months in the intervention arm of a randomized trial using a digital voice assistant device that delivered osteoporosis education videos, medication reminders, home exercise sessions, nutrition advice and monthly quizzes. Thematic analysis found strong support for the program's appropriateness and acceptability, moderate support for adoption, fidelity, feasibility and sustainability, and limited support on cost. Women particularly valued clear audiovisual guidance, natural conversational interaction, and flexible access at home.

**Why it matters:** Bone health depends heavily on day-to-day self-management — taking medication on schedule, doing weight-bearing exercise, getting enough calcium and protein — and voice-based devices appear to be an acceptable support for older women who may not use apps.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you struggle to keep up with osteoporosis medication timing or your home exercise routine, a voice assistant or smart speaker reminder system may be worth trying — the women in this study found spoken, conversational prompts easier to live with than written instructions. Ask your clinic whether any structured digital bone-health program is available to you.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living operators considering technology purchases: participants flagged the need for streamlined content, reliable delivery, accessible tech support and clear privacy rules — practical procurement criteria, and a reminder that cost was the weakest-supported dimension in this evaluation.

**Caveats:** Small qualitative sample (N=22) drawn only from the intervention arm with no comparison group; self-reported experiences rather than measured bone or adherence outcomes; one author reports financial support from Amgen, another has a relationship with the device company Great Australian Pty. Ltd., and a co-author is Editor-in-Chief of the journal.

### 17. Routine X-Rays After Hip Fracture Surgery Changed Nothing in 1,300 Patients

**Journal:** *Geriatric Orthopaedic Surgery & Rehabilitation* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 11
**PMID:** 42732263 | **DOI:** 10.1177/21514593261484984

**The study:** Israeli surgeons reviewed 1,303 consecutive patients who had partial hip replacement (hemiarthroplasty) for a broken hip and attended routine follow-up with X-rays between six weeks and three months after surgery. Not a single routine radiograph in a patient without symptoms led to a change in weight-bearing advice or a decision to re-operate (0%, 95% CI 0.0–0.28%). All 70 patients who did need further surgery during that window had arrived at the emergency department with symptoms first.

**Why it matters:** It suggests the standard early post-op X-ray in a comfortable, well-functioning hip fracture patient adds radiation, cost and a trip to the clinic without changing care — while symptoms, not images, are what actually signal trouble.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're recovering well after hip fracture surgery, it's reasonable to ask your surgeon whether a routine follow-up X-ray will actually change anything in your care. What matters most is reporting new or worsening pain, inability to bear weight, or a change in leg position promptly — those symptoms, not a scheduled image, are what prompt action.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and transport coordinators: this supports prioritizing symptom monitoring and education over getting a frail resident to a routine imaging appointment, and gives you a concrete evidence point when discussing follow-up plans with the surgical team.

**Caveats:** Single-center retrospective study (cannot establish causation and may reflect one institution's surgical technique and follow-up culture); patients who did not attend follow-up were not captured; median follow-up was only 52 days, so later complications weren't assessed; cementless hemiarthroplasty only.

### 18. Trouble Chewing or Speaking Clearly Predicted Frailty Four Years Later — Especially in Women

**Journal:** *GeroScience* | **Published:** 2026 Oct (Epub 2025 Dec 2)
**PMID:** 41331404 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11357-025-02015-8

**The study:** Researchers followed 1,348 robust, non-frail community-dwelling Korean adults aged 70–84 for four years, asking at baseline about difficulty chewing and difficulty with pronunciation. Those reporting oral functional limitation were about 70% more likely to become frail (adjusted hazard ratio 1.74), and the effect held up under competing-risk analysis. The association was strong in women (HR 2.44) but absent in men, and did not differ according to whether participants had cardiovascular disease or osteoporosis.

**Why it matters:** Chewing and speech difficulties are easy to notice and easy to dismiss, yet they appear to be early warning signs of the downward spiral into frailty — which drives falls, fractures and loss of independence.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If chewing has become harder or your speech feels less clear, don't write it off as normal aging — mention it at your next medical or dental visit. Keeping up with dental care, properly fitting dentures and adequate protein intake are reasonable steps while you get the problem assessed.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For caregivers and senior living staff: a simple two-question check about chewing and pronunciation could flag residents — particularly women — who warrant closer nutrition and physical function monitoring, at essentially no cost.

**Caveats:** Observational cohort, so it cannot prove that oral problems cause frailty; oral function was self-reported with just two questions rather than clinically measured; findings come from a Korean cohort aged 70–84 and may not generalize elsewhere.

### 19. Belly Fat, Not Body Weight, Tracks With Weaker Bones — and the Pattern Differs by Sex

**Journal:** *Experimental Gerontology* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 15 (Epub 2026 Aug 25)
**PMID:** 42641825 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.exger.2026.113301

**The study:** Chinese researchers used low-dose chest CT scans from 927 people to measure visceral fat (the deep fat around organs), subcutaneous fat, and spine bone mineral density. After adjustment, higher visceral fat was linked to lower bone density across every age and sex group, with stronger associations in women; a higher proportion of visceral fat was linked to greater odds of low bone mass mainly in men (odds ratio 1.30). Visceral fat also appeared to partly account for the bone loss associated with older age.

**Why it matters:** It challenges the common assumption that carrying extra weight protects bones — where the fat sits may matter more than what the scale or BMI says.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Don't assume a higher body weight means your bones are protected. If you have significant abdominal weight, it's worth discussing bone density screening with your doctor alongside the usual metabolic checks — and weight-bearing and resistance exercise addresses both concerns at once.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For wellness program planners: this adds to the case for pairing strength and weight-bearing activity with metabolic health programming rather than treating bone health and waistline health as separate tracks. No immediate change to screening protocols is warranted on this evidence.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design — cannot establish causation or direction; single-center Chinese sample including adults under 45, so results may not generalize to older Western populations; bone density measured at L1–L2 by CT rather than standard DXA; mediation analyses were explicitly exploratory.

### 20. Men at High Risk of Osteoporosis Almost Never Get Bone Density Tests, U.S. Data Show

**Journal:** *Bone* | **Published:** 2026 Oct
**PMID:** 42493193 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.bone.2026.117944

**The study:** Harvard-affiliated researchers analyzed 4,573 U.S. primary care visits from the National Ambulatory Medical Care Survey (2012-2019) by patients who met Endocrine Society criteria for osteoporosis screening — men 70 and older, women 65 and older, and younger adults aged 50+ with risk factors such as very low body weight, smoking, alcohol use disorder, or steroid therapy. Median age was 72 and 61% of visits were by women. A DXA bone density scan was ordered at just 0.04% of eligible men's visits versus 5.1% of eligible women's visits — roughly a 100-fold gap — with no improvement over the eight years studied.

**Why it matters:** Men account for a substantial share of hip fractures and have higher post-fracture mortality than women, yet this analysis suggests screening that could catch their bone loss early is essentially not happening in routine primary care.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you are a man 70 or older — or 50 to 69 with risk factors like smoking, low body weight, heavy drinking, or long-term steroid use — do not assume your doctor will raise bone density. Bring it up yourself at your next visit and ask whether a DXA scan is appropriate for you.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living operators and family caregivers: male residents' fracture risk may be invisible in the medical record because no one ever measured it. Fall-prevention and wellness reviews are a natural place to flag men who have never had a bone density scan and prompt a primary care conversation.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional observational analysis of visit records — it captures orders placed at a given visit, not whether a patient was screened elsewhere or previously, so true screening rates may be somewhat higher. Guideline eligibility had to be approximated using available survey variables. Data end in 2019.

### 21. Dental Work Right Before Starting Bone Drugs Linked to Higher Jaw Complication Risk

**Journal:** *Bone* | **Published:** 2026 Oct
**PMID:** 42372806 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.bone.2026.117993

**The study:** Using a Japanese regional insurance claims database, researchers followed 104,349 patients aged 40 and older with osteoporosis or cancer who started a bisphosphonate or denosumab between 2015 and 2022, tracking medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw (MRONJ). MRONJ was rare in the osteoporosis-dose group (about 47 cases per 100,000 person-years) but far more common at cancer-level doses (about 2,116 per 100,000 person-years). Having 'oral risk treatment' — such as an extraction or other invasive dental procedure — in the month before starting therapy roughly tripled to quadrupled the risk in both groups; anemia and oral corticosteroid use were additional risk markers at the lower dose.

**Why it matters:** Fear of jaw osteonecrosis keeps many older adults from taking bone-protective drugs, and this very large study puts the risk in perspective at osteoporosis doses while pointing to a concrete, modifiable factor: the timing and condition of dental care around drug initiation.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Before you start a bisphosphonate or denosumab, ask your doctor and dentist to coordinate: get a dental check first, finish any needed extractions or other invasive work, and allow healing time rather than starting the medication right on the heels of a procedure. At standard osteoporosis doses, the absolute risk of jaw problems remains very low.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For care teams and senior living health staff: this supports building a dental review into the pre-treatment checklist for new antiresorptive prescriptions, and paying extra attention to residents who are also on oral steroids or have anemia.

**Caveats:** Retrospective claims-database study from one Japanese prefecture — observational, cannot establish causation, and unmeasured factors (such as why urgent dental work was needed) may explain part of the association. MRONJ identification relies on billing codes. Funding support was provided by Shizuoka Prefecture. Findings may not generalize beyond Japan.

### 22. Past Medication Habits Predict Who Will Stick With a New Osteoporosis Injection

**Journal:** *Bone* | **Published:** 2026 Oct
**PMID:** 42362099 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.bone.2026.117995

**The study:** Researchers examined Medicare records for 1,746 women aged 65 and older who started romosozumab — a monthly bone-building injection — in 2019, looking back 36 months at their prior osteoporosis medication use. Compared with women who had consistently taken prior therapy, those with no prior therapy were less likely to keep up with the romosozumab schedule, and those whose prior adherence had been declining were 35% more likely to stop before completing treatment. Taking antidepressants and/or blood pressure medications at the time of initiation also predicted higher discontinuation.

**Why it matters:** Romosozumab is given as a fixed 12-month course and much of its fracture-prevention benefit depends on completing the doses on schedule, so identifying who is likely to fall off early allows for reminders and support before the course unravels.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you are starting a monthly bone injection, treat the schedule as a 12-month commitment: set calendar reminders, book all your appointments up front, and tell your clinician honestly if you have struggled to stay on bone medications before so they can build in extra support.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and senior living nursing staff: a resident's history of drifting off osteoporosis pills is a practical early warning that the new injectable will likely be missed too. Adding these residents to a medication-tracking or appointment-escort list is a low-cost intervention.

**Caveats:** Observational Medicare claims analysis — associations only, no causation; limited to women 65+ in the first year after the drug's approval, so patterns may differ now. Several authors have received research support from or consulted for Amgen, the manufacturer of romosozumab.

### 23. After a Hip Fracture, Fewer Than a Third of Patients Start Bone Medication — But the Number Is Rising

**Journal:** *Bone* | **Published:** 2026 Oct
**PMID:** 42288172 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.bone.2026.117979

**The study:** Using a nationwide Japanese claims database, researchers tracked adults aged 50 and older who had a first hip or vertebral fracture between 2017 and 2023 and had not previously been on osteoporosis drugs, measuring how many started treatment within three months. Initiation after hip fracture rose starting in 2022 — coinciding with a new national payment incentive for post-hip-fracture care — reaching 29.5% in 2023, while initiation after vertebral fracture rose only modestly to 35.7%. Oral bisphosphonate and romosozumab starts increased, while teriparatide starts after hip fracture declined.

**Why it matters:** A fracture after age 50 is the single strongest warning sign of the next one, yet roughly two-thirds of hip fracture patients still leave the system without starting bone-protective treatment — a gap that families and clinicians can close deliberately.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have broken a hip, wrist, or vertebra after age 50, ask directly at your follow-up appointment: 'Should I be on a medication to prevent the next fracture?' Do not assume the orthopedic surgeon who fixed the break has handed that question to anyone else.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For rehab and senior living teams: discharge after fracture repair is the key handoff point where osteoporosis treatment gets dropped. Policy incentives moved the needle in Japan, but a simple internal rule — every post-fracture resident gets a documented bone-health review — accomplishes the same thing locally.

**Caveats:** Descriptive cross-sectional study of claims data; it cannot prove the 2022 payment change caused the increase, and there was no control group. Japanese health system and prescribing patterns may not generalize to other countries. One author has a collaborative research agreement with Amgen; the study received Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare support.

### 24. Bone, Muscle and Fat Don't Decline on a Straight Line — and the Timing Differs Sharply by Sex

**Journal:** *Geroscience* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 30
**PMID:** 42811242 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11357-026-02496-1

**The study:** Researchers analyzed body composition in 9,717 adults aged 20 to 90, using bioelectrical impedance to estimate bone, skeletal muscle, fat, and fat stored inside muscle (IMAT). Men peaked earlier and stayed relatively stable through midlife before falling below women in later life; women started lower, showed sharper downturns around menopause, and had more complex fat patterns. Fat infiltrating muscle rose steadily with age in both sexes, and people meeting criteria for 'osteosarcopenic adiposity' — weak bones, low muscle, and excess fat together — showed earlier and less stable declines, with some cases appearing even in the youngest participants.

**Why it matters:** It reframes bone loss as part of a package deal with muscle loss and fat redistribution, suggesting that strength and body composition — not bone density alone — deserve attention well before older age.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Think of your bones and muscles as a single system: resistance training and adequate protein support both at once. If you are approaching or past menopause, this is the window the data flag as the steepest, and it is worth asking your clinician about muscle strength and bone density together rather than one in isolation.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living wellness programming: the finding that fat inside muscle climbs steadily with age supports exercise programs that emphasize strength and function rather than weight on the scale, since a 'normal' body weight can hide poor muscle and bone quality.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design — it compares different people at different ages rather than following individuals over time, so it cannot show how any one person changes. Body composition was estimated by a proprietary bioelectrical impedance device (BIA-ACC), not DXA, and one co-author is affiliated with the device company (BioTekna). Participants were all Caucasian adults, limiting generalizability.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**
- **The post-fracture treatment gap holds across a third health system.** The previous digest's "Broken Wrist, Missed Diagnosis" (PMID 42448012) and "Osteoporosis in men is common, dangerous — and mostly untreated" (PMID 42735505) are reinforced here by the same Austrian analysis, carried forward as "Broken Wrist, Missed Diagnosis: Nationwide Data Show Osteoporosis Goes Unrecorded After Many Fragility Fractures" (PMID 42448012), and extended by new Japanese data in "After a Hip Fracture, Fewer Than a Third of Patients Start Bone Medication — But the Number Is Rising" (PMID 42288172), where initiation reached only 29.5% in 2023.
- **Bone, muscle, and fat keep traveling as one package.** "Body Shape Changes With Age May Be a Hidden Link Between Osteoporosis and Losing Strength" (PMID 42678368) from the previous run is matched by "Bone, Muscle and Fat Don't Decline on a Straight Line" (PMID 42811242) and "Belly Fat, Not Body Weight, Tracks With Weaker Bones" (PMID 42641825).
- **Men remain the under-identified group.** Alongside the repeated "Men at High Risk of Osteoporosis Almost Never Get Bone Density Tests" (PMID 42493193), two new studies add to the picture from different directions: men had the higher silent-fracture rate in "One in six adults over 40 had a spinal fracture they didn't know about" (PMID 42624233), and men were more than twice as likely to land in the lowest knowledge tier in "Older adults scored under 30% on a basic osteoporosis quiz" (PMID 42600719).
- **Several studies carry over unchanged and simply harden prior conclusions:** the elbow implant findings (PMID 42733408), the FRAXplus reclassification analysis (PMID 42689245), the β-CTX deferral study (PMID 42398845), the MRONJ dental-timing analysis (PMID 42372806), the romosozumab adherence study (PMID 42362099), the voice-assistant evaluation (PMID 42442524), the nutrition scoping review (PMID 42476484), the VBQ/DXA mismatch study (PMID 42492717), and the post-hip-fracture X-ray study (PMID 42732263).

**Advances**
- **Romosozumab moves from a single 12-month course to a five-year question.** The previous digest's "A Powerful Bone-Building Drug Boosts Spine and Hip Scores — But Wrist and Shin Bone Didn't Follow" (PMID 42711379) looked at one course and one year; "Five years of repeat romosozumab courses" (PMID 42769300) now reports real-world density, pain and retention data over five years, with roughly three in ten patients dropping off — which dovetails with the adherence predictors in PMID 42362099.
- **From "DXA can mislead" to a practical way to find what it misses.** "Why a Spine Bone Density Scan Can Look Normal When Bone Quality Isn't" (PMID 42492717) framed the problem; "One in six adults over 40 had a spinal fracture they didn't know about" (PMID 42624233) shows a low-radiation add-on to the same scanner surfacing silent vertebral fractures in 15.9% of a primary care sample.
- **From a documented education gap to its exact location.** The previous run tested a delivery method with the smart speaker study (PMID 42442524); the new knowledge survey (PMID 42600719) pinpoints what to deliver — people scored 35/100 on calcium sources but 11.1/100 on balance exercise — and the exercise meta-analysis "162 Trials, One Message" (PMID 42575297) supplies the content that gap is missing.
- **De-implementation widens beyond imaging.** "Routine X-Rays After Hip Fracture Surgery Changed Nothing" (PMID 42732263) is now joined by "Big vitamin D doses didn't prevent broken bones in a 5-year trial" (PMID 42704968), which makes high-dose supplementation a candidate for medication review rather than routine continuation.

**Counterpoints**
- **Vitamin D takes a hit.** "Losing Weight in Later Life Costs You Bone" (PMID 42714789) named calcium, vitamin D and protein as the countermeasures; the randomized Finnish trial (PMID 42704968) found neither 1,600 nor 3,200 IU/day reduced fractures over 7.3 years in people who were already sufficient — a reminder that the nutrient matters for deficiency, not as a dose-escalation strategy.
- **Weight and bone, revisited.** The previous run's message that losing weight costs bone (PMID 42714789) is complicated by "Belly Fat, Not Body Weight, Tracks With Weaker Bones" (PMID 42641825), where higher visceral fat tracked with *lower* bone density across age and sex groups — so a higher number on the scale is not reassurance.

**New themes**
- **Medication administration as a safety issue, not just drug choice:** "When an Osteoporosis Pill Burns" (PMID 42776717) shows an oral bisphosphonate causing chemical mouth ulceration in a nursing home resident with dysphagia and dementia — an administration-route problem that the prior safety thread (PMID 42372806) did not touch.
- **Emergency care patterns in this population:** "Which older adults keep returning to the emergency department" (PMID 42762629) brings aged care residency, rural location and information-giving at the bedside into a digest otherwise focused on bone.
- **Under-served subgroups with compounded risk:** "Women with epilepsy face added bone and heart risks at menopause" (PMID 42810186) flags a group routinely excluded from osteoporosis trials.
- **Everyday function as an early signal:** "Trouble Chewing or Speaking Clearly Predicted Frailty Four Years Later" (PMID 41331404).
- **Diet pattern over single nutrients:** "The Mediterranean diet and your bones" (PMID 42641790).

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Taken together, this batch redraws the line between bone advice that holds up and bone advice that doesn't. The largest randomized evidence here says extra vitamin D bought nothing — "Big vitamin D doses didn't prevent broken bones in a 5-year trial of older adults" (PMID 42704968) — and "Can What You Eat Speed Up Fracture Healing?" (PMID 42476484) finds the supplement shelf still unproven, while "162 Trials, One Message: Exercise Builds Bone" (PMID 42575297) and "The Mediterranean diet and your bones" (PMID 42641790) point to loading the skeleton and overall eating patterns. The sting is in "Older adults scored under 30% on a basic osteoporosis quiz — and men did worst" (PMID 42600719): people scored highest on calcium sources and worst, by far, on naming a balance exercise — strong on the part that is oversold, blank on the part that matters most.

**Why pitch this now:** Four studies landing in one window line up into a single, unusually clean message about where to put effort and money, at a moment when supplement spending among people over 50 keeps climbing and "take more vitamin D for your bones" is still routine advice.

**Angle:** A practical reckoning for readers over 50 and the families who stock their medicine cabinets: what the newest evidence supports doing (progressive resistance and weight-bearing work, named balance exercises, a Mediterranean-style pattern of eating), what it no longer supports buying, and the questions that sort one from the other at a doctor's visit.

**Potential headlines:**
- What the Newest Bone Research Says to Keep Doing — and What You Can Stop Buying
- Big Vitamin D Doses Didn't Prevent Fractures. Lifting Something Heavy Showed Up on the Scan.
- Most People Over 50 Can Name a Calcium-Rich Food. Almost None Can Name a Balance Exercise.

**Potential outlets:**
- **Sixty and Me** — a direct, non-scolding "what to stop buying, what to start doing" piece for women 50+ is exactly their practical-health register, and the exercise findings give readers something concrete to act on at home.
- **Next Avenue** — service journalism for older adults and families; the vitamin D trial plus the exercise synthesis supports a well-sourced piece on spending money and effort where the evidence is.
- **Experience Life** — the 162-trial analysis is explicitly about training type, site and dose, which suits an active-aging readership that wants specifics rather than "stay active."
- **LeadingAge** — the knowledge gap on balance exercise and the site-specific exercise findings translate into a programing audit for communities whose wellness calendars lean on chair-based and aerobic classes.
