### 1. Korea's Hospital-Based Medication Review Program Cut Readmissions and Costs for Older Adults

**Journal:** *Drugs & Aging* | **Published:** August 8, 2026
**PMID:** 42570168 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40266-026-01325-6

**The study:** Researchers evaluated South Korea's national pilot polypharmacy management program, which targeted hospitalized patients aged 65 and older who were taking ten or more medications (or five or more with additional risk factors). The program, implemented across 34 hospitals, involved pharmacist-led medication reviews. Using propensity-score matching to compare 1,135 program participants with 1,125 historical controls, the study found that participants had a 15% lower risk of hospital readmission within 90 days and incurred significantly lower hospitalization costs ($6,400 vs. $6,740). The program generated a benefit-to-cost ratio of 3.8 from the healthcare payer's perspective.

**Why it matters:** This real-world evidence demonstrates that structured, pharmacist-led medication reviews for older adults with complex medication regimens can reduce hospital returns and healthcare spending simultaneously.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're taking many medications and facing a hospital stay, ask whether a pharmacist review of all your prescriptions is available before you go home—it may help prevent problems that could land you back in the hospital.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Health systems and senior living communities may consider advocating for or implementing similar pharmacy-led review programs, particularly for residents transitioning from hospital to home or facility care.

**Caveats:** Historical control group (not randomized); single-country healthcare system may limit generalizability; retrospective design.

### 2. Pharmacist-Led Deprescribing Safely Reduced Medications in Seniors with Hyperpolypharmacy

**Journal:** *Age and Ageing* | **Published:** July 2, 2026
**PMID:** 42472621 | **DOI:** 10.1093/ageing/afag209

**The study:** In this Dutch cluster-randomized trial, 318 patients aged 75 and older taking extensive medications (hyperpolypharmacy) and using multi-dose dispensing systems were assigned to either a deprescribing-focused clinical medication review by trained pharmacists or usual care. After six months, patients in the intervention group had significantly more medications reduced or stopped (average 2.5 vs. 1.7 medications). Importantly, there were no significant differences in health problems or quality of life between groups, suggesting deprescribing was safe.

**Why it matters:** This trial provides reassurance that carefully supervised medication reduction in heavily medicated older adults can be done without negative health consequences, potentially simplifying daily routines and reducing pill burden.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're taking many medications each day, consider asking your pharmacist or doctor about a medication review—some of your prescriptions may no longer be necessary, and stopping them safely could make your routine easier.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Pharmacies serving senior populations, particularly those managing complex medication packaging systems, could adopt deprescribing-focused reviews as a standard service offering.

**Caveats:** Six-month follow-up may be too short to detect long-term benefits or harms; study population used multi-dose dispensing (may not generalize to all older adults).

### 3. Diuretics, Antipsychotics, and Multiple Medications Linked to Injurious Falls in Australian Seniors

**Journal:** *Drugs & Aging* | **Published:** July 23, 2026
**PMID:** 42489786 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40266-026-01320-x

**The study:** Researchers followed 952 Australians aged 65 and older for up to 12 years, tracking emergency department visits for fall-related injuries. Taking five or more medications (polypharmacy) more than doubled the risk of injurious falls, while diuretic use nearly tripled the risk and antipsychotic use more than doubled it. Diuretics accounted for the largest share of fall injuries at the population level (23% of cases attributable to their use).

**Why it matters:** These findings highlight specific medication classes that warrant careful review in fall-prone older adults, with diuretics emerging as a particularly important target for reassessment.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you take a water pill (diuretic) or other medications that make you dizzy or affect your balance, talk with your doctor about whether adjustments might reduce your fall risk—this conversation could help keep you on your feet.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living staff and family caregivers should be particularly vigilant about fall risk when residents or loved ones are prescribed diuretics or antipsychotics, and should advocate for periodic medication reviews.

**Caveats:** Observational design (cannot prove medications caused falls); Australian population may not generalize globally.

### 4. Specific Drug Classes—Not Just Pill Counts—Predict Recurrent Falls in Well-Functioning Seniors

**Journal:** *European Geriatric Medicine* | **Published:** August 12, 2026
**PMID:** 42584792 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s41999-026-01568-x

**The study:** Danish researchers followed 241 community-dwelling adults aged 75 and older for one year using monthly fall calendars verified by phone. While 74% used at least one fall-risk-increasing drug and 71% had polypharmacy, the total number of such drugs did not predict falls. However, specific drug classes did: benzodiazepine-related drugs tripled the risk of recurrent falls (two or more), strong anticholinergics more than doubled it, and antiepileptics nearly quintupled it.

**Why it matters:** Simply counting medications may miss the real culprits—certain drug classes pose outsized fall risks and deserve targeted attention during medication reviews.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you take sleeping pills, anxiety medications, or drugs for seizures, ask your healthcare provider whether these could be contributing to fall risk and whether alternatives or dose adjustments might be safer.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Fall prevention programs in senior living should move beyond generic "reduce medications" messaging to specifically flag high-risk drug classes for clinical review.

**Caveats:** Small sample (N=241); single Danish municipality; one-year follow-up.

### 5. Fall-Risk Drugs Linked to Higher Hospitalization Rates in Italian Older Adults

**Journal:** *Drugs & Aging* | **Published:** June 24, 2026
**PMID:** 42343007 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40266-026-01312-x

**The study:** Using Italian health insurance data, researchers matched over 16,000 older adults hospitalized for fall injuries with 16,000 similar adults who were not hospitalized. Use of any fall-risk-increasing drug was associated with 51% higher odds of fall-related hospitalization. Opioids and antidepressants showed the strongest associations (40% and 45% increased risk, respectively). Longer exposure (more than nine months) and current use (within 30 days) carried the highest risks.

**Why it matters:** This large-scale study reinforces that opioids and antidepressants warrant particular scrutiny in older adults concerned about falls, especially with prolonged use.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you've been taking pain medications or antidepressants for many months, this may be a good time to discuss with your doctor whether the benefits still outweigh the risks, including fall risk.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Healthcare providers and senior living pharmacists should consider duration of exposure when assessing fall risk—longer use appears to compound danger.

**Caveats:** Observational design using claims data; cannot establish causation; Italian healthcare system context.

### 6. Hip Strength and Diuretic Use Are Key Predictors of Injurious Falls

**Journal:** *Archives of Gerontology and Geriatrics* | **Published:** June 8, 2026
**PMID:** 42296796 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.archger.2026.106333

**The study:** Analyzing data from 952 Australian adults aged 65 and older, researchers built prediction models to determine which modifiable factors best identified people at risk for injurious falls. Hip abductor (outer hip) muscle strength and diuretic use contributed most strongly to prediction accuracy, followed by smoking status. Polypharmacy and self-reported fear of falling added little predictive value beyond these factors.

**Why it matters:** Strengthening hip muscles and reviewing diuretic prescriptions emerge as high-priority, actionable targets for fall prevention—more important than simply reducing total medication counts.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Exercises that strengthen your hip muscles—like side leg raises or resistance band work—may be particularly valuable for preventing falls. Ask your physical therapist or doctor about adding these to your routine.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living exercise programs and physical therapy protocols should emphasize hip strengthening exercises, and medication reviews should specifically scrutinize diuretic necessity.

**Caveats:** Observational design; Australian cohort; prediction models require external validation.

### 7. Umbrella Review: Focused Deprescribing Works Best for Sleep and Anxiety Medications

**Journal:** *Age and Ageing* | **Published:** August 3, 2026
**PMID:** 42607278 | **DOI:** 10.1093/ageing/afag243

**The study:** Researchers synthesized evidence from 27 systematic reviews examining "focused deprescribing"—the targeted withdrawal of specific drug classes rather than comprehensive medication review. The strongest evidence supported reducing benzodiazepines (sleep and anxiety drugs) and other psychotropics, especially when combined with psychotherapy or multiple intervention components. Evidence for safely reducing diabetes medications was also promising. However, clinical outcomes like falls, mortality, and cognition rarely improved.

**Why it matters:** For healthcare systems with limited resources, targeting benzodiazepines and similar drugs for deprescribing may offer the best return on investment, though expectations for dramatic health improvements should remain modest.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you've been taking sleep or anxiety medication for a long time, evidence suggests it's often possible to reduce or stop these drugs safely—especially with support from counseling or other non-drug approaches. Discuss options with your doctor.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living communities and primary care practices may achieve better results by focusing deprescribing efforts on psychotropics rather than attempting comprehensive medication overhauls.

**Caveats:** Most included reviews were critically low quality; evidence certainty was generally low; clinical benefits beyond reducing pill counts remain uncertain.

### 8. Blood Pressure Medications Called ARBs Linked to Lower Muscle Loss Risk in Hospitalized Seniors

**Journal:** *Aging Clinical and Experimental Research* | **Published:** July 8, 2026
**PMID:** 42414787 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40520-026-03452-x

**The study:** Turkish researchers assessed 206 hospitalized patients aged 65 and older for sarcopenia (age-related muscle loss) and medication use. While overall polypharmacy rates were similar between those with and without sarcopenia, patients taking angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs)—a type of blood pressure medication—were significantly less likely to have sarcopenia. This association remained significant after accounting for other factors, suggesting a potential muscle-protective effect of ARBs.

**Why it matters:** While more research is needed, this finding raises the possibility that certain blood pressure medications may offer benefits beyond heart health—potentially helping preserve muscle mass in older adults.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you take blood pressure medication, this research is preliminary but intriguing—some types may have additional benefits for muscle health. Don't change your medications based on this alone, but it's worth monitoring future research.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Clinicians selecting blood pressure medications for older patients might consider ARBs when muscle preservation is a priority, though prospective trials are needed to confirm any protective effect.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design (cannot establish causation); single-center study in Turkey; modest sample size (N=206).

### 9. Underweight Stroke Patients on Multiple Medications Show Poorer Recovery

**Journal:** *Drugs & Aging* | **Published:** August 4, 2026
**PMID:** 42550458 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40266-026-01326-5

**The study:** Japanese researchers analyzed 1,245 stroke patients in rehabilitation, examining how body weight category affected the relationship between polypharmacy and functional recovery. Among underweight patients (BMI under 18.5), those taking five or more medications had significantly lower daily functioning scores at discharge compared to underweight patients on fewer medications. This pattern was not seen in normal-weight or obese patients.

**Why it matters:** Underweight older adults recovering from stroke may be particularly vulnerable to the negative effects of multiple medications, suggesting this group deserves extra attention during medication reviews.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or a loved one is underweight and recovering from a stroke, be especially proactive about discussing medications with the healthcare team—reducing unnecessary prescriptions may support better recovery.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Rehabilitation facilities should consider nutritional status when prioritizing patients for medication review, with underweight individuals potentially benefiting most from deprescribing efforts.

**Caveats:** Retrospective single-center study; relatively modest effect size; Japanese population may not generalize globally.

### 10. High Anticholinergic Drug Burden Doubles Chemotherapy Toxicity Risk in Older Cancer Patients

**Journal:** *Drugs & Aging* | **Published:** July 22, 2026
**PMID:** 42487095 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40266-026-01316-7

**The study:** Dutch researchers studied 434 cancer patients aged 70 and older starting chemotherapy or targeted therapy. Those with high anticholinergic burden scores (ACB ≥3, indicating significant cumulative anticholinergic effects from their medications) experienced severe treatment toxicity twice as often as those with lower scores. This association persisted even after accounting for frailty status.

**Why it matters:** For older adults facing cancer treatment, the medications they're already taking may significantly affect how well they tolerate chemotherapy—making pre-treatment medication review especially important.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Before starting cancer treatment, ask your oncologist and pharmacist to review all your current medications for anticholinergic effects—reducing these may help you tolerate treatment better.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Oncology teams treating older patients should routinely calculate anticholinergic burden scores and consider deprescribing high-burden medications before chemotherapy begins.

**Caveats:** Single-center observational study; anticholinergic burden scores can vary by calculation method.

### 11. Older Heart Failure Patients Receive Less Guideline-Recommended Therapy Than Younger Patients

**Journal:** *European Geriatric Medicine* | **Published:** July 14, 2026
**PMID:** 42446811 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s41999-026-01553-4

**The study:** German researchers analyzed medication patterns among 3,289 heart failure patients, comparing those aged 70 and older with younger adults. Despite having more advanced heart failure, older patients were significantly less likely to receive aldosterone antagonists (a key guideline-recommended medication) and beta-blockers. This gap persisted even after accounting for kidney function. Conversely, older patients were more likely to receive calcium channel blockers, which are not first-line heart failure treatments.

**Why it matters:** Older adults with heart failure may be systematically undertreated with medications proven to extend life, potentially due to concerns about side effects that could be managed with careful monitoring.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have heart failure, ask your doctor whether you're receiving all the recommended medications—some treatments proven to help may not be prescribed simply because of age, even when they could benefit you.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Cardiologists and geriatricians should scrutinize prescribing patterns for heart failure patients over 70, ensuring that age alone doesn't lead to withholding evidence-based therapies.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design; single German cohort; cannot determine whether undertreatment affected outcomes.

### 12. Chronic Opioid-Benzodiazepine Combination Nearly Triples Frailty Risk in Older Americans

**Journal:** *Experimental Gerontology* | **Published:** June 12, 2026
**PMID:** 42285234 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.exger.2026.113201

**The study:** Analyzing nationally representative data from over 18,000 Americans aged 50 and older across seven NHANES survey cycles, researchers found that chronic co-prescription of opioids and benzodiazepines (both for 90+ days) was associated with 2.88 times higher odds of frailty compared to using neither drug. Adding a gabapentinoid (creating "triple therapy") raised the odds to 3.71 times higher. These associations held across all demographic and health subgroups examined.

**Why it matters:** The combination of pain medications and anxiety/sleep drugs—already carrying FDA warnings—appears strongly linked to the broader syndrome of frailty, suggesting these prescriptions may accelerate aging-related decline.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're taking both a pain medication (opioid) and a sleep or anxiety medication (benzodiazepine) long-term, this research suggests the combination may increase your risk of becoming frail. Discuss alternatives with your doctor.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Prescribers should view chronic opioid-benzodiazepine co-prescription as a potential driver of frailty, not just respiratory risk, and prioritize deprescribing discussions with affected patients.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design (cannot prove causation); self-reported/prescription data; frailty measured by questionnaire-based index.

### 13. Statins Appear Safe for Cognition in Older Adults, but Definitive Trials Are Underway

**Journal:** *GeroScience* | **Published:** April 20, 2026
**PMID:** 42010230 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11357-026-02259-y

**The study:** This comprehensive narrative review synthesized evidence from randomized trials, observational studies, and genetic analyses examining whether statins affect cognition in older adults. Most randomized trials found no harmful effect on memory or thinking, and some observational studies suggested potential protective effects. Genetic studies analyzing over 100,000 individuals found no causal link between statin-related genetic variants and dementia. The review notes that two large trials specifically designed to answer this question (PREVENTABLE and STAREE) are currently underway.

**Why it matters:** Older adults taking statins for heart health can be reassured that current evidence does not support stopping these medications due to cognitive concerns, though individual factors like frailty should still guide decisions.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you've worried about statins affecting your memory, current research suggests this concern is unfounded for most people. Definitive answers from ongoing major studies should arrive within the next few years.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Healthcare providers should not discontinue statins in older patients solely based on cognitive concerns, but should continue individualizing decisions based on cardiovascular benefit, frailty status, and patient preferences.

**Caveats:** Narrative review (not systematic); older adults remain underrepresented in trials with cognitive outcomes; awaiting results from dedicated trials.

### 14. Pharmacist Medication Reviews Identified Hundreds of Drug Problems in Older Cancer Patients

**Journal:** *Journal of Geriatric Oncology* | **Published:** June 26, 2026
**PMID:** 42364399 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jgo.2026.103036

**The study:** At Princess Margaret Cancer Centre in Toronto, pharmacists conducted comprehensive medication reviews for 205 older adults (average age 81) referred to a geriatric oncology clinic over one year. Patients were taking an average of 11.5 medications. Pharmacists identified 300 drug therapy problems, with adverse drug reactions being the most common. Geriatricians accepted or acknowledged 65% of pharmacist recommendations. Each review took approximately 103 minutes.

**Why it matters:** Integrating pharmacists into cancer care for older adults uncovers substantial medication issues that might otherwise go unaddressed, supporting safer treatment planning.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're an older adult being treated for cancer, consider requesting a pharmacist review of all your medications—including non-cancer drugs—before starting treatment. Many potential problems can be identified and addressed.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Cancer centers serving older populations should consider embedding clinical pharmacists in geriatric oncology programs, with adequate time allocated for thorough medication review.

**Caveats:** Single-center quality improvement project; no comparison group; labor-intensive intervention (over 1.5 hours per patient).

### 15. Digital Health App Plus Pharmacist Telehealth Improved Medication Habits in Older African Americans

**Journal:** *Senior Care Pharmacist* | **Published:** June 1, 2026
**PMID:** 42288945 | **DOI:** 10.4140/TCP.n.2026.139

**The study:** In this six-month pilot study, 40 African American adults aged 65 and older in Houston who were taking five or more medications used a smartphone medication reminder app combined with two pharmacist-led telehealth sessions. The proportion reporting they forgot medications dropped from 74% to 44%. Use of health technology increased from 14% to 68%, and participation in virtual medication management rose from 15% to 85%. Self-reported medication knowledge also improved significantly.

**Why it matters:** This study suggests that combining simple technology tools with pharmacist support can meaningfully improve medication management in a population often underrepresented in digital health research.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Even if you've never used health apps before, a simple pill reminder on your phone combined with pharmacist support may help you stay on track with your medications—and the technology may be easier to learn than you expect.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Community pharmacies and health systems serving diverse older populations should consider pairing digital tools with human support, rather than relying on technology alone.

**Caveats:** Small pilot study (N=40); no control group; predominantly female sample; self-reported outcomes.

### 16. Blood-Based Alzheimer's Tests Show Promise but Require Careful Interpretation in Older Adults

**Journal:** *Age and Ageing* | **Published:** July 2, 2026
**PMID:** 42476554 | **DOI:** 10.1093/ageing/afag213

**The study:** This clinical commentary reviews the rapidly evolving field of blood-based biomarkers for Alzheimer's disease, particularly plasma p-tau217, which has shown strong accuracy for detecting Alzheimer's pathology across multiple testing platforms. The authors note that while factors common in older adults—kidney disease, polypharmacy, and frailty—may affect absolute biomarker levels, diagnostic performance appears preserved when results are interpreted appropriately within a comprehensive geriatric assessment.

**Why it matters:** Blood tests for Alzheimer's disease are becoming clinically available, but older adults and their families should understand these are currently intended for symptomatic patients seen by specialists, not for screening healthy individuals.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're experiencing memory concerns, new blood tests may help your specialist determine whether Alzheimer's disease is the cause. However, these tests are not yet recommended for people without symptoms and should be interpreted alongside a thorough evaluation.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Geriatricians and primary care providers should prepare to counsel patients and families about blood-based Alzheimer's biomarkers, emphasizing that results must be interpreted within the context of a complete geriatric assessment.

**Caveats:** Commentary/review (not original research); clinical guidelines still evolving; tests not recommended for asymptomatic screening.

### 17. Digital Twin Technology Shows Promise for Personalized Elder Care, but Barriers Remain

**Journal:** *Archives of Gerontology and Geriatrics* | **Published:** August 3, 2026
**PMID:** 42570533 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.archger.2026.106371

**The study:** This narrative review examined applications of "digital twin" technology—virtual replicas of individuals continuously updated with real-time health data—in geriatric care. The review identified promising applications in cardiovascular monitoring, fall prevention, dementia management, and polypharmacy optimization. However, significant barriers remain, including data privacy concerns, high computational costs, interoperability challenges, and ethical questions about consent in cognitively impaired populations.

**Why it matters:** While still largely experimental, digital twin technology could eventually enable highly personalized health monitoring and medication management for older adults, though significant development and regulatory work remains.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** In the coming years, technology may create a "digital version" of your health that helps doctors predict problems before they occur and personalize your care. This is still developing, but it's worth staying informed.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living technology planners should monitor digital twin developments while advocating for privacy protections and equitable access as these tools mature.

**Caveats:** Narrative review of emerging technology; most applications are experimental; implementation barriers are substantial.

### 18. Geriatric Assessment Reveals Hidden Health Problems in Cancer Patients of All Ages

**Journal:** *Journal of Geriatric Oncology* | **Published:** August 4, 2026
**PMID:** 42551215 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jgo.2026.103074

**The study:** Researchers at the University of North Carolina assessed 97 lymphoma patients (average age 57, range 22-84) starting treatment using a modified geriatric assessment tool. Despite 94% having normal performance status by traditional measures, 56% had deficits in two or more geriatric domains. Even among younger patients (under 65), substantial proportions had unintentional weight loss (34%), falls (27%), difficulty with daily activities (27%), or anxiety/depression (45%).

**Why it matters:** Standard performance assessments miss important vulnerabilities in cancer patients. Age-based cutoffs for geriatric assessment may be too restrictive—many younger patients could benefit from this comprehensive evaluation.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have cancer, a geriatric assessment—regardless of your age—may identify issues like nutrition problems, fall risk, or mood concerns that could affect your treatment. Ask whether this evaluation is available.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Oncology programs should consider offering geriatric assessment based on patient needs and complexity rather than age thresholds alone.

**Caveats:** Single-center observational study; modest sample size; lymphoma-specific population.

### 19. Polypharmacy and Poor Nutrition Weigh Heavily on Rural Thai Seniors' Health Burden

**Journal:** *Aging Clinical and Experimental Research* | **Published:** June 23, 2026
**PMID:** 42334761 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40520-026-03435-y

**The study:** This large population-based study of nearly 588,000 Thai adults aged 60 and older from rural areas calculated disease burden using disability-adjusted life years (DALYs). Polypharmacy affected 60% of the population and contributed over 4 million DALYs, with premature death accounting for 54% of this burden. Potentially inappropriate medications were present in 32% of patients. Diabetes caused the highest overall disease burden despite lower prevalence than hypertension.

**Why it matters:** In aging rural populations, polypharmacy and inappropriate prescribing represent substantial—and potentially modifiable—sources of health burden that warrant systematic intervention.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Living in a rural area doesn't mean accepting complicated medication regimens as inevitable. If you're taking many medications, a review with your healthcare provider could identify ones that may no longer be helping or could be causing harm.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Rural health systems should prioritize medication optimization programs, particularly targeting inappropriate prescribing patterns that disproportionately affect these underserved populations.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design; Thai healthcare system may not generalize; hospital-based sample may not represent all rural older adults.

### 20. Community Pharmacy Frailty Screening Tool Validated Using Belgian Health Survey Data

**Journal:** *Drugs & Aging* | **Published:** June 15, 2026
**PMID:** 42295695 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40266-026-01307-8

**The study:** Belgian researchers validated a frailty screening tool designed for community pharmacies using routinely available data (age, sex, number of chronic medications, and specific medication use) against frailty assessments from 2,191 older adults in a national health survey. The tool showed good discrimination (AUC 0.77) with 64% sensitivity and 77% specificity. Among patients already identified as having polypharmacy, sensitivity improved to 77%.

**Why it matters:** Community pharmacists could use this validated screening tool to identify older adults who might benefit from more comprehensive frailty assessment and targeted interventions, without requiring additional patient testing.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Your local pharmacist may soon be able to use your prescription records to identify whether you might be at risk for frailty-related problems—a screening that could connect you with helpful resources.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Community pharmacies serving older populations can implement this validated screening approach using existing dispensing data to identify high-risk patients for outreach and intervention.

**Caveats:** External validation study; Belgian healthcare context; screening tool flags risk rather than diagnosing frailty.

- Korea's national polypharmacy program demonstrates real-world cost-effectiveness: The NEW digest adds a propensity-matched evaluation (PMID 42570168) showing 15% lower 90-day readmission rates and a 3.8 benefit-to-cost ratio for hospital-based pharmacist medication reviews—moving beyond single-center pilots to health-system-level evidence.

- Specific drug classes, not pill counts, predict recurrent falls: The Danish study (PMID 42584792) in the NEW digest finds benzodiazepines tripled and antiepileptics nearly quintupled recurrent fall risk, while total fall-risk-increasing drug counts did not predict falls—refining the PREVIOUS digest's broader "FRIDs increase risk" message.
- Umbrella review identifies focused deprescribing targets: The NEW digest synthesizes 27 systematic reviews (PMID 42607278) concluding that benzodiazepine and psychotropic deprescribing yields the strongest evidence, offering more actionable guidance than the general deprescribing safety data in the PREVIOUS digest.
- High anticholinergic burden doubles chemotherapy toxicity in older cancer patients: The Dutch study (PMID 42487095) in the NEW digest extends oncology polypharmacy concerns beyond drug interactions to measurable treatment tolerance outcomes.

**Counterpoints**
- Hip strength emerges as the dominant modifiable fall predictor: The Australian prediction model study (PMID 42296796) in the NEW digest found hip abductor strength and diuretic use contributed most to fall risk, while polypharmacy and fear of falling added little predictive value—partially counterbalancing the emphasis on total medication counts in the PREVIOUS digest's global meta-analysis (PMID 42412515).

**New themes**
- Diuretics identified as high-population-attributable-risk fall culprit: Two Australian studies (PMIDs 42489786, 42296796) in the NEW digest converge on diuretics as responsible for 23% of fall injuries at the population level and as a top prediction model variable—a specific, actionable target not highlighted in the PREVIOUS digest.
- Underweight stroke patients on polypharmacy show poorer rehabilitation outcomes: The Japanese study (PMID 42550458) introduces nutritional status as a modifier of polypharmacy harm, suggesting targeted deprescribing for underweight patients.
- Older heart failure patients systematically undertreated with guideline therapies: The German study (PMID 42446811) documents that patients 70+ receive fewer aldosterone antagonists and beta-blockers despite more advanced disease—an undertreatment concern distinct from the overtreatment focus of most polypharmacy research.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Four studies in this batch—the Australian cohort (PMID 42489786), the Australian prediction model (PMID 42296796), the Danish fall calendar study (PMID 42584792), and the Italian hospitalization analysis (PMID 42343007, also in prior digest)—collectively reframe fall prevention away from generic "reduce your medications" messaging toward identifying a short list of high-yield drug classes: diuretics, benzodiazepines, antiepileptics, opioids, and antidepressants. Diuretics alone account for nearly a quarter of fall-related injuries at the population level, yet they rarely feature in consumer-facing fall prevention advice.

**Why pitch this now:** Falls remain the leading cause of injury death among older adults, and "take fewer pills" has become a well-worn but imprecise recommendation. These studies offer specific, evidence-ranked targets that clinicians, pharmacists, and families can act on immediately—making the research directly translatable to practice.
