### Study 2: Hard childhoods leave a mark on aging bodies: adults with early adversity are twice as likely to become frail
**PMID:** 42633655 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Causal framing in headline** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Hard childhoods leave a mark on aging bodies: adults with early adversity are twice as likely to become frail"
- **Abstract says:** "ACEs are associated with increased risks of frailty and adverse cognitive outcomes in later life... 26 observational studies met the inclusion criteria."
- **Problem:** 'Leave a mark' and 'become frail' imply a causal, incident effect, while the pooled evidence is from observational studies reporting associations (and the body text says ACEs 'predict' outcomes). The digest's own caveat acknowledges causation cannot be established.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reframe as association, e.g. 'Adults who report early adversity have about double the odds of frailty'"

**Issue B. Odds reported as likelihood** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "People reporting adverse childhood experiences had roughly double the odds of frailty (OR 2.05)"
- **Abstract says:** "ACEs were associated with higher odds of frailty (OR = 2.05, 95% CI 1.61-2.63)"
- **Problem:** The body text is accurate ('odds'), but the headline's 'twice as likely' converts an odds ratio into a risk statement, which overstates the effect when outcomes are common.
- **Suggested fix:** "Keep 'odds' language in the headline as well as the body"

### Study 3: More schooling blunts the effect of Alzheimer's plaques on thinking — but does nothing for other brain pathologies
**PMID:** 42868033 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Non-significant subgroup presented as part of a significant finding** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Higher education significantly softened the relationship between amyloid burden and cognition, particularly for global cognitive measures and in samples mixing cognitively healthy and impaired participants."
- **Abstract says:** "Higher education significantly attenuated the amyloid-cognition association (r = 0.114, adjusted-p = 0.012), a trend concentrated in the global cognitive domain (r = 0.108, adjusted-p = 0.060) and in studies with mixed cognitively unimpaired and cognitively impaired participants (r = 0.130, adjusted-p = 0.020)."
- **Problem:** The global cognitive domain result did not reach significance after FDR correction (adjusted-p = 0.060); the abstract calls it a trend. Grouping it with the significant mixed-diagnosis result implies both were statistically significant.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say the effect was significant in mixed-diagnosis samples and showed a non-significant trend for global cognition"

**Issue B. Headline absolutism** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "but does nothing for other brain pathologies"
- **Abstract says:** "No significant interaction was found for pTau, vascular, or structural pathology (all adjusted-p > 0.100)."
- **Problem:** Absence of a significant interaction in 17 cross-sectional studies is not evidence that education 'does nothing'; the body text's 'no such buffering effect' is better hedged than the headline.
- **Suggested fix:** "'— but showed no detectable buffering against other brain pathologies'"

### Study 7: "I can't do that test" is itself a warning sign, two national studies find
**PMID:** 42868983 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Scope of ELSA replication overstated** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Those 11.5% of Chinese participants (and a comparable English group) went on to have higher rates of new disability in daily activities and higher odds of death, even after adjusting for baseline health and function."
- **Abstract says:** "The study included 6267 participants from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), with functional outcome replication in 6175 participants from the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA)... Functional outcome findings in the English cohort were directionally consistent."
- **Problem:** Mortality was analyzed only in CHARLS; ELSA contributed functional outcome replication, described as 'directionally consistent' rather than as a confirmed mortality association. The sentence attributes both disability and death findings to both cohorts.
- **Suggested fix:** "Note that the disability findings were directionally replicated in the English cohort and that the mortality association comes from the Chinese cohort only"

### Study 9: An autopsy offers the first direct look at what amyloid drugs actually leave behind in the brain
**PMID:** 42437499 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. 'First' claim** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "An autopsy offers the first direct look at what amyloid drugs actually leave behind in the brain"
- **Abstract says:** "The long-term efficacy of amyloid-targeting therapies hinges on their ability to slow downstream neuropathologic change, but little is known about the influence of amyloid clearance on tau pathology and neurodegeneration."
- **Problem:** The abstract says little is known but does not claim this is the first such clinicopathologic evaluation; priority claims are unverifiable from the abstract alone. The body's hedged 'among the first' is safer.
- **Suggested fix:** "Soften the headline to 'a rare direct look' or 'one of the first direct looks'"

**Issue B. Conflict-of-interest attribution** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "several authors report consulting relationships with Eli Lilly, Eisai and Biogen, and institutional research support from Biogen, the maker of aducanumab"
- **Abstract says:** "Ilya Nasrallah has served on the scientific advisory board for Eisai and done educational speaking for Biogen. David Wolk has served as a paid consultant for Eli Lilly, Eisai, and Beckman Coulter... He has received research support paid to his institution by Biogen. Edward Lee has served as a paid consultant for Wavebreak Therapeutics and Eli Lilly."
- **Problem:** The Biogen ties were educational speaking and institutional research support, not consulting; Beckman Coulter and Wavebreak are omitted. The gist is right but the specific relationships are mischaracterized.
- **Suggested fix:** "'several authors report paid consulting for Eli Lilly, Eisai and others, educational speaking for Biogen, and institutional research support from Biogen, the maker of aducanumab'"

### Study 10: A small slip of the hand during memory testing may signal deeper brain changes in Alzheimer's

### Study 13: Nursing homes are quietly turning away dialysis patients — a COVID-era habit that never ended
**PMID:** 42868932 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline overstates reported practice** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Nursing homes are quietly turning away dialysis patients — a COVID-era habit that never ended"
- **Abstract says:** "many NHs have reduced admissions of dialysis-dependent residents, threatening access for this vulnerable population"

### Study 14: Dementia residents eat more when staff talk with them, not at them — videotaped meals show the pattern
**PMID:** 42868877 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Eat more" vs. pace of intake** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Dementia residents eat more when staff talk with them, not at them"
- **Abstract says:** "Increased pace of staff-initiated food intake was correlated with preceding staff mealtime person-oriented verbal approaches."
- **Problem:** The outcome coded was the pace (rate) of food intake during the observed segments, not total amount eaten; the abstract notes videos did not capture full meals.
- **Suggested fix:** ""Dementia residents take bites faster when staff talk with them, not at them.""

**Issue B. Coding granularity** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "coded 160 videos of mealtime interactions ... second by second"
- **Abstract says:** "Videos were coded using the refined Cue Utilization and Engagement in Dementia Mealtime Video-Coding Scheme ... timed-event window-based sequential approach"
- **Problem:** The abstract describes a timed-event window-based coding scheme; "second by second" is a precision claim not verifiable from the abstract.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say "coded moment by moment using a timed-event sequential scheme.""

### Study 15: Medicare home health patients with dementia come in through a different door — and it affects what gets paid for
**PMID:** 42868934 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

**Issue A. Institutional admission finding misstated** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Those with dementia were far more likely to be admitted from the community rather than after a hospital or facility stay"
- **Abstract says:** "ADRD-highly likely beneficiaries had more community admissions (mean, 2.7 vs 2.4; IRR, 2.75) ... ADRD status was not associated with the likelihood of any institutional admission (odds ratio, 1.00; 95% CI, 0.98-1.01). Among beneficiaries with at least 1 institutional admission, ADRD-highly likely beneficiaries had higher institutional admission rates (IRR, 1.12)"
- **Problem:** The abstract reports no difference in the likelihood of institutional admission and, among those with any, higher institutional admission rates in the dementia group — so the contrast is not that dementia patients avoid the post-acute route. The mean community admission counts also differ only modestly (2.7 vs 2.4).
- **Suggested fix:** "State that dementia beneficiaries had more community admissions and more late episodes, while the likelihood of institutional (post-acute) admission did not differ between groups."

**Issue B. Claim that PDGM pays less for these pathways** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the year just before Medicare's Patient-Driven Groupings Model began paying less for exactly those community-origin and late episodes ... the payment rules now favor the admission pathway dementia patients use least"
- **Abstract says:** "the Patient-Driven Groupings Model (PDGM) implementation, which incorporated these factors into HH payment"
- **Problem:** The abstract says only that admission source and episode timing were incorporated into payment; it does not state the direction or size of payment differences. The second clause also rests on the misstated institutional finding.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say PDGM began basing payment on admission source and episode timing, and flag the payment direction as not addressed in the abstract ("Unverifiable from abstract only")."

### Study 16: Stiffer arteries, not a faulty reflex, may explain why frail older adults get dizzy standing up
**PMID:** 42867998 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Predicted" for a cross-sectional association** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Lower compliance predicted bigger swings in blood pressure on standing"
- **Abstract says:** "lower TAC was associated with greater systolic BPV during sitting-to-standing (SBPV-VLF: β = -0.46, p = 0.006) and lying-to-standing"
- **Problem:** These are concurrent regression associations in a cross-sectional lab study; "predicted" implies temporal/prospective prediction.
- **Suggested fix:** ""Lower compliance was associated with bigger swings in blood pressure on standing.""

**Issue B. Single-laboratory claim** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Small sample (N=50), single laboratory, cross-sectional and observational"
- **Abstract says:** "Community-dwelling older adults aged 60-79 years were classified as non-frail (n=28) or frail (n=22) using the Frailty Index."
- **Problem:** The abstract does not state that testing occurred at a single laboratory site.
- **Suggested fix:** "Drop "single laboratory" or mark as unverifiable from the abstract."

### Study 17: Where your stroke journey starts may decide whether you get clot-removing treatment
**PMID:** 42743443 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Overwhelmingly" overstates facility proportions** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "overwhelmingly arrived first at rural hospitals, hospitals with fewer than 100 beds, or critical access hospitals"
- **Abstract says:** "Their presenting facility was more likely to be rural (55.3% vs 22.6%), have <100 beds (56.6% vs 11.9%), or be a critical access hospital (24.9% vs 2.8%)"
- **Problem:** Roughly 55% presented at rural facilities and only 25% at critical access hospitals; "overwhelmingly" implies a far larger share than reported.
- **Suggested fix:** ""were much more often first seen at rural hospitals, hospitals with fewer than 100 beds, or critical access hospitals (55.3%, 56.6% and 24.9% respectively).""

### Study 18: Worrying about climate change and being anxious about it aren't the same thing — and older adults' well-being tracks the difference
**PMID:** 42864713 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Education finding broadened** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Lower education and tighter finances were associated with poorer well-being."
- **Abstract says:** "Higher education was associated with higher life satisfaction, and poorer economic circumstances with lower quality of life and life satisfaction."
- **Problem:** Education was linked only to life satisfaction in the abstract, not to quality of life; the digest's "well-being" implies both outcomes.
- **Suggested fix:** ""Lower education was associated with lower life satisfaction, and tighter finances with both lower quality of life and lower life satisfaction.""

### Study 19: Insomnia leaves a different fingerprint on women's sleeping brains than men's
**PMID:** 42454951 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Treatment recommendation not from this study** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "it's worth asking your clinician about cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia rather than settling for sleeping pills"
- **Abstract says:** "Spindle density, slow oscillation (SO) density, relative sigma power and SWA were derived during NREM sleep ... No treatment arm is described."
- **Problem:** This mechanistic polysomnography study tested no interventions; the digest's own caveat states "No treatment implications were tested," so the angle outruns the evidence presented.
- **Suggested fix:** "Attribute the CBT-I suggestion to existing clinical guidance rather than to this study, e.g., "existing guidelines recommend cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia as first-line care.""
