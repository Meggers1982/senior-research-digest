### 1. Most Fractures in Men Over 85 Happen Indoors, During Ordinary Activity — and Often Without the Walker

**Journal:** *Osteoporos Int* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 19
**PMID:** 42897253 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s00198-026-08265-2

**The study:** Researchers in the Osteoporotic Fractures in Men (MrOS) Study followed 1,469 surviving men averaging 90 years old for about three years, contacting them every four months and confirming fractures by radiology report. Of 169 confirmed fractures, 160 (nearly 95%) were fall-related; 88% came from falls at or below standing height, 71% happened indoors, and 83% occurred while the man was doing normal or less-than-normal activity. Among men who said they regularly used a walking aid, 42% were not using it when the fracture occurred.

**Why it matters:** The image of a fracture as the result of a dramatic accident is wrong for the oldest men — these breaks happen at home, during everyday movement, suggesting prevention efforts should target the living room and hallway rather than outdoor hazards.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you use a cane or walker, the data suggest the moments you skip it — a quick trip to the bathroom, crossing your own kitchen — are exactly when fractures tend to happen; it's worth thinking through how to have your aid within reach around the house, and asking your clinician for a home safety review.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and senior living staff: this argues for auditing indoor environments and routine indoor transfers, and for making walking aids genuinely convenient (multiple aids, strategic placement) rather than assuming a prescribed device is one that gets used.

**Caveats:** Observational cohort — describes circumstances but cannot establish cause; participants were surviving men from a long-running study who were active enough to be interviewed, so findings may not generalize to women, frailer men, or nursing home residents; fall circumstances were self-reported.

### 2. Turn Your Head While Walking: A New Test Reveals Who's Losing Their Balance

**Journal:** *Geroscience* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 19
**PMID:** 42895590 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11357-026-02602-3

**The study:** Belgian researchers asked 61 healthy, community-dwelling older adults (average age 76, 40 women), some with a history of falls, to walk while turning their heads up, down, left and right on cue from an augmented-reality headset, with motion capture measuring stability. They separately tested neck sensorimotor control, inner-ear (vestibular) function and visual acuity. Adults with poorer neck and vestibular function walked more cautiously during head turns — wider steps, slower steps — while poorer visual acuity was linked to genuinely reduced walking stability, and these sensory links were strongest in people who had already fallen.

**Why it matters:** It points to eyes, inner ear and neck function as under-examined contributors to fall risk, and suggests that everyday head movements — looking for a street sign, checking over your shoulder — are a meaningful real-world balance challenge.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you feel unsteady when you turn your head while walking, that's worth mentioning at your next appointment — and it's a reminder to keep your eye exams current and ask whether dizziness or neck stiffness could be contributing to your balance.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For care professionals: standard balance tests are done with the head still. Observing residents during head-turning tasks may surface instability that routine screening misses, particularly among those with a prior fall.

**Caveats:** Small sample (N=61), single center, cross-sectional and observational — cannot show that treating sensory deficits reduces falls; participants were healthy community dwellers and may not represent frailer older adults.

### 3. In Knee Arthritis, Hearing, Vision and Mood Predicted First Falls Better Than the Knee Itself

**Journal:** *Arch Gerontol Geriatr* | **Published:** 2026 Dec
**PMID:** 42759165 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.archger.2026.106410

**The study:** Chinese researchers analyzed 28 demographic, health and behavioral variables from 1,097 people with knee osteoarthritis in the CHARLS national aging cohort, testing ten machine learning algorithms to predict a first fall and validating the best model in a separate hospital cohort. The final model showed good discrimination (AUC 0.836) and held up in external validation. The most influential predictors were not joint-specific measures but hearing and vision problems and depressive symptoms.

**Why it matters:** It reframes fall prevention for the millions of older adults with knee arthritis: the knee may not be the main driver of the first fall, and treatable sensory and mood problems deserve attention alongside the joint.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have knee arthritis, don't assume falls are purely a knee problem — ask whether your hearing, vision and mood have been checked recently, since those were the strongest signals in this analysis.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and clinicians: a knee arthritis diagnosis is a natural prompt to arrange hearing and vision testing and screen for depression, rather than routing the person only to orthopedics or physical therapy.

**Caveats:** Retrospective analysis of observational cohort data — predictive, not causal; model has not been tested prospectively or shown to reduce falls; both cohorts were Chinese, so generalizability to other populations is unproven; falls and symptoms largely self-reported.

### 4. A One-Page Checklist Transformed What Hospitals Record After a Patient Falls

**Journal:** *Aging Med (Milton)* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 14
**PMID:** 42877601 | **DOI:** 10.1002/agm2.70113

**The study:** Australian clinicians reviewed 167 first falls among hospital inpatients over age 65 across three years, comparing cases assessed with a structured Falls Proforma (125 cases) against those without. Documentation was dramatically more complete with the proforma across every clinical domain — for example, mobility assessment was recorded in 92% of proforma cases versus 7% without, cognition in 95% versus 19%, and bleeding-risk (coagulopathy) review in 98% versus 17%. Patients with preexisting cognitive impairment were somewhat less likely to have the proforma used, though the difference was not statistically significant after adjustment.

**Why it matters:** After a fall in the hospital, missed checks — for a head injury in someone on blood thinners, or a hip fracture — can be life-threatening, and this shows a simple structured form closes most of those gaps.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or a loved one falls while in the hospital, you're entitled to a thorough assessment: ask whether a structured post-fall checklist was used and whether head, neck, hip, neurological and blood-thinner checks were all documented.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living and hospital leaders: adopting a standardized post-fall proforma is a low-cost intervention with large documentation gains — but watch for the signal that patients with dementia were less likely to receive it, the very group at highest risk.

**Caveats:** Retrospective, single-center, observational — measures documentation quality, not patient outcomes; modest sample (167 falls); the proforma group may differ in unmeasured ways from the comparison group.

### 5. Depression That Never Lets Up Speeds the Slide Into Frailty, Four Global Studies Agree

**Journal:** *J Affect Disord* | **Published:** 2027 Jan 1
**PMID:** 42822543 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jad.2026.122574

**The study:** Researchers pooled 57,894 older participants from four major aging cohorts (HRS in the US, ELSA in England, SHARE in Europe, CHARLS in China) and classified each person's depressive symptom course across their first four assessments into five patterns: persistently healthy, remitting, late-onset, fluctuating, and persistent high burden. The persistent high burden group — 8% to 20% of participants depending on the cohort — consistently went on to accumulate frailty faster and had 1.3 to 2.75 times the odds of becoming frail. Lower education predicted membership in the worse trajectories across all four cohorts.

**Why it matters:** Frailty brings falls, fractures and loss of independence, and this suggests that chronically untreated low mood — not a single bad spell — is a marker for that decline, making sustained depression management a potential lever on physical health.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If low mood has been a constant for years rather than a passing episode, it's worth raising again with your doctor — this research links persistent, unrelieved depressive symptoms to faster physical decline, while symptoms that remitted were not associated with the same trajectory.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For caregivers and senior living staff: the pattern over time matters more than a single screening score. Residents with chronically high symptom burden warrant ongoing follow-up and re-treatment, not a one-time referral.

**Caveats:** Observational cohort data — associations cannot establish that treating depression prevents frailty; depressive symptoms measured by self-report scales (CES-D, EURO-D) rather than clinical diagnosis; trajectory classification was rule-based and the CHARLS association was concurrent rather than predictive.

### 6. Frailty — Not Muscle Loss Alone — Predicts Falls Over Six Years

**Journal:** *Arch Gerontol Geriatr* | **Published:** 2026 Nov
**PMID:** 42600484 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.archger.2026.106369

**The study:** Researchers followed 2,067 community-dwelling older adults in the Korean Frailty and Aging Cohort Study, checking every two years for six years whether participants developed trouble with daily tasks, falls, or severe mobility limits. They separated people with sarcopenia (low muscle mass and strength) from those with physical frailty (the Fried phenotype: weakness, slowness, exhaustion, low activity, weight loss). Frailty alone predicted a 60% higher risk of falls, a 54% higher risk of basic daily-living disability, and nearly double the risk of severe mobility limitation; sarcopenia alone predicted none of these outcomes.

**Why it matters:** It suggests that a short frailty check — walking speed, grip strength, fatigue, activity level, weight loss — may flag fall risk more usefully than muscle-mass measurements alone, and frailty markers are things exercise and nutrition can move.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have been told you have low muscle mass, ask your clinician whether you also meet criteria for frailty — slow walking, weak grip, unintentional weight loss, exhaustion, low activity. Those frailty signs, more than a muscle scan, were what tracked with falls in this study, and they are worth discussing at your next visit.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living and rehab staff: a brief Fried-phenotype screen (gait speed, grip, self-reported exhaustion and activity) may identify residents at risk for falls and mobility loss earlier than body-composition testing, and can be built into routine wellness check-ins.

**Caveats:** Observational cohort — cannot establish causation; single-country (South Korea) population may not generalize; falls and some disability outcomes were assessed at two-year intervals, which can miss events; authors declare potential competing financial interests.

### 7. Why Uneven Ground Trips Up Some Older Walkers More Than Others — a Brain Clue

**Journal:** *Neurobiol Aging* | **Published:** 2026 Dec
**PMID:** 42679689 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.neurobiolaging.2026.08.004

**The study:** Researchers had 22 young adults (average age 22) and 86 older adults (average age 75) walk on a treadmill across flat and three levels of uneven terrain while also undergoing brain MRI scans of white matter — the wiring that connects brain regions. Older adults were split into higher- and lower-functioning groups using the Short Physical Performance Battery. Lower-functioning older adults had more irregular step timing at baseline and their stepping became far more erratic as the terrain got bumpier; their gait irregularity was also more strongly tied to a marker of fluid accumulation in brain white matter, especially in the corpus callosum.

**Why it matters:** It helps explain why some older adults manage curbs, gravel, and lawn just fine while others with similar health destabilize quickly — differences in brain wiring health, not just leg strength, appear to be part of the picture.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you notice that uneven surfaces — grass, gravel, cracked sidewalks — feel much harder than flat floors, that is a meaningful signal worth mentioning to your doctor or physical therapist rather than simply avoiding those surfaces. A simple balance and gait assessment can show where you stand.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and community planners: a resident who walks confidently down a hallway may still be at real risk outdoors. Audit the uneven surfaces people actually cross daily — parking lot transitions, garden paths, door thresholds — and consider supervised practice on varied terrain as part of mobility programs.

**Caveats:** Observational and cross-sectional — cannot establish causation; small young-adult comparison group (N=22); treadmill terrain is not the same as real-world walking; study measured gait variability, not actual falls; single-center.

### 8. Visual Hallucinations From Sight Loss May Be an Overlooked Cause of Falls

**Journal:** *Clin Geriatr Med* | **Published:** 2026 Nov
**PMID:** 42833804 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.cger.2026.08.004

**The study:** This clinical review examines Charles Bonnet Syndrome (CBS) — vivid visual hallucinations that occur in people losing their eyesight, not from dementia or mental illness — and its emerging link to falls. The authors argue that while CBS has traditionally been framed as a psychological issue, hallucinated images such as patterns, figures, or distorted floors can cause trips, missteps, and startle reactions. They note awareness of CBS is low among both sight-loss and falls-prevention specialists, and propose that falls services screen for it across primary, hospital, and community settings.

**Why it matters:** Many people with CBS never mention their hallucinations because they fear being thought to have dementia — meaning a genuinely treatable, modifiable fall hazard goes unspoken in the exam room.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If your vision is declining and you sometimes see things that are not there — patterns, faces, figures, odd floor textures — this is a recognized consequence of sight loss, not a sign you are losing your mind. Tell your eye doctor or GP, especially if you have had near-misses or falls, because it can be factored into your fall-prevention plan.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For caregivers and senior living staff: hallucinations in a resident with macular degeneration or glaucoma should prompt consideration of Charles Bonnet Syndrome before dementia or medication-induced psychosis is assumed. Ask directly and gently — most people will not volunteer it — and flag it in fall risk assessments.

**Caveats:** Narrative clinical review, not original research — no new data, no effect sizes; the CBS–falls link is described as emerging rather than established; no trial evidence yet that CBS-adapted falls programs reduce falls.

### 9. America's New National Falls Prevention Plan: What It Asks of Clinics and Communities

**Journal:** *Clin Geriatr Med* | **Published:** 2026 Nov
**PMID:** 42833801 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.cger.2026.08.001

**The study:** Authors from the National Council on Aging describe the 2025 National Falls Prevention Action Plan, built from extensive surveys of professionals and a nationwide summit involving hundreds of people across aging services, disability, housing, government, technology, research, and advocacy. The article lays out the plan's strategies and translates them into recommendations for clinical practice and for formal partnerships between health care providers and community organizations.

**Why it matters:** It signals that falls prevention is shifting from a once-a-year doctor's question to a coordinated effort spanning housing, community exercise programs, and technology — which means more free or low-cost local resources older adults can actually use.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Ask your primary care provider whether they can refer you to an evidence-based community falls program such as a balance or tai chi class — the national plan explicitly pushes clinicians to make those referrals instead of stopping at a verbal warning. Your local Area Agency on Aging is another direct route in.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living operators and community organizations: the plan frames clinical-community linkage as the central strategy, creating an opening to build referral relationships with local health systems and to position housing and transportation fixes as falls interventions, not just amenities.

**Caveats:** Policy and framework article, not a research study — no outcome data showing the plan reduces falls; authored by the organization that produced the plan; U.S.-specific.

### 10. Falls Prevention Works in Lower-Income Countries Too — But the Evidence Is Thin

**Journal:** *Disabil Rehabil* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 19
**PMID:** 42895234 | **DOI:** 10.1080/09638288.2026.2749688

**The study:** Researchers searched three databases and screened 792 articles, ultimately reviewing 14 randomized and quasi-experimental studies of falls prevention for older adults across seven low- and middle-income countries. Multifactorial programs, exercise-based programs, and home safety modifications were each associated with fewer falls, lower fall risk, and reduced fear of falling. But the authors found little evidence on how well these programs adapt to local cultures, scale up, or last over time.

**Why it matters:** The same core ingredients — strength and balance training plus home hazard fixes — appear to work across very different settings, reinforcing that these are the fundamentals worth investing in regardless of resources.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** The strategies with the broadest evidence behind them are unglamorous and low-cost: regular strength and balance exercise, and removing hazards at home like loose rugs, poor lighting, and clutter on stairs. You can start both this week without specialized equipment.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For organizations serving culturally diverse or resource-limited older populations: the review's central caution is that programs designed in wealthy countries need genuine local adaptation, not direct transplant — consider housing type, family structure, and footwear norms when adapting a curriculum.

**Caveats:** Integrative review of only 14 studies with wide variation in interventions and outcome measures; authors themselves urge caution in interpretation; limited data on long-term sustainability; no pooled effect estimate.

### 11. Painkiller Prescribing After Hip Fracture Has Barely Budged — Even as Sedative Use Plummets

**Journal:** *J Am Geriatr Soc* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 9
**PMID:** 42852585 | **DOI:** 10.1111/jgs.70739

**The study:** Researchers linked Ontario health administrative records for 114,055 adults aged 66 and older admitted with an acute hip fracture between 2008 and 2024, tracking what they were dispensed before hospitalization and for a full year after discharge. Benzodiazepine dispensing fell sharply, from 33.7% of patients in 2008 to 13.3% in 2024, and combined opioid-plus-benzodiazepine use dropped from 3.7% to 1%. Opioid dispensing, however, peaked at 55.8% in 2015 and still stood at 44.6% in 2024, with hydromorphone and combination products most common. Most people on 90 days of continuous opioids received under 50 mg average daily morphine equivalents.

**Why it matters:** Both drug classes raise the risk of a second fall, dependence, and death with prolonged use — and nearly half of hip fracture patients are still leaving the hospital on opioids a year out, in a country with no standardized prescribing guidance for this group.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you are recovering from a hip fracture, ask your prescriber at every visit how long your pain medication is meant to continue and what the plan is for tapering it — and mention any new dizziness, grogginess, or near-falls, since those drugs can set up the next fracture.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For care teams and senior living staff: the dramatic drop in benzodiazepine use shows system-wide prescribing change is achievable, which raises the question of why opioid continuation after hip fracture has resisted the same trend. Medication reviews at the 3- and 6-month post-discharge marks are a concrete place to intervene.

**Caveats:** Observational cohort using dispensing records, so it cannot establish causation or confirm that medications were actually taken; single-province Canadian data that may not generalize to other health systems; the analysis excluded people who died within the first ~90 days of the follow-up year.

### 12. Which Falls End in a Head Injury — and the Movements That Seem to Prevent It

**Journal:** *J Am Med Dir Assoc* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 5
**PMID:** 42832847 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jamda.2026.106493

**The study:** Investigators screened 2,921 studies and identified 13 examining what makes an older adult's fall result in head impact, most based on observational analysis of video-recorded real falls in long-term care. Female sex, falls initiated forward, and forward landings were consistently linked to higher odds of hitting the head, while rotating the body away from the initial fall direction and using the arms during descent were linked to lower odds. One randomized trial showed older adults could learn protective movements and reduce head impact during experimentally induced falls.

**Why it matters:** Head impact is what turns a fall from an inconvenience into a life-altering or fatal injury, and this review suggests that how you land — unlike your age or sex — may be partly trainable.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Most fall prevention focuses on not falling; this research points to a second skill worth asking a physical therapist about — how to fall more safely, including using your arms and rotating as you go down rather than landing flat and forward.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For long-term care operators and rehab staff: protective-response training, flooring and environmental design, and protective headgear are the modifiable levers here, and the evidence base is thin enough that facilities adopting them should be documenting outcomes.

**Caveats:** Scoping review of only 13 studies, mostly observational video analysis in long-term care settings that may not generalize to community-dwelling older adults; only one interventional trial, conducted with experimentally induced rather than real-world falls; no pooled effect estimates.

### 13. An ER Visit for a Fall May Be an Early Warning Sign of an Undiagnosed Seizure Disorder

**Journal:** *Neurology* | **Published:** 2026 Nov 10
**PMID:** 42848964 | **DOI:** 10.1212/WNL.0000000000218597

**The study:** Using emergency department and inpatient databases from 11 US states covering 2016–2021, researchers identified 57,547 adults with a first-time seizure diagnosis and used a case-crossover design — comparing each patient with their own history a year earlier — to see whether fall-related ED visits clustered before the seizure. Among the 7,307 patients with a prior fall visit, the odds of an ED visit for a fall were 2.6 times higher in the week before the first recognized seizure than in the matched control period, and the signal persisted up to 22 weeks beforehand. For patients whose first presentation was status epilepticus, the odds in the preceding week rose to 4.5, and results held after excluding traumatic brain injury cases.

**Why it matters:** Seizures go unrecognized in a large share of people with epilepsy, and this suggests some unexplained falls — especially recurrent or unwitnessed ones — are actually seizures being missed in the emergency department.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have had an unexplained fall with no clear trip or slip, and especially if you had a gap in memory, confusion afterward, a bitten tongue, or loss of bladder control, tell the clinician directly and ask whether a seizure should be ruled out.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and senior living staff: your observation of what happened immediately before and after a fall is diagnostic information no test can replace. Write down whether the person was responsive, whether there were jerking movements, and how long confusion lasted, and bring that note to the ED.

**Caveats:** Retrospective administrative-claims study relying on ICD-10 coding, so it cannot establish that the falls were caused by seizures; mean age of the fall subgroup was 49.3 years, younger than the typical senior-living population, though findings were reported as consistent across age strata; data from 11 states only.

### 14. "It's Not for Me": Why Older Adults With Disabilities Avoid Fall Prevention Classes

**Journal:** *Disabil Rehabil* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 5
**PMID:** 42831317 | **DOI:** 10.1080/09638288.2026.2740573

**The study:** Researchers conducted semi-structured virtual interviews with 11 adults aged 50 and older (mean age 69.8) who reported a mobility, vision, or hearing impairment, asking what kept them out of community-based fall prevention exercise programs. Four themes emerged: poor awareness and advertising, layered accessibility barriers spanning physical, sensory, logistical, and financial dimensions, the shifting experience of acquiring disability with age, and program design mismatches. Participants said the programs carried a stigma of being "for those who are weak," and that self-stereotyping deterred them from signing up.

**Why it matters:** Fall prevention exercise programs work, but they only work for people who enroll — and this study identifies branding and stigma, not just transportation or cost, as a reason the people at highest risk stay away.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have skipped a balance or fall prevention class because it felt like admitting defeat, consider that the evidence supports these programs as strength and independence training. It is reasonable to ask a program whether it can accommodate your vision, hearing, or mobility needs before you commit.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For program directors and senior living activity staff: the name on the flyer may be your biggest barrier. Participants recommended co-designing programs with people who have disabilities and rebranding away from deficit language toward mobility, strength, and independence.

**Caveats:** Small qualitative study (N=11) from a single Canadian research group; self-reported impairments; findings describe perceptions and are not designed to measure program effectiveness or generalize statistically.

### 15. Stopping Blood Pressure Pills in Nursing Homes: The Evidence Is Thinner Than You'd Expect

**Journal:** *Drugs Aging* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 16
**PMID:** 42888381 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40266-026-01342-5

**The study:** Researchers systematically searched for randomized trials of planned antihypertensive deprescribing among long-term care residents and found just three studies covering 1,548 residents. The trials differed so much in strategy, monitoring, restart rules, and follow-up that the authors declined to pool them. In DANTON, deprescribing raised systolic blood pressure by 11.0 mmHg and cut antihypertensive drugs by 1.2 per resident at 16 weeks; mortality estimates diverged across trials (hazard ratio 1.02 in RETREAT-FRAIL over a median 38.4 months versus 1.65 in DANTON to 32 weeks), and evidence on falls, hospitalization, cognition, function, and quality of life was sparse or at risk of bias.

**Why it matters:** Deprescribing blood pressure medication is frequently promoted as a way to cut dizziness and falls in frail nursing home residents, but this review shows the randomized evidence does not yet demonstrate benefit on falls or any other outcome residents care about.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If a clinician proposes reducing your blood pressure medications to lower fall risk, it is fair to ask what the plan is for monitoring, what blood pressure would trigger restarting, and how long the trial period will last — the research shows these details vary enormously and matter more than the decision to taper itself.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For long-term care prescribers and nursing staff: this review argues that trial-specific protocols, with explicit monitoring and restart criteria, are more useful guidance right now than any single pooled effect estimate. Deprescribing should be treated as a supervised, reversible trial rather than a one-time discontinuation.

**Caveats:** Only three randomized trials met inclusion criteria, with substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity preventing meta-analysis; outcomes relevant to falls were sparse and at risk of bias; follow-up ranged from 32 weeks to over three years, limiting comparability.

### 16. A Nudge in the Doctor's Computer Cut Falls for 12,000 Older Patients

**Journal:** *JAMA Netw Open* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 1
**PMID:** 42886484 | **DOI:** 10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.39240

**The study:** Researchers randomized 22 primary care practices in a US health system to either usual care or eSTEPS — a fall-prevention prompt built into the electronic health record that gave clinicians tailored exercise recommendations, an optional physical therapy referral, and printed/app-based materials for patients. Among 11,945 patients aged 65 and older (mean age 77, 67% female) who screened positive for fall risk, practices using eSTEPS had 19.3 patients with falls per 100 patient-years versus 21.2 in control practices — a 9% relative reduction — and a 6% reduction in fall-related injuries. Nearly 78% of clinicians in intervention practices used the tool at least once, though only 22% of eligible patients actually received an exercise prescription.

**Why it matters:** Exercise is proven to prevent falls, but it rarely makes it into a busy 15-minute primary care visit; this trial shows that embedding the prescription into the clinic's existing software moves the needle at scale, across thousands of patients, without new staff or clinics.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If your doctor asked you three quick questions about falling — have you fallen, do you feel unsteady, do you worry about falling — that screen is supposed to lead somewhere. At your next visit, you can ask directly: "Based on my fall risk, what balance and strength exercises do you recommend, and would physical therapy help?" A written exercise plan, not just a warning to be careful, is what reduced falls in this trial.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For health system leaders and senior living medical directors: this is an implementation story. The benefit was modest but real, and it came from redesigning workflow rather than adding programs — yet only one in five at-risk patients got a prescription, suggesting a lot of headroom if uptake improves. Care coordinators can also act as the backstop, checking whether a positive fall screen in the chart was followed by an actual referral.

**Caveats:** Falls were captured from ICD-10 diagnostic codes in medical records, which undercount falls that don't prompt a medical visit; effect sizes were modest; practices were within a single integrated health system, so results may not generalize to smaller or less digitally equipped settings. Funded by the National Institute on Aging; one co-author reports equity in health-technology companies.

### 17. Day 3 and Day 7 in the Hospital: When Fall Risk Quietly Changes

**Journal:** *Eur Geriatr Med* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 7
**PMID:** 42842205 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s41999-026-01622-8

**The study:** Using electronic records from two Japanese tertiary hospitals (2018–2024), researchers tracked over 83,000 hospitalized patients aged 65 to 106 and checked how their ability to transfer in and out of bed, plus blood markers, changed by hospital days 3 and 7. Patients whose transfer ability stayed impaired or got worse had significantly higher odds of falling in the following 30 days, and drops in hemoglobin and serum sodium were independently linked to falls as well. Changes in albumin were not associated with fall risk.

**Why it matters:** Most hospitals assess fall risk once, on admission — but this very large study shows risk evolves during the stay, meaning a patient scored as low-risk on day one can become high-risk by day three or seven without anyone re-checking.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or a loved one is in the hospital for more than a few days, fall risk is not fixed at the front door. It's reasonable to ask the nurse or physician partway through the stay: "Has my fall risk been reassessed since admission? Has my blood count or sodium changed?" Needing more help getting out of bed than you did on day one is worth reporting, not pushing through.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For nurses, hospitalists, and discharge planners: this supports building scheduled fall-risk reassessments at days 3 and 7 into standard protocol rather than relying on admission scores, and flags falling hemoglobin and sodium as routinely available early-warning signals. Families visiting daily are often the first to notice a decline in transfer ability and should be told their observations matter.

**Caveats:** Retrospective and observational — it identifies associations, not causes. Falls were taken from incident reports, which can be underreported. Conducted at two tertiary hospitals in Japan, so case mix and care practices may not generalize to US community hospitals or nursing facilities.

### 18. A Taiwan Study of 245,000 Seniors Maps Which Combinations of Decline Predict ER Visits for Falls

**Journal:** *Exp Gerontol* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 1
**PMID:** 42575258 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.exger.2026.113274

**The study:** Researchers followed 244,899 Taiwanese adults aged 65 and older for about a year using the national ICOPE screening program linked to insurance claims, scoring six domains of "intrinsic capacity": cognition, mobility, vitality, vision, hearing, and psychological well-being. Impairments in mobility (58% higher rate), cognition (23% higher), and vision (20% higher) each independently predicted fall-related emergency department visits, and risk climbed as the number of impaired domains grew. The highest-risk group — those with combined physical, cognitive, and sensory decline — had nearly double the rate of fall-related ER visits.

**Why it matters:** It's the stacking that matters: these findings suggest fall risk is driven less by any single deficit than by combinations, especially when slowing mobility is paired with memory changes and poor vision.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you've noticed more than one change at once — walking has slowed, your memory feels foggier, and your glasses prescription is out of date — that cluster deserves attention as a package, not three separate shrugs. Ask your doctor for a check across mobility, thinking, vision, hearing, nutrition, and mood, and start with the fixables: an eye exam and a cataract evaluation are among the most concrete steps available.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living staff and care managers: this argues for multi-domain screening rather than single-item fall checklists, and for prioritizing residents who show combined physical-cognitive-sensory decline for the most intensive prevention resources. Those residents are a minority but carry disproportionate emergency department risk.

**Caveats:** Observational cohort — cannot establish causation. Short follow-up (2022–2023) and outcomes limited to ER visits, so falls handled at home or in clinic were missed. Population is Taiwanese adults who voluntarily participated in national screening, which may skew toward the health-engaged; funded in part by Taiwan's Health Promotion Administration.

### 19. Meals Plus Exercise at Home: Homebound Seniors Say Pain and Fear of Falling Got in the Way

**Journal:** *J Am Geriatr Soc* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 8
**PMID:** 42847733 | **DOI:** 10.1111/jgs.70780

**The study:** As a follow-up to the "Strength on Wheels" randomized trial, researchers analyzed open-ended survey responses from 101 homebound older adults (mean age 74) who received 12 weeks of Meals on Wheels deliveries combined with a home-based exercise program. Participants enjoyed the meals and engaged with the exercise materials, but many reported persistent pain and worry about falling while exercising alone. Reactions to the commercial activity tracker were mixed — some found it motivating, while others struggled to charge it, read its icons, or keep track of it. Participants said what they also needed was social contact, cognitive activity, physical therapy, transportation, and home modifications.

**Why it matters:** The original trial found no improvement in physical measures, and these participant voices explain why: an exercise program mailed to a homebound older adult with untreated pain and real fear of falling may go largely unperformed, no matter how good the design.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're doing home exercises on your own and holding back because something hurts or you're afraid of losing your balance, that's not a motivation problem — it's a signal to get pain addressed and to ask your doctor about a home physical therapy visit so someone can watch you move and set up a safe routine. Also worth saying out loud: if a wearable tracker is more frustrating than helpful, you don't need one to benefit from exercise.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For home-delivered meal programs and home care agencies: pairing nutrition with exercise isn't enough on its own. These participants named pain management, in-person physical therapy, home modifications, and social connection as the missing pieces — and drivers delivering meals are well positioned to flag who is in pain or afraid to move.

**Caveats:** Qualitative analysis of free-text survey responses, not a test of effectiveness; the parent trial found no improvement in physical measures. Self-reported experiences, single program setting, and homebound Meals on Wheels clients may not represent other older adults.

### 20. Hip Fracture Recovery: A Five-Question Survey Beat Grip Strength at Predicting Who Bounces Back

**Journal:** *J Am Med Dir Assoc* | **Published:** 2026 Oct
**PMID:** 42696807 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jamda.2026.106450

**The study:** Finnish researchers followed 430 adults aged 70 and older for one year after hip fracture surgery at a single university hospital, comparing three ways of screening for sarcopenia (age-related muscle loss): handgrip strength, calf circumference, and the five-item SARC-F questionnaire, which asks about strength, walking, rising from a chair, climbing stairs, and falls. Only SARC-F consistently predicted outcomes — patients flagged by it were far less likely to get up and moving in the first days after surgery (odds ratio 7.56 by day 3) and had worse walking ability and larger drops in daily function at four months. Notably, neither group improved meaningfully in daily function between 4 and 12 months, suggesting the early months are the critical recovery window.

**Why it matters:** A one-minute questionnaire that requires no equipment can identify, right at admission, which hip fracture patients are headed for a poor recovery — and those first four months appear to be when rehabilitation effort pays off.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or a loved one is admitted with a hip fracture, ask the care team whether a sarcopenia screen like SARC-F has been done and what the result means for your rehab plan. The study suggests the first four months after surgery are when functional gains happen, so that is the time to push for intensive physical therapy rather than assuming recovery will keep improving over the full year.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For rehab and post-acute teams, this is an argument for a cheap, equipment-free triage tool at admission: SARC-F outperformed handgrip dynamometry and calf circumference at flagging patients who will need more intensive, front-loaded rehabilitation resources and likely more discharge support.

**Caveats:** Single-center study at one Finnish university hospital; observational cohort design, so SARC-F predicts but does not cause outcomes; population may not generalize to other health systems; one author has served on an Amgen advisory board.

### 21. Balance Exercise for People With Mild Memory Problems: The Trial Fell Short, but Those Who Stuck With It Improved

**Journal:** *Gerontologist* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 21
**PMID:** 42904808 | **DOI:** 10.1093/geront/gnag245

**The study:** Australian researchers ran a single-blind randomized controlled trial of "Balance on the Brain," a six-month multimodal program combining walking with two balance training programs, in 126 community-dwelling adults with mild cognitive impairment (median age 76, 63% female), followed by a six-month maintenance period. The trial did not recruit enough participants to be adequately powered, so the lack of difference between groups on falls and balance is inconclusive rather than negative. In exploratory analysis, the 59% of intervention participants classified as "adherers" showed significantly better balance at both 6 and 12 months than low-adherers and controls, and fell less, though the fall difference was not statistically significant.

**Why it matters:** People with mild cognitive impairment fall at high rates and are often excluded from fall-prevention programs, and this trial suggests the real obstacle may be sticking with the exercise rather than whether it works.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have been told you have mild cognitive impairment, don't assume balance training isn't for you — but know that the benefit in this trial showed up only among people who actually kept doing the exercises. Ask your clinician or physiotherapist what support exists to help you stay with a program: scheduled sessions, a walking partner, or reminders.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living operators and family caregivers, the headline finding is about adherence infrastructure. Roughly four in ten participants in the exercise arm didn't keep up with it; programs for residents with cognitive impairment likely need built-in prompting, supervision, and routine rather than a handout and good intentions.

**Caveats:** Underpowered — the trial did not reach its target sample size, so the primary falls and balance results are inconclusive, not negative; the adherer analysis is exploratory, post hoc, and compares self-selected groups, so motivated participants may have differed in other ways; single-country community sample.

### 22. Could Imagining Movement Improve Balance? Researchers Say the Evidence Is Still Too Scattered to Tell

**Journal:** *Exp Gerontol* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 1
**PMID:** 42501905 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.exger.2026.113253

**The study:** UK researchers conducted a scoping review of "action simulation" interventions — mentally rehearsing movements (motor imagery) or watching others perform them (action observation) — as a way to improve balance, gait, and mobility in healthy community-dwelling adults aged 60 and over. Nineteen studies covering 587 older adults were identified, most of them randomized controlled trials, with motor imagery the most common approach. Findings were highly heterogeneous across studies and outcome types, only one study looked beyond a month after the intervention, and few reported on whether the approach was practical or acceptable to participants.

**Why it matters:** Mental rehearsal is appealing for older adults who can't easily do physical exercise because of pain, frailty, or fear of falling, but at present there is not enough consistent evidence to treat it as a substitute for actual balance training.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have seen claims that visualizing movement can improve your balance, treat it as an unproven add-on rather than a replacement for physical exercise — supervised, progressive balance and strength training remains the approach with the strongest fall-prevention evidence. If physical activity is difficult for you, that's worth raising directly with your doctor or physiotherapist.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For activity directors and therapists tempted to add imagery-based sessions to programming, this review is a caution: the research base is small, inconsistent, and almost entirely short-term, with little data on whether older adults find the techniques acceptable or can do them unsupervised.

**Caveats:** Scoping review rather than a meta-analysis, so no pooled effect estimate; total pool of only 587 participants across 19 studies; studies limited to healthy community-dwelling older adults, so findings don't apply to frail or cognitively impaired populations; almost no long-term follow-up data.

### 23. In a Study of Nearly a Million Diabetes Patients, One Drug Class Was Linked to Fewer Falls and Fractures Than Another

**Journal:** *Diabet Med* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 19
**PMID:** 42896077 | **DOI:** 10.1111/dme.70504

**The study:** Using Taiwan's national health insurance database, researchers emulated a clinical trial comparing 224,510 people with type 2 diabetes who newly started an SGLT2 inhibitor against 698,012 who newly started a DPP-4 inhibitor, statistically balancing the groups on baseline characteristics. The SGLT2 inhibitor group had modestly lower rates of fractures or falls (hazard ratio 0.93), plus lower rates of urinary tract infection, acute kidney injury, and all-cause mortality (HR 0.66). The one clear downside was genital infection, which was about 44% more common with SGLT2 inhibitors. Results held in most "vulnerable" subgroups, including frailer patients.

**Why it matters:** SGLT2 inhibitors have been held back in older and frailer patients partly on fears about falls, dehydration, and infection, and this very large real-world comparison suggests those specific fears may be overstated relative to the alternative.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have type 2 diabetes and have been steered away from an SGLT2 inhibitor because of concerns about falls or urinary infections, this is a reasonable topic to raise at your next appointment — but this is observational data from insurance records, not a randomized trial, and the right medication depends on your kidney function, heart history, and other drugs you take. Do not change or stop any diabetes medication on your own.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For prescribers and care home clinicians managing residents with diabetes, the finding that the safety advantage persisted across vulnerable subgroups is the notable part — though genital infections were meaningfully more common, which matters for residents who need help with personal hygiene.

**Caveats:** Observational database study using propensity-score weighting — it cannot establish causation, and unmeasured differences between people prescribed each drug class (confounding by indication) remain possible; falls and fractures were identified from administrative claims rather than direct assessment; conducted entirely in Taiwan, so results may not generalize to other populations or prescribing cultures; the fall/fracture difference was small in absolute terms.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**

- **Multi-domain decline, not any single deficit, drives falls.** "A Taiwan Study of 245,000 Seniors Maps Which Combinations of Decline Predict ER Visits for Falls" (PMID 42575258) and "In Knee Arthritis, Hearing, Vision and Mood Predicted First Falls Better Than the Knee Itself" (PMID 42759165) both carry over from the previous digest ("A Six-Part Health Check Predicts Who Lands in the ER After a Fall," PMID 42575258; "Hearing, Vision and Mood — Not Just Knees," PMID 42759165), and the new "Turn Your Head While Walking: A New Test Reveals Who's Losing Their Balance" (PMID 42895590) adds a mechanism: eye, inner-ear and neck function measured directly, with the strongest links in people who had already fallen.
- **Frailty and brief questionnaires still beat equipment.** "Frailty — Not Muscle Loss Alone — Predicts Falls Over Six Years" (PMID 42600484) and "Hip Fracture Recovery: A Five-Question Survey Beat Grip Strength at Predicting Who Bounces Back" (PMID 42696807) both repeat from the previous digest (PMIDs 42600484, 42696807), reinforcing the same conclusion from two different directions — community risk and post-surgical recovery.
- **Depression's duration is the signal.** "Depression That Never Lets Up Speeds the Slide Into Frailty, Four Global Studies Agree" (PMID 42822543) recurs from "Persistent Depression in Later Life Predicts Faster Decline Into Frailty" (PMID 42822543), and now sits alongside PMID 42759165, where depressive symptoms were among the top predictors of a first fall.
- **Timing within a hospital stay matters.** "Day 3 and Day 7 in the Hospital: When Fall Risk Quietly Changes" (PMID 42842205) repeats the previous digest's finding (PMID 42842205) that admission-day scoring misses risk that develops over the first week.

**Advances**

- **A scalable intervention finally shows a fall reduction.** The previous digest ended with a policy roadmap — "A New National Blueprint Says Falls Prevention Can't Be Left to Doctors Alone" (PMID 42833801) — and this batch supplies the first matching trial evidence: "A Nudge in the Doctor's Computer Cut Falls for 12,000 Older Patients" (PMID 42886484), where an electronic health record prompt produced a 9% relative reduction in patients with falls across 22 practices.
- **From where falls happen to exactly when and where.** "Most Fractures in Men Over 85 Happen Indoors, During Ordinary Activity — and Often Without the Walker" (PMID 42897253) is new and sharpens the environmental picture that "Why Uneven Ground Trips Up Some Older Walkers More Than Others" (PMID 42679689, also in the previous digest) drew outdoors: for the oldest men, 71% of fracture-causing falls were indoors during ordinary movement.
- **Post-fall practice gets a tool.** "A One-Page Checklist Transformed What Hospitals Record After a Patient Falls" (PMID 42877601) is genuinely new territory, and complements the day-3/day-7 reassessment case in PMID 42842205 — one covers risk before the fall, the other what gets checked afterward.
- **Global evidence base widens.** "Falls Prevention Works in Lower-Income Countries Too — But the Evidence Is Thin" (PMID 42895234) extends the exercise-plus-home-modification core that earlier runs established in wealthy countries, while flagging cultural adaptation as the open question.

**Counterpoints**

- **Deprescribing is not automatically a falls intervention.** "Stopping Blood Pressure Pills in Nursing Homes: The Evidence Is Thinner Than You'd Expect" (PMID 42888381) finds only three randomized trials and no demonstrated benefit on falls — a direct check on the assumption behind the previous digest's prescribing items, including "Sedatives Are Finally Fading After Hip Fractures" (PMID 42852585, repeated here as PMID 42852585).
- **A drug class avoided over fall fears may not deserve it.** "In a Study of Nearly a Million Diabetes Patients, One Drug Class Was Linked to Fewer Falls and Fractures Than Another" (PMID 42896077) runs against reflexive caution about SGLT2 inhibitors in frailer patients, much as the previous digest's "Newer Sleep Drugs Don't Appear to Raise Fall Risk" (PMID 42799573) complicated blanket sedative rules.
- **A balance trial that couldn't answer its own question.** "Balance Exercise for People With Mild Memory Problems: The Trial Fell Short, but Those Who Stuck With It Improved" (PMID 42904808) is a cautionary note against the clean exercise-works message, and pairs with the previous digest's stigma finding in "'It's Not for Me'" (PMID 42831317, repeated here).
- **Mental rehearsal stays unproven.** "Could Imagining Movement Improve Balance?" (PMID 42501905) repeats the previous digest's verdict (PMID 42501905) without new evidence to move it.

**New themes**

- Structured post-fall documentation as a cheap quality lever, with a worrying signal that residents with dementia were less likely to receive it — PMID 42877601.
- Falls prevention in low- and middle-income countries, and the limits of transplanting wealthy-country curricula — PMID 42895234.
- Routine diabetes prescribing decisions as a falls variable — PMID 42896077.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Several studies in this batch converge on the same uncomfortable finding — the things proven to prevent falls keep not getting used. In "Most Fractures in Men Over 85 Happen Indoors" (PMID 42897253), 42% of men who regularly used a walking aid were not using it when they broke a bone; in "A Nudge in the Doctor's Computer Cut Falls for 12,000 Older Patients" (PMID 42886484), only 22% of eligible patients actually received an exercise prescription; in "Balance Exercise for People With Mild Memory Problems" (PMID 42904808), benefit appeared only among the 59% who stuck with the program; and "'It's Not for Me'" (PMID 42831317) and "Meals Plus Exercise at Home" (PMID 42847733) explain the refusals — stigma, pain, fear of exercising alone, and devices people couldn't manage.

**Why pitch this now:** The 2025 National Falls Prevention Action Plan described in PMID 42833801 assumes clinical referrals land in community programs that people attend and finish — and this batch is the first to put numbers on every point where that chain breaks.

**Angle:** A practical feature on the gap between the fall-prevention plan someone is given and the one they actually live with — why walkers stay in the closet, why classes feel like an insult, and what makes a program stick — written for older adults and family caregivers, with a sidebar for program directors and home care agencies on designing for adherence rather than attendance.

**Potential headlines:**
- The Walker in the Hall Closet: What Fall Prevention Looks Like When It Doesn't Get Used
- The Balance Class Worked. Four in Ten People Stopped Going.
- Your Fall-Prevention Plan Is Only as Good as the Part You Keep Doing

**Potential outlets:**
- **National Council on Aging** — their own staff authored the national action plan in this batch, and the uptake chain it depends on is exactly what these studies measure.
- **Mobility Management** — the finding that 42% of regular walking-aid users weren't using the device at the moment of fracture is a direct challenge to how mobility equipment is prescribed, placed and followed up.
- **HomeCare Magazine** — home care agencies are the ones positioned to notice untreated pain, fear of moving alone, and exercise handouts gathering dust after a meal delivery.
- **The Senior List** — a consumer-facing read on why trackers, canes and classes go unused, and how to pick ones you'll actually stay with.

- **Turn Your Head While Walking: A New Test Reveals Who's Losing Their Balance (PMID 42895590):** Feeling wobbly when you glance over your shoulder is worth reporting, and standard balance tests never check it, for a consumer health explainer with a screening sidebar for rehab staff.
