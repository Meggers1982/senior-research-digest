### 1. Dynamic Exercise Outperforms Brief Strength Sessions for Balance and Walking Speed in Women with Bone Loss

**Journal:** *Aging Clinical and Experimental Research* | **Published:** July 5, 2026
**PMID:** 42402085 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40520-026-03421-4

**The study:** Swedish researchers randomized 194 women aged 65-79 with osteopenia or osteoporosis to nine months of either OsteoStrong (once-weekly, 20-minute high-intensity resistance sessions) or dynamic multicomponent exercise (twice-weekly, 60-minute sessions combining strength, balance, and mobility work). Both groups improved grip strength, back strength, and sit-to-stand performance. However, women in the dynamic exercise group gained significantly more in walking speed (+7.1% vs. +3.2%), back endurance (+27.6% vs. +4.4%), and one-leg standing balance (+13% vs. -2%).

**Why it matters:** For older women concerned about falls and fractures, this trial suggests that comprehensive exercise programs addressing multiple physical domains may offer greater functional benefits than brief, high-intensity-only approaches.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have low bone density, consider exercise programs that combine strength, balance, and walking practice—not just resistance training alone. The extra time investment may pay off in better balance and mobility, which are key to preventing falls.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living activity directors and physical therapists may want to prioritize multicomponent exercise classes over single-modality strength sessions when designing fall-prevention programming for residents with osteoporosis.

**Caveats:** Secondary outcome analysis (main study focused on bone density); no blinding possible given exercise differences; adherence rates may differ between once- and twice-weekly programs.

### 2. Low Handgrip Strength Predicts Future Osteoporosis Diagnosis Over 12 Years

**Journal:** *Experimental Gerontology* | **Published:** May 20, 2026
**PMID:** 42167463 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.exger.2026.113165

**The study:** Researchers followed 5,921 adults aged 50 and older without osteoporosis from the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing for a median of 12.5 years. Those with low handgrip strength at baseline (defined by European sarcopenia guidelines) had a 41% higher risk of being diagnosed with osteoporosis during follow-up, even after adjusting for age, sex, BMI, lifestyle factors, and other health conditions. The association was particularly strong among women.

**Why it matters:** Grip strength testing is quick, inexpensive, and can be done almost anywhere. This study suggests it may help identify people at risk for bone loss years before a fracture occurs.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** A simple grip strength test—available at many doctor's offices and gyms—could flag your risk for bone problems down the road. If your grip is weak, it's worth discussing bone health screening with your healthcare provider.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Primary care clinics and senior centers could use routine grip strength measurement as an inexpensive first-step screening tool to identify individuals who may benefit from bone density testing or preventive interventions.

**Caveats:** Osteoporosis diagnosis was self-reported; observational design cannot prove grip weakness causes bone loss; results may be strongest in women.

### 3. Older Adults with Arm Fractures Describe Fear of Falling and Gaps in Osteoporosis Education

**Journal:** *Journal of Geriatric Physical Therapy* | **Published:** July 1, 2026
**PMID:** 42358089 | **DOI:** 10.1519/JPT.0000000000000507

**The study:** Canadian researchers conducted in-depth interviews with 13 adults (average age 67) who had osteoporosis and had recently broken a bone in their arm from a minor fall. Participants described complex factors leading to their falls, fear of falling affecting daily activities, and limited awareness that osteoporosis itself increases fall and fracture risk. Many felt they received inadequate education about bone health and insufficient access to rehabilitation addressing both physical recovery and psychological concerns.

**Why it matters:** Upper extremity fractures are often a warning sign of underlying bone fragility, yet patients report feeling under-supported in understanding and preventing future falls.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you've broken a bone from a minor fall, ask your healthcare team directly about osteoporosis screening, fall prevention strategies, and whether physical or occupational therapy might help rebuild your confidence and strength.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Therapists and fracture clinic staff should proactively address fear of falling and bone health education—not just the broken bone—since patients may not realize these topics are connected or feel empowered to ask.

**Caveats:** Small qualitative study (13 participants); single center in Ontario; findings reflect patient perceptions, not measured outcomes.

### 4. Slow Gait Plus Depression or Weak Grip Linked to Sharply Higher Fracture Risk

**Journal:** *GeroScience* | **Published:** May 2, 2026
**PMID:** 42067696 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11357-026-02271-2

**The study:** In 16,357 Australians aged 70 and older from the ASPREE trial, researchers examined how slow walking, weak grip strength, and depressive symptoms—alone and in combination—predicted fractures over several years. Slow gait combined with depressive symptoms was associated with a 46% higher risk of any fracture and 64% higher risk of major osteoporotic fracture. Weak grip combined with depressive symptoms showed similarly elevated risks. These combined associations were stronger than either factor alone and more pronounced in men and people with diabetes.

**Why it matters:** Physical function and mental health interact to influence fracture risk. Addressing depression may be an overlooked component of fracture prevention.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you notice you're walking more slowly or feeling persistently down, mention both concerns to your doctor. Together, these issues may increase your risk of breaking a bone—and both can often be improved with the right support.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Fracture prevention programs in senior living communities should consider integrating mental health screening and support alongside physical therapy, particularly for residents with mobility limitations.

**Caveats:** Observational study cannot prove causation; depression and slow gait may share underlying causes; Australian population may not generalize to all settings.

### 5. Restricting Dietary Sugar in Early Life Linked to Lower Frailty Decades Later

**Journal:** *Journal of Nutrition, Health and Aging* | **Published:** May 29, 2026
**PMID:** 42214193 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jnha.2026.100883

**The study:** Using UK Biobank data from 63,793 participants, researchers examined people born during or just after World War II sugar rationing in Britain. Those exposed to sugar restriction during pregnancy and early infancy had lower frailty levels in older adulthood, with longer exposure periods showing stronger associations. Frailty also partially mediated (explained) the link between early sugar restriction and lower rates of osteoporosis and heart failure later in life.

**Why it matters:** This natural experiment suggests that early-life nutrition may have lasting effects on healthy aging, including bone health and overall resilience in older adulthood.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** While you can't change your early childhood diet, this research reinforces that nutrition matters across the lifespan. Limiting added sugars now may still support your overall health and vitality as you age.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Public health messaging to younger generations about reducing sugar intake may have benefits that extend far into old age, potentially reducing the future burden of frailty and osteoporosis.

**Caveats:** Observational/quasi-experimental design; rationing affected multiple nutrients, not just sugar; relies on historical exposure data; cannot establish direct causation.

### 6. Time-Restricted Eating May Protect Against Age-Related Bone Loss in Mice

**Journal:** *Aging Cell* | **Published:** June 2026
**PMID:** 42259765 | **DOI:** 10.1111/acel.70582

**The study:** Researchers found that aging mice had disrupted circadian clock activity in their muscles, including reduced levels of a key clock protein called Bmal1. When they genetically removed this protein from muscle cells, mice developed osteoporosis-like bone loss. The mechanism involved increased inflammatory signaling that promoted bone breakdown. Importantly, restricting feeding to nighttime hours (when mice naturally eat) restored healthy rhythms and reduced bone loss in both genetically altered and naturally aged mice.

**Why it matters:** This animal study suggests that when you eat—not just what you eat—may influence bone health as you age, pointing to time-restricted eating as a potential strategy worth investigating in humans.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** While human studies are still needed, this research adds to growing evidence that eating within a consistent daily window may support healthy aging. Discuss any dietary timing changes with your healthcare provider, especially if you have other health conditions.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living dining services might consider whether meal timing consistency could be optimized to support residents' circadian health, though human evidence for bone-specific benefits remains preliminary.

**Caveats:** Animal study (mice); human translation uncertain; time-restricted feeding protocols vary and may not be appropriate for all older adults, particularly those with diabetes or malnutrition risk.

### 7. Hip Abductor Strength and Diuretic Use Are Key Modifiable Predictors of Injurious Falls

**Journal:** *Archives of Gerontology and Geriatrics* | **Published:** June 8, 2026
**PMID:** 42296796 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.archger.2026.106333

**The study:** Among 952 adults aged 65 and older from the Geelong Osteoporosis Study, researchers built statistical models to identify which modifiable factors best predicted emergency department visits for fall injuries. Adding hip abductor muscle strength and diuretic medication use to basic clinical information improved prediction accuracy most substantially. Smoking status also contributed, while polypharmacy and fear of falling added little predictive value.

**Why it matters:** Strengthening hip muscles and reviewing diuretic prescriptions may be particularly high-yield targets for preventing serious fall injuries in older adults.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're concerned about falls, ask your physical therapist about exercises specifically targeting your hip muscles (the ones that help you balance side-to-side). Also, review your medications with your doctor—especially water pills—to see if adjustments might reduce your fall risk.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Fall-prevention programs should prioritize hip abductor strengthening exercises, and pharmacists conducting medication reviews should flag diuretics as a modifiable risk factor worth discussing with prescribers.

**Caveats:** Observational design; single geographic region (Australia); prediction models need validation in other populations.

### 8. Three Osteoporosis Medications Show Similar Bone Benefits in Men

**Journal:** *Age and Ageing* | **Published:** May 4, 2026
**PMID:** 42096654 | **DOI:** 10.1093/ageing/afag121

**The study:** In a randomized trial of 390 men with osteoporosis or osteopenia, participants received either denosumab (twice-yearly injection), zoledronic acid (annual infusion), or alendronate (weekly pill) for 12 months. All three medications similarly increased bone mineral density at the spine (4.3-5.2%) and hip (2.5-2.8%) and improved trabecular bone score, a measure of bone quality. Denosumab and alendronate caused fewer side effects than zoledronic acid. Benefits were similar regardless of testosterone levels.

**Why it matters:** Men with osteoporosis—often underdiagnosed and undertreated—now have evidence that multiple treatment options work comparably, allowing for individualized choices based on convenience, cost, and side effect profiles.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're a man diagnosed with osteoporosis or low bone density, know that several effective treatment options exist. Talk with your doctor about which medication format (pill, injection, or infusion) fits best with your lifestyle and health history.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Healthcare providers should actively screen older men for osteoporosis and discuss treatment options, since men often receive less attention for bone health despite facing significant fracture risk.

**Caveats:** Open-label design (participants knew which treatment they received); 12-month duration; conducted in China, which may affect generalizability.

### 9. Osteoporosis Screening Falls Short of New Guidelines in Primary Care

**Journal:** *Clinical Interventions in Aging* | **Published:** May 1, 2026
**PMID:** 42093905 | **DOI:** 10.2147/CIA.S605868

**The study:** Researchers reviewed medical records of 2,186 patients aged 60 and older at a Saudi Arabian university-affiliated primary care center following the 2023 Saudi Osteoporosis Society guideline recommending universal screening. Bone density screening was ordered for only 19.7% of eligible patients, and just 4.1% completed the test. Women, patients with prior fractures or osteoporosis, and those seen by female physicians were more likely to be screened.

**Why it matters:** Even when guidelines recommend universal bone density screening for older adults, implementation in real-world primary care settings faces significant barriers.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Don't assume your doctor will automatically order bone density testing. If you're 60 or older and haven't had a recent scan, ask whether one might be appropriate for you—and follow through with scheduling if it's recommended.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Primary care practices need system-level supports—such as automated reminders, patient outreach, and streamlined scheduling—to close the gap between screening recommendations and actual testing completion.

**Caveats:** Single center; Saudi Arabian healthcare context may differ from other settings; retrospective chart review.

### 10. New Ultrasound-Based Technology Tracks Bone Response to Osteoporosis Treatment Without Radiation

**Journal:** *Aging Clinical and Experimental Research* | **Published:** April 11, 2026
**PMID:** 41964812 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40520-026-03391-7

**The study:** Italian researchers used Radiofrequency Echographic Multi-Spectrometry (REMS)—a radiation-free ultrasound technique—to monitor hip bone density in 74 postmenopausal women starting romosozumab (a newer bone-building medication) and 52 untreated women. After just six months, REMS detected significant bone density increases of 3.7-4.7% at the hip in treated women, while untreated women showed no change. Treatment-naïve patients and those with multiple prior fractures showed the largest gains.

**Why it matters:** This radiation-free technology may allow more frequent bone density monitoring, helping patients and providers track treatment response without repeated X-ray exposure.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're on osteoporosis medication and curious whether it's working, ask your doctor about newer monitoring technologies that don't use radiation. More frequent feedback could help you and your healthcare team make timely treatment decisions.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living health clinics and geriatric practices might consider REMS technology as a way to monitor residents' bone health more frequently, particularly those on osteoporosis treatments or at high fracture risk.

**Caveats:** No control group receiving romosozumab without REMS; relatively small sample; REMS is not yet widely available; six-month follow-up is short-term.

### 11. Bisphosphonates Don't Delay Fracture Healing; Teriparatide May Speed It Up

**Journal:** *The Lancet Healthy Longevity* | **Published:** May 2026
**PMID:** 42229491 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.lanhl.2026.100827

**The study:** A systematic review and meta-analysis of 28 trials involving over 5,000 patients examined whether common osteoporosis medications affect fracture healing. Bisphosphonates (like alendronate and zoledronic acid) showed no significant effect on healing time, delayed union, or non-union rates compared to placebo or no treatment. Teriparatide (a bone-building medication) was associated with fractures healing about 3 weeks faster on average.

**Why it matters:** Older adults and their doctors sometimes hesitate to continue osteoporosis medications after a fracture, fearing interference with healing. This evidence suggests bisphosphonates can be safely continued, while teriparatide might actually help bones heal faster.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you break a bone and are already taking osteoporosis medication, don't assume you need to stop it. Ask your doctor—current evidence suggests most osteoporosis drugs don't interfere with healing and may even help.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Orthopedic surgeons and fracture care teams should be aware that bisphosphonate continuation appears safe after fracture, and teriparatide could be considered when faster healing is clinically important.

**Caveats:** Heterogeneous studies with varying medications, doses, and fracture types; teriparatide finding based on fewer studies; meta-analysis quality depends on underlying trial quality.

### 12. Long-Term Cancer Survivors Face Higher Osteoporosis and Physical Limitation Risks

**Journal:** *Journal of Geriatric Oncology* | **Published:** June 25, 2026
**PMID:** 42349049 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jgo.2026.103037

**The study:** Researchers analyzed data from 5,891 Irish adults aged 50 and older, comparing those diagnosed with cancer within the past 5 years, more than 5 years ago, or never. Recent cancer survivors were 81% more likely to report physical limitations than those without cancer history. Long-term survivors (5+ years) had triple the odds of severe cumulative physical limitations and double the risk of osteoporosis based on heel ultrasound measurements, even while living independently in the community.

**Why it matters:** Cancer survivors face elevated bone and functional health risks that persist years after treatment, yet may not receive adequate long-term monitoring and support.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you've survived cancer—even many years ago—ask your healthcare provider about bone density screening and discuss any physical limitations you're experiencing. These concerns deserve ongoing attention, not just during active cancer treatment.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living communities and primary care practices should recognize cancer survivorship as a long-term risk factor for osteoporosis and functional decline, warranting proactive screening and rehabilitation referrals.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional analysis (snapshot in time); Irish population; self-reported physical limitations; cancer treatments and types varied.

### 13. Biomarker Profiles Can Predict Chronic Disease Trajectories and Mortality Risk

**Journal:** *Age and Ageing* | **Published:** May 4, 2026
**PMID:** 42160766 | **DOI:** 10.1093/ageing/afag135

**The study:** Researchers analyzed 18 blood biomarkers representing immune, metabolic, and organ function in 4,961 Irish adults aged 50+ and validated findings in 3,914 Americans. They identified three biomarker "signatures" reflecting low, medium, and high biological risk. Over 8-12 years, the high-risk signature predicted 89% higher mortality and significantly higher rates of cardiovascular disease, diabetes, frailty, and disability. The signature also tracked with specific disease patterns including osteoporosis combined with thyroid and respiratory conditions.

**Why it matters:** A panel of routine blood tests may help identify older adults at greatest risk for multiple chronic conditions, potentially enabling earlier preventive interventions.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Standard blood tests may reveal more than you realize about your overall health trajectory. Ask your doctor to explain what your results might mean for your risk of future health problems—not just whether individual numbers are "normal."
- **About older adults and seniors:** Healthcare systems could consider using biomarker profiling to identify high-risk older adults who might benefit most from intensive preventive care, lifestyle interventions, or closer monitoring.

**Caveats:** Observational design; biomarker panels require specific laboratory testing; clinical utility of risk stratification needs further validation.

### 14. Diabetes, Prolonged Bed Rest, and Weak Back Muscles Predict Vertebral Collapse After Spine Fractures

**Journal:** *Clinical Interventions in Aging* | **Published:** May 15, 2026
**PMID:** 42165006 | **DOI:** 10.2147/CIA.S597128

**The study:** Researchers followed 626 patients aged 50+ with acute osteoporotic spine fractures treated conservatively (without surgery) to identify who developed Kümmell's disease—a serious complication where the fractured vertebra collapses further weeks to months later. Diabetes increased risk 40-fold, and bed rest exceeding two weeks increased risk nearly 10-fold. Protective factors included anti-osteoporosis medication, higher bone density, and greater back muscle mass measured on MRI. The resulting prediction tool achieved 94% accuracy.

**Why it matters:** After a spine fracture, prolonged bed rest—often thought to be protective—may actually increase the risk of serious complications, while early treatment and muscle preservation appear protective.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have a spine compression fracture, ask your doctor when it's safe to start moving again. Extended bed rest may do more harm than good, and starting osteoporosis medication promptly appears to help healing.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Skilled nursing facilities managing patients with vertebral fractures should balance rest with early mobilization, monitor diabetic patients closely, and ensure osteoporosis treatment is initiated promptly.

**Caveats:** Single-center retrospective study; Kümmell's disease diagnosis varies across studies; Chinese population; prediction model needs external validation.

### 15. Gut Bacteria Differ in Older Adults with Fragility Fractures and Severe Arthritis

**Journal:** *Mechanisms of Ageing and Development* | **Published:** June 10, 2026
**PMID:** 42269784 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.mad.2026.112210

**The study:** Italian researchers compared gut bacteria profiles in 13 elderly patients with fragility fractures (ages 71-90), 11 with severe osteoarthritis (ages 65-87), and 20 healthy older adults (ages 65-100). Both patient groups showed "dysbiotic" gut profiles with more potentially harmful bacteria and fewer beneficial species that produce short-chain fatty acids. Functional analysis revealed differences in pathways related to energy and amino acid metabolism.

**Why it matters:** The gut microbiome may play a role in skeletal health during aging, opening possibilities for future interventions targeting gut bacteria to support bone health.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Emerging research suggests gut health may influence bone health. While specific recommendations aren't yet available, maintaining a varied diet rich in fiber may support beneficial gut bacteria as scientists learn more about these connections.
- **About older adults and seniors:** This pilot study adds to evidence that the gut-bone connection deserves attention in geriatric care, though practical microbiome-based interventions for osteoporosis remain in early development.

**Caveats:** Very small sample sizes (pilot study); cross-sectional design cannot establish causation; dietary and medication differences may confound results.

### 16. Standing Up Powerfully May Indicate Bone Strength Better Than Grip Strength

**Journal:** *Experimental Gerontology* | **Published:** July 2, 2026
**PMID:** 42392462 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.exger.2026.113225

**The study:** In 58 community-dwelling adults aged 60-95, researchers compared how well leg power during a single sit-to-stand movement and grip strength related to bone parameters measured at the wrist using specialized CT scanning. Sit-to-stand power showed stronger associations with bone strength, geometry, and architecture than grip strength, particularly at the cortical (outer) bone site.

**Why it matters:** A simple chair-rise test measuring how powerfully someone can stand up might be a practical way to screen for bone health in community settings where bone density scanners aren't available.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** How easily and quickly you can rise from a chair may say something about your bone health. If standing up from sitting has become difficult, it's worth discussing with your healthcare provider as a potential signal to check your bone density.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior centers, fitness programs, and primary care offices could incorporate timed sit-to-stand tests as simple, no-cost indicators of musculoskeletal health that might flag individuals needing bone density evaluation.

**Caveats:** Small sample size (58 participants); cross-sectional design; wrist bone measurements may not reflect spine or hip; single community in California.

### 17. European Quality Improvement Project Boosts Osteoporosis Treatment After Hip Fractures

**Journal:** *European Geriatric Medicine* | **Published:** May 21, 2026
**PMID:** 42168731 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s41999-026-01497-9

**The study:** Geriatric medicine teams across six European countries (Portugal, Finland, Germany, Romania, Italy, and Malta) implemented a coordinated quality improvement initiative in their orthogeriatric units. The project focused on education for healthcare teams, patient engagement through informational materials, and standardized treatment protocols. The development of an osteoporosis treatment algorithm significantly increased medication prescription rates in Portugal and Finland, and new bone health and fall prevention clinics were established in Malta and Italy.

**Why it matters:** Practical, low-cost interventions can improve osteoporosis treatment rates after hip fractures—a critical gap since many fracture patients never receive bone-protective therapy.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or a family member has a hip fracture, ask the hospital team whether osteoporosis treatment is part of the discharge plan. Evidence-based medications can reduce the risk of another fracture, but they're often overlooked.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living facilities and hospitals can adapt these educational materials and standardized protocols to improve their own secondary fracture prevention programs without major resource investments.

**Caveats:** Quality improvement project, not a controlled trial; results varied by country; long-term fracture outcomes not yet measured.

### 18. Survey Reveals Inconsistent Practices for Managing Post-Infusion Reactions to IV Osteoporosis Medications

**Journal:** *Australasian Journal on Ageing* | **Published:** June 2026
**PMID:** 42289804 | **DOI:** 10.1111/ajag.70195

**The study:** Australian researchers surveyed 75 geriatricians, rheumatologists, and endocrinologists about how they manage acute phase responses—flu-like symptoms that can occur after intravenous bisphosphonate infusions for osteoporosis. While 88% used paracetamol and 53% used corticosteroids, prescribing patterns varied considerably. Only half of clinicians using paracetamol found it helpful, compared to 92% who found corticosteroids beneficial. One-quarter of respondents said concerns about these reactions influenced their prescribing decisions.

**Why it matters:** The lack of clear guidelines for managing infusion reactions may lead to suboptimal care and could discourage use of effective osteoporosis treatments.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're scheduled for an IV osteoporosis infusion and worried about flu-like symptoms afterward, ask your doctor what they recommend to manage any reactions. Different approaches exist, and discussing this beforehand can help you feel prepared.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living medical directors and infusion clinic nurses should develop consistent protocols for preventing and treating acute phase responses, potentially including corticosteroids for patients with prior severe reactions.

**Caveats:** Small pilot survey (75 respondents); Australian practice patterns; no randomized trials comparing prevention/treatment strategies.

### 19. Denosumab Improves Bone Density in Breast Cancer Patients on Aromatase Inhibitors

**Journal:** *Aging Clinical and Experimental Research* | **Published:** April 20, 2026
**PMID:** 42008054 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40520-026-03402-7

**The study:** Italian researchers monitored bone density using radiation-free REMS ultrasound in 364 postmenopausal breast cancer patients taking aromatase inhibitors (medications that block estrogen and accelerate bone loss). Patients receiving denosumab showed progressive bone density increases at the spine and hip over 18 months (+5.3% at spine, +4.6% at hip), while those without bone-protective treatment experienced ongoing bone loss (-3.9% at spine, -3.7% at hip).

**Why it matters:** Women taking aromatase inhibitors for breast cancer face accelerated bone loss, but denosumab can reverse this trajectory—and monitoring with radiation-free technology allows tracking response without repeated X-ray exposure.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're taking hormone-blocking medication for breast cancer, ask your oncologist about bone protection. Medications like denosumab can help maintain your bone density during treatment.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Oncologists and primary care providers should proactively assess bone health in breast cancer patients on aromatase inhibitors and consider early bone-protective therapy, particularly for those with additional osteoporosis risk factors.

**Caveats:** Retrospective study design; patients weren't randomized to treatment groups; REMS technology not yet widely available; Italian population.

### 20. Chinese Prescribing Patterns Show Shift Toward Denosumab, Away from Traditional Treatments

**Journal:** *Aging Clinical and Experimental Research* | **Published:** April 11, 2026
**PMID:** 41964816 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40520-026-03382-8

**The study:** Researchers analyzed prescriptions for 363,529 osteoporosis patients (average age 65, 73% female) in Eastern China from 2019-2024. Active vitamin D analogs were most commonly prescribed (72%), followed by bisphosphonates (22%), traditional Chinese medicine (15%), and denosumab (14%). Over the study period, denosumab use increased dramatically (from 3% to 22%), while vitamin D analogs, bisphosphonates, and traditional Chinese medicine declined. Most patients received single-drug therapy, with only 8% on combination treatment.

**Why it matters:** This large real-world analysis reveals how osteoporosis treatment patterns are evolving, with newer injectable options gaining ground while questions remain about the evidence base for some widely used treatments.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Osteoporosis treatment options continue to expand. If you haven't discussed your bone medication options recently, ask your doctor whether newer treatments might offer advantages for your situation.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Healthcare systems should ensure prescribing patterns align with evidence-based guidelines and monitor whether shifts toward newer, more expensive medications improve patient outcomes.

**Caveats:** Chinese healthcare system and prescribing patterns may differ from other countries; database study without clinical outcomes; traditional Chinese medicine evidence base varies.
