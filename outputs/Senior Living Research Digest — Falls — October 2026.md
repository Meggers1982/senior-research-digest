### 1. A Fall That Lands You in the ER May Be an Early Warning Sign of Seizures

**Journal:** *Neurology* | **Published:** 2026 Nov 10
**PMID:** 42848964 | **DOI:** 10.1212/WNL.0000000000218597

**The study:** Researchers analyzed emergency department and inpatient records from 11 US states (2016-2021), identifying 57,547 adults aged 18 and older treated for a first-time seizure, 7,307 of whom had at least one prior ER visit for a fall. Using each patient as their own control (comparing the period before the seizure with the same period one year earlier), they found falls were far more common in the weeks right before the seizure — odds were 2.6 times higher in the 7 days immediately prior, and the association was detectable as far back as 22 weeks. For people whose first seizure was status epilepticus, the odds in the prior week were 4.5 times higher.

**Why it matters:** Falls are often chalked up to clumsiness, weakness, or a slippery floor, but this suggests a meaningful subset may be unrecognized seizures — meaning an ER visit for a fall could be a missed chance to diagnose epilepsy months earlier.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have had an unexplained fall — especially one you cannot fully remember, or one preceded by a strange sensation, confusion, or a blank spell — it is worth describing those details precisely to your doctor rather than brushing it off as a trip. Ask whether anything beyond balance should be evaluated.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For caregivers and senior living staff: your incident reports matter. Documenting whether a resident was witnessed falling, whether there was loss of awareness, incontinence, confusion afterward, or no memory of the event gives clinicians the information needed to distinguish a mechanical fall from a possible seizure.

**Caveats:** Retrospective analysis of administrative billing codes; observational design cannot establish that the falls were caused by seizures. Mean age of the fall subgroup was 49.3 years, so findings are not specific to older adults. Coding errors and missed diagnoses are possible, and prospective studies are needed.

### 2. Sedatives Are Finally Fading After Hip Fractures — But Opioid Prescribing Has Barely Budged

**Journal:** *J Am Geriatr Soc* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 9
**PMID:** 42852585 | **DOI:** 10.1111/jgs.70739

**The study:** Using linked provincial health data, researchers tracked 114,055 Ontario adults aged 66 and older admitted for an acute hip fracture between 2008 and 2024, examining what was dispensed to them before hospitalization and in the year after discharge. Benzodiazepine dispensing fell sharply, from 33.7% in 2008 to 13.3% in 2024, and concurrent opioid-plus-benzodiazepine use dropped from 3.7% to 1%. Opioid dispensing, however, peaked at 55.8% in 2015 and still stood at 44.6% in 2024, with hydromorphone and combination products most common.

**Why it matters:** Both drug classes raise the risk of further falls, dependence, and death in older adults — so the fact that nearly half of hip fracture patients are still filling opioid prescriptions in the year after discharge is a flag for anyone recovering from, or caring for someone with, a broken hip.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you are recovering from a hip fracture, ask your prescriber directly: how long is this opioid meant to last, what is the plan to taper it, and what non-opioid pain options are available? Lingering opioid use can itself raise your risk of the next fall — but never stop a prescription abruptly on your own.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and senior living professionals: the transition home or to a facility after a hip fracture is where prescriptions quietly become permanent. A medication review at 4 to 12 weeks post-discharge, with explicit attention to opioids and any remaining sedatives, is a concrete intervention point.

**Caveats:** Observational analysis of dispensing records — it cannot show whether prescribing was appropriate for any individual, and dispensing does not prove the drug was taken. Single-province Canadian data under a public drug plan may not generalize to US or other systems. Analysis excluded people who died within roughly three months of discharge.

### 3. Hearing, Vision and Mood — Not Just Knees — Drive Fall Risk in People With Knee Arthritis

**Journal:** *Arch Gerontol Geriatr* | **Published:** 2026 Dec
**PMID:** 42759165 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.archger.2026.106410

**The study:** Researchers built machine learning models to predict a first fall among 1,097 adults with knee osteoarthritis in the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), using 28 demographic, health, and behavioral variables, then tested the final model in a separate hospital-based group. The best-performing model reached an AUC of 0.836, indicating good discrimination. Strikingly, the most influential predictors were not joint-specific measures but hearing and vision problems and depressive symptoms.

**Why it matters:** It suggests that for older adults with painful knees, the most modifiable fall risks may sit outside the orthopedic clinic — in an eye exam, a hearing test, or a conversation about low mood.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If knee arthritis is limiting you, do not let the knee absorb all the attention at your appointments. Current glasses prescriptions, a hearing check, and an honest discussion about depression or low mood are worth raising, because this research suggests they track closely with who falls first.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living staff and family caregivers: fall-risk screening for residents with arthritis may be incomplete if it only measures gait and strength. Logging sensory impairments and mood changes alongside mobility gives a fuller picture of who needs closer watching.

**Caveats:** Observational cohort data; the model predicts risk but does not prove that treating hearing, vision, or depression prevents falls. Chinese cohort — findings may not generalize to other populations or care systems. Retrospective design, and fall history in CHARLS is self-reported. External validation was at a single hospital.

### 4. Broken Wrists and Shoulders Rarely Get Flagged as Osteoporosis — Especially in Men

**Journal:** *Bone* | **Published:** 2026 Nov
**PMID:** 42448012 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.bone.2026.118014

**The study:** Austrian researchers analyzed nationwide hospital insurance records covering 1,705,684 patients aged 50-79 admitted between 2003 and 2014, mapping which diagnoses cluster around osteoporosis and whether osteoporosis was recorded when patients were hospitalized with fractures. Hip fractures were strongly linked to an osteoporosis diagnosis, particularly in women 60 and older — but forearm, wrist, hand, and upper-arm fractures showed no significant co-occurrence with osteoporosis coding in any age group, despite being classic early fragility fractures. Women had far broader networks of co-occurring conditions than men, while men's fracture networks at ages 50-69 skewed toward trauma- and alcohol-related diagnoses.

**Why it matters:** The wrist or shoulder fracture from a minor fall is often the first and best warning that bones are thinning — and these data suggest that warning is routinely being missed, leaving people unprotected against the hip fracture that may follow.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have broken a wrist, arm, or shoulder after a fall from standing height, ask explicitly whether you should be assessed for osteoporosis and fall risk. Do not assume the fracture clinic has already connected those dots — this research suggests it frequently does not, and the gap is widest for men and for people in their 50s and 60s.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and care professionals: treat any low-trauma fracture — not just a hip — as a trigger to request a bone density scan, medication review, and falls assessment. Coordinated Fracture Liaison Service programs exist precisely to close this gap and are worth asking your local hospital about.

**Caveats:** Retrospective analysis of administrative coding data, which reflects documentation practices rather than confirmed clinical status; under-coding may partly be a recording artifact rather than missed diagnosis. Austrian data from 2003-2014 may not reflect current practice elsewhere. Age limited to 50-79; inpatients only, so outpatient-managed fractures are excluded.

### 5. Bed and Chair Alarms Don't Prevent Falls — So Why Are Hospitals Still Using Them?

**Journal:** *Clin Geriatr Med* | **Published:** 2026 Nov
**PMID:** 42833806 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.cger.2026.06.002

**The study:** A team of nursing and geriatrics researchers reviewed the evidence base for fall prevention alarms in hospitals alongside other fall prevention practices, examining why alarms remain so widely used despite weak supporting evidence. The review draws on low-value care and de-implementation science to explain the persistence of the practice and describes strategies currently being tested to phase alarms out.

**Why it matters:** Alarms are noisy, disrupt sleep, can discourage patients from moving, and contribute to alarm fatigue among staff — yet many patients and families still assume they are a sign of good safety care.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or a loved one is hospitalized and a bed or chair alarm is switched on, you can ask what evidence supports it and what else is being done — supervised walking, a toileting schedule, a medication review, good lighting, and proper footwear have better support than an alarm does.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living operators and nursing leaders: this review frames alarm reduction as a de-implementation project, not just a policy change — requiring staff reassurance, family communication, and substitution with purposeful rounding and mobility programs rather than simply switching devices off.

**Caveats:** Narrative review rather than new primary data; no pooled effect estimates or systematic search methodology reported in the abstract. Findings concern hospital settings and may not translate directly to long-term care or home environments.

### 6. How Fast You Walk in Your 50s May Predict Falls Decades Later

**Journal:** *JMIR Aging* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 6
**PMID:** 42837653 | **DOI:** 10.2196/103917

**The study:** Researchers analyzed data from 309 independently living adults aged 45 to 64 in the Midlife in the United States (MIDUS) cohort, who completed walking and strength tests between 2004 and 2009 and were followed up between 2013 and 2021. About 20% reported recurrent falls (two or more in the past year) at follow-up. Slower gait speed in midlife was associated with higher odds of recurrent falls later (odds ratio 1.43), while grip strength, sit-to-stand time and calculated leg power were not. Notably, muscle performance declined over the follow-up period while gait speed stayed relatively stable.

**Why it matters:** It suggests that vulnerability to falls can be spotted long before old age, using a simple timed walk rather than expensive testing — opening a much wider window for prevention.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** A timed walk is one of the cheapest health checks there is. If you're in midlife or beyond, consider asking your clinician to time your usual walking pace at routine visits and track it over the years — a slowing trend is worth discussing, and walking, balance and strength programs are low-risk ways to respond.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living and wellness staff: gait speed is an easy, repeatable metric to build into resident check-ins and into programming for younger residents and independent-living populations, where fall prevention is often assumed to be unnecessary.

**Caveats:** Observational design — cannot establish that slow walking causes falls; modest sample (N=309); falls were self-reported; the authors describe the analysis as exploratory and call for confirmation.

### 7. Which Falls End in a Head Injury — and the Movements That Seem to Protect You

**Journal:** *J Am Med Dir Assoc* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 5
**PMID:** 42832847 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jamda.2026.106493

**The study:** Researchers screened 2,921 studies and synthesized the 13 that examined what makes a fall more likely to involve a head impact in adults aged 60 and older. Most of the evidence came from video-recorded real falls in long-term care. Female sex and falls that started and landed in a forward direction were consistently linked to higher odds of hitting the head, while rotating the body away from the initial fall direction and using the arms during descent were associated with lower odds. One randomized trial found older adults could be taught protective movements that reduced head impact during experimentally induced falls.

**Why it matters:** Head impacts drive the most serious fall outcomes, including traumatic brain injury and death, and this review points to a small set of potentially trainable behaviors and environmental changes rather than treating head injury as pure bad luck.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Most fall-prevention advice focuses on not falling at all; this research suggests how you fall also matters. Ask a physical therapist whether 'safe landing' or protective-response training is appropriate for you, and pay particular attention to forward-trip hazards — raised thresholds, cords, loose rugs — since forward falls carry the highest head-impact risk.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For long-term care operators: the evidence base here comes largely from your settings' own fall cameras. Consider whether flooring, furniture edges, hip and head protection options, and staff training in protective-response coaching deserve a place alongside standard fall-risk screening.

**Caveats:** Scoping review of only 13 studies, mostly observational video analyses from long-term care with just one interventional trial; findings may not generalize to community-dwelling older adults; most identified risk factors (sex, fall direction) are not modifiable.

### 8. Frailty, Not Muscle Loss, Was the Stronger Warning Sign for Falls in a 6-Year Study

**Journal:** *Arch Gerontol Geriatr* | **Published:** 2026 Nov
**PMID:** 42600484 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.archger.2026.106369

**The study:** Investigators followed 2,067 community-dwelling older adults in the Korean Frailty and Aging Cohort Study, assessing disability, falls and mobility every two years for six years. Physical frailty alone was associated with increased risk of falls (hazard ratio 1.60), basic daily-activity disability, instrumental activity disability and severe mobility limitation. Sarcopenia (low muscle mass and strength) on its own showed no significant association with any of these outcomes.

**Why it matters:** It argues that frailty screening — which captures slowness, weakness, exhaustion, low activity and weight loss — may be more useful than muscle measurement alone for identifying who is heading toward falls and lost independence.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you've been told you have low muscle mass, that alone may not be the whole picture. Ask your clinician whether you've been screened for frailty — things like unintentional weight loss, exhaustion, slow walking and low activity — because that combination tracked more closely with falls and disability in this study.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For care teams and assessors: this supports prioritizing a frailty phenotype assessment in routine intake and annual review, rather than relying on body composition or grip strength alone to flag residents and clients at risk of falls and functional decline.

**Caveats:** Observational cohort — cannot establish causation; conducted entirely in a Korean population, so findings may not generalize elsewhere; falls assessed only every two years, relying on recall; the combined frailty-plus-sarcopenia group was smaller, which may explain some null results.

### 9. Uneven Ground Is Where Walking Gets Risky — and Brain Scans Show Why

**Journal:** *Neurobiol Aging* | **Published:** 2026 Dec
**PMID:** 42679689 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.neurobiolaging.2026.08.004

**The study:** Researchers had 22 young adults (average age 22) and 86 older adults (average age 75) undergo brain MRI and then walk on a treadmill across flat and three grades of uneven terrain. Older adults were split into higher- and lower-functioning groups using the Short Physical Performance Battery. Lower-functioning older adults showed more step-to-step variability at baseline and a steeper rise in variability as the terrain got bumpier, and their gait variability was more strongly tied to a brain imaging marker of fluid accumulation in white matter, particularly in the corpus callosum.

**Why it matters:** It helps explain why some older adults manage sidewalks, lawns and gravel paths easily while others destabilize quickly, and supports using a simple in-office physical performance test to identify who needs extra caution on uneven ground.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Flat hallways can mask a problem that shows up on grass, gravel and cracked sidewalks. If you notice your steps becoming inconsistent on uneven ground, that's worth raising with your clinician — and ask about the Short Physical Performance Battery, a quick test of balance, walking speed and chair rises used here to separate higher- from lower-functioning walkers.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and senior living grounds managers: outdoor paths, thresholds and garden surfaces deserve the same scrutiny as indoor hazards, and residents with lower physical performance scores may benefit from supervised practice or route planning on even surfaces rather than being discouraged from going outside altogether.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional and observational — cannot establish causation or predict actual falls; modest sample, with only 22 young adults as comparison; treadmill terrain is a laboratory simulation of real-world walking; single site.

### 10. Meals Plus Exercise at Home: What Homebound Older Adults Said Was Missing

**Journal:** *J Am Geriatr Soc* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 8
**PMID:** 42847733 | **DOI:** 10.1111/jgs.70780

**The study:** This qualitative analysis drew on biweekly and exit survey comments from 101 homebound older adults (mean age 74) in the 'Strength on Wheels' randomized trial, which paired Meals on Wheels deliveries with a 12-week home-based exercise program. Participants generally enjoyed the meals and engaged with exercise materials, but many reported ongoing pain and fear of falling. Reactions to a commercial activity tracker were mixed — some found it motivating, others struggled to charge it, interpret its icons, or keep track of it. Participants said social contact, cognitive activities, physical therapy, transportation and home modifications also mattered for staying independent.

**Why it matters:** It shows that nutrition-plus-exercise packages for homebound seniors can fall short unless they also address pain, fear of falling and practical home supports — and that wearable devices are not automatically helpful for this group.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If pain or fear of falling is holding you back from a home exercise program, say so — it's one of the most common barriers participants raised. Ask whether a physical therapy referral, home safety assessment or grab-bar installation should come before or alongside the exercise plan.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For meal-delivery programs and home care agencies: this is a practical blueprint for what to bundle next — pain management, fall-safety home modifications, transportation and social connection — and a caution that handing out activity trackers without hands-on setup and support may frustrate more than it motivates.

**Caveats:** Qualitative analysis of free-text survey responses, not a controlled comparison; the parent randomized trial found no improvement in physical measures (only loneliness improved in the exercise group); self-reported experiences; sample of 101 drawn from a single program and region may not generalize.

### 11. Visual Hallucinations From Sight Loss Can Cause Falls — and Few Clinicians Are Looking for Them

**Journal:** *Clin Geriatr Med* | **Published:** 2026 Nov
**PMID:** 42833804 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.cger.2026.08.004

**The study:** This clinical review examines Charles Bonnet Syndrome (CBS), in which people with declining vision experience visual hallucinations — seeing patterns, figures or objects that aren't there — despite intact cognition. The authors argue that CBS has historically been framed as a psychological issue, but emerging evidence links it to physical consequences, including causing falls and increasing susceptibility to them. They note awareness of CBS is low among both sight-loss and falls-prevention specialists, and call for falls prevention approaches that span primary care, specialist and community services.

**Why it matters:** Older adults with macular degeneration, glaucoma or other vision loss often stay silent about hallucinations for fear of being labeled as having dementia — and that silence may be hiding a correctable contributor to falls.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have significant vision loss and see things you know aren't real, you are not losing your mind — this is a recognized condition tied to sight loss, and it may affect your risk of falling. Tell your eye specialist or doctor, and ask that it be factored into your falls risk assessment.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For caregivers and senior living staff: asking residents with vision impairment directly and nonjudgmentally about hallucinations can surface a falls risk factor that routine screening tools miss entirely, and reassure someone who has been frightened into silence.

**Caveats:** Narrative clinical review, not original research — presents no new data, effect sizes or quantified fall risk; the CBS–falls link is described as emerging and warrants confirmation in primary studies.

### 12. Hospital Fall Risk Changes by the Day — and a One-Time Admission Check May Miss It

**Journal:** *Eur Geriatr Med* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 7
**PMID:** 42842205 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s41999-026-01622-8

**The study:** Researchers analyzed electronic health records from two Japanese tertiary hospitals covering 112,103 patients aged 65 to 106, re-assessing them on hospital day 3 (83,297 patients) and day 7 (61,071 patients) and tracking first falls over the following 30 days. Patients whose ability to transfer in and out of bed stayed impaired or got worse had higher fall risk at both checkpoints, and a sudden functional decline predicted falls by day 7. Falling hemoglobin and falling serum sodium were also independently linked to higher fall risk, while albumin changes were not.

**Why it matters:** Most hospitals score fall risk once, at admission, but this large cohort suggests vulnerability evolves over the first week — meaning a patient judged 'low risk' on day one can become high risk by day three without anyone rechecking.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or a loved one is admitted to the hospital, ask the nursing team how often fall risk is reassessed, and tell them right away if moving from bed to chair has become harder than it was on admission — that change itself may signal rising risk.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For hospital and post-acute care teams, this supports building day-3 and day-7 re-screening into the workflow and flagging drops in hemoglobin or sodium as mobility-safety signals, not just lab abnormalities to be corrected in isolation.

**Caveats:** Retrospective observational design using administrative and chart data — associations only, not causation; conducted in two tertiary hospitals in Japan, so staffing patterns and patient mix may not generalize to US or European settings; falls captured only if documented.

### 13. "It's Not for Me": Why Older Adults With Disabilities Avoid Fall Prevention Classes

**Journal:** *Disabil Rehabil* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 5
**PMID:** 42831317 | **DOI:** 10.1080/09638288.2026.2740573

**The study:** Canadian researchers interviewed 11 adults aged 50 and older (mean age 69.8) who reported a mobility, vision, or hearing impairment about what keeps them out of community fall prevention exercise programs. Participants described accessibility as far more than ramps — physical, sensory, transportation, and cost barriers all compounded each other. The dominant theme was identity: people said the programs are perceived as being for 'weak' or frail people, and they did not see themselves that way, even as their own function was changing.

**Why it matters:** Fall prevention exercise programs work, but only for people who walk through the door — and this study suggests stigma and branding, not just logistics, are turning away exactly the people at highest risk.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If the words 'fall prevention class' make you bristle, you are not alone — consider looking for strength, balance, or mobility classes framed around staying independent, and ask program coordinators directly about seating options, large-print or audio materials, transportation help, and sliding-scale fees.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living and community program staff, the practical takeaway is to rename and co-design offerings with residents who have disabilities, rather than assuming low turnout reflects low interest; marketing language and the perceived identity of a class may matter as much as the exercise content.

**Caveats:** Small qualitative sample (N=11), single region in Canada, interviews conducted virtually which may exclude those with limited technology access; findings describe perspectives and are not designed to measure program effectiveness.

### 14. After a Wrist or Shoulder Fracture, Fear of Falling Often Goes Untreated

**Journal:** *J Geriatr Phys Ther* | **Published:** 2026 Oct-Dec 01
**PMID:** 42358089 | **DOI:** 10.1519/JPT.0000000000000507

**The study:** Researchers in Ontario conducted in-depth interviews with 13 older adults (mean age 67.3) who had osteoporosis and a recent upper extremity fragility fracture. Participants described both helpful adaptations and harmful ones — cutting back on activities out of fear of another fall — and reported limited awareness that osteoporosis itself relates to fall and fracture risk. Many described gaps in education, psychological support, and access to rehabilitation that addressed the emotional side of falling.

**Why it matters:** A broken wrist or shoulder is often treated as an orthopedic event and then closed out, but these accounts suggest the lasting damage can be a fear-driven retreat from activity that accelerates deconditioning and future fall risk.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If a fall left you with a broken wrist, arm, or shoulder, ask your doctor two questions beyond the cast: should I be assessed and treated for osteoporosis, and can I be referred to rehabilitation that addresses both balance and the anxiety about falling again?
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and care staff, watch for quiet activity withdrawal in the months after a fracture — skipping walks, declining outings, avoiding stairs — and raise it with the care team as a treatable problem rather than an understandable reaction.

**Caveats:** Small qualitative study (N=13), single hospital in Canada, self-reported experiences; not designed to quantify how common fear of falling or care gaps are in the broader population.

### 15. Persistent Depression in Later Life Predicts Faster Decline Into Frailty

**Journal:** *J Affect Disord* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 1
**PMID:** 42822543 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jad.2026.122574

**The study:** Analyzing 57,894 participants across four major aging cohorts (HRS in the US, ELSA in England, SHARE in Europe, and CHARLS in China), researchers sorted older adults into five depression trajectories based on symptoms at their first four assessments: persistently healthy, remitting, late-onset, fluctuating, and persistent high burden. The persistent high burden group — 7.9% to 20.3% of participants depending on the cohort — accumulated frailty significantly faster over subsequent years and had elevated odds of becoming frail (odds ratios 1.29 to 2.75). Lower education consistently predicted belonging to a worse depression trajectory.

**Why it matters:** Frailty is the pathway to falls, fractures, and loss of independence, and these data suggest that chronically untreated depressive symptoms — not a single bad patch — are a measurable driver of that slide.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If low mood, low energy, or loss of interest has been with you for years rather than weeks, treat it as a physical health issue as well as an emotional one, and ask your clinician about sustained treatment and follow-up rather than accepting it as a normal part of aging.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and care communities, the signal to act on is duration: someone whose depressive symptoms persist across repeated check-ins warrants escalation and ongoing monitoring, since this group — not the briefly low — showed accelerated frailty.

**Caveats:** Observational cohort study — cannot establish that treating depression prevents frailty; depressive symptoms measured by self-report screening scales (CES-D, EURO-D) rather than clinical diagnosis; depression trajectory classification was rule-based and definitions of frailty index components vary across cohorts.

### 16. A New National Blueprint Says Falls Prevention Can't Be Left to Doctors Alone

**Journal:** *Clin Geriatr Med* | **Published:** 2026 Nov
**PMID:** 42833801 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.cger.2026.08.001

**The study:** Authors from the National Council on Aging describe the 2025 National Falls Prevention Action Plan, developed from extensive surveys of professionals and a nationwide summit drawing input from hundreds of people across aging services, disability, housing, government, technology, research, and advocacy. The article lays out the plan's strategies and how clinicians can embed them in practice and link patients to community organizations.

**Why it matters:** It reframes falls as a housing, transportation, and community-services problem as much as a medical one, giving families and providers a shared roadmap and specific referral pathways rather than generic advice to 'be careful.'

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Ask your primary care provider whether they screen for falls annually and whether they can refer you to an evidence-based community program — the national plan is built on the assumption that these clinical-to-community handoffs happen, and you can prompt one.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living operators and community agencies, the plan offers an argument and a framework for cross-sector partnerships — with housing, public health, and technology vendors — that individual organizations typically cannot build alone.

**Caveats:** This is a descriptive policy article, not original research; recommendations reflect expert and stakeholder consensus rather than new outcome data, and the plan is US-specific.

### 17. A Six-Part Health Check Predicts Who Lands in the ER After a Fall

**Journal:** *Exp Gerontol* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 1
**PMID:** 42575258 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.exger.2026.113274

**The study:** Researchers linked Taiwan's national ICOPE screening database to insurance claims and death records to follow 244,899 adults aged 65 and older through 2022-2023. Each person was scored on six domains of "intrinsic capacity" — cognition, mobility, vitality, vision, hearing and mood — and tracked for emergency department visits caused by falls. Problems with mobility (58% higher rate), cognition (23%) and vision (20%) each independently predicted fall-related ER visits, and risk climbed step by step as more domains were impaired. The single highest-risk profile combined physical and cognitive decline with sensory loss (87% higher rate).

**Why it matters:** It shows that a short, non-invasive screening of six everyday capacities can flag who is heading toward a fall serious enough to need emergency care — well before the fall happens.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have more than one age-related change stacking up — slower walking plus memory lapses plus fading eyesight — the combination may matter more than any one problem alone. It is worth asking your primary care provider for a full check across mobility, thinking, vision, hearing, nutrition and mood rather than addressing each in isolation.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living and primary care teams, this large cohort supports building multi-domain screening into routine assessment and prioritizing residents whose profile mixes physical, cognitive and sensory decline — the group with nearly double the rate of fall-related emergency visits.

**Caveats:** Observational cohort — identifies associations, not cause and effect; follow-up was short (about two years); only falls serious enough to reach an emergency department were counted, so everyday falls were missed; funded by Taiwan's Health Promotion Administration, Ministry of Health and Welfare; findings come from a Taiwanese screening program and may not fully generalize elsewhere.

### 18. The Warning Signs Show Up Before Frailty Does

**Journal:** *J Geriatr Phys Ther* | **Published:** 2026 Oct-Dec 01
**PMID:** 42138269 | **DOI:** 10.1519/JPT.0000000000000499

**The study:** Brazilian researchers put 159 community-dwelling older adults through a battery of physical tests — handheld strength testing, the Functional Gait Assessment, single-leg stance, a five-time sit-to-stand test, a forward compensatory stepping task, and a fear-of-falling questionnaire — and compared results across people classified as robust, prefrail or frail. Frail participants were weaker and worse on every measure, and reported more falls in the past year and more fear of falling. Notably, prefrail adults — people not yet classified as frail — already showed significant declines in leg strength, balance, walking stability, sit-to-stand ability and reactive stepping.

**Why it matters:** The "prefrail" stage is not a neutral waiting room: measurable losses in the exact abilities that prevent a fall are already underway, which makes it a practical window for intervention.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** You don't have to feel frail to be losing the abilities that catch you when you stumble. Simple benchmarks — how many times you can rise from a chair in a set period, whether you can balance on one leg, whether you can take a quick corrective step — are worth asking a physical therapist to measure, even if you consider yourself in good shape.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For therapists and wellness staff, the findings argue for assessing reactive balance (the ability to step and recover) and not just static balance, and for extending strength and balance programming to residents who look "fine but slowing" rather than waiting for a frailty diagnosis.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design — cannot establish that these declines cause falls or that fixing them prevents falls; modest sample size (N=159) from a single Brazilian setting; fall history was self-reported and retrospective.

### 19. Can Imagining Movement Improve Balance? The Evidence Is Thin but Intriguing

**Journal:** *Exp Gerontol* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 1
**PMID:** 42501905 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.exger.2026.113253

**The study:** A scoping review searched six databases for studies testing "action simulation" — mentally rehearsing movement (motor imagery) or watching someone else move (action observation) — as a way to improve balance, gait and mobility in healthy adults aged 60 and over. Nineteen studies covering 587 participants were found, most of them randomized controlled trials, with motor imagery the most common approach. Results varied widely across studies and outcome measures, only one study looked beyond the end of the program (one month later), and few reported whether older adults actually found the techniques acceptable or practical.

**Why it matters:** Mental practice could in principle help people who can't yet do much physical exercise, but right now the research base is too scattered and short-term to recommend it as a substitute for proven strength and balance training.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you see mental-rehearsal or video-based "balance training" marketed to you, treat it as an unproven add-on rather than a replacement for supervised strength and balance exercise, which has strong evidence behind it. Ask any provider offering it what outcomes they measure and over what period.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For rehab and activities professionals, this is a signal to watch rather than adopt: if you pilot imagery or observation techniques with residents who cannot tolerate physical practice, document acceptability and dropout, because that is precisely the evidence the field is missing.

**Caveats:** Scoping review of heterogeneous studies rather than a pooled analysis of effects; included trials were mostly small, with varied outcomes and almost no long-term follow-up; participants were healthy community-dwelling older adults, so findings may not apply to frail or cognitively impaired people.

### 20. A Subtle Change in Stride Flags Stroke Survivors Who Are About to Stumble

**Journal:** *Gait Posture* | **Published:** 2027 Jan
**PMID:** 42696957 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.gaitpost.2026.110627

**The study:** Japanese researchers analyzed the walking of 157 people recovering from stroke, 82 of whom experienced a near-fall during gait testing and 75 of whom did not. After accounting for cane use, braces, motor recovery and time since stroke, two features stood out: a reduced trailing limb angle (how far the back leg extends behind the body to push off) and greater trunk rotation while walking. Trailing limb angle alone distinguished near-fallers quite well, with an optimal cutoff around 3.26 degrees.

**Why it matters:** Rather than relying on a patient's memory of past falls, clinicians may be able to spot instability in real time from how a person pushes off and how much the torso twists — pointing rehabilitation toward push-off power and trunk control.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you are recovering from a stroke, ask your physical therapist whether your push-off and trunk control are being assessed, not just your walking speed — these mechanics appear closely tied to near-stumbles, and near-stumbles are a signal worth reporting rather than brushing off.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For caregivers and rehab staff, near-falls deserve the same documentation as actual falls; this study suggests they reflect measurable gait mechanics and may give an earlier warning than fall history alone.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional, observational design — cannot prove that improving trailing limb angle prevents falls; near-falls were captured during supervised gait testing rather than daily life; participants were subacute stroke survivors at Japanese rehabilitation settings, so findings may not generalize to community-dwelling older adults without stroke; conducted at a limited number of centers.

### 21. Newer Sleep Drugs Don't Appear to Raise Fall Risk — But the Evidence Is Still Thin

**Journal:** *J Am Geriatr Soc* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 26
**PMID:** 42799573 | **DOI:** 10.1111/jgs.70745

**The study:** Researchers pooled 19 studies (10 randomized trials and 9 observational studies, 183,595 participants total) comparing people who took dual orexin receptor antagonists (DORAs) — a newer class of insomnia medication that includes drugs like suvorexant and lemborexant — with people who did not. Overall, DORA use was not linked to more falls (OR 0.76, 95% CI 0.56–1.04), and randomized trial data actually suggested fewer falls (OR 0.59). There was no association with fractures. The authors caution that fall and fracture events were rare, the pooled data fell short of the number of events needed for a confident conclusion, and data specific to older adults were limited.

**Why it matters:** Traditional sleeping pills such as benzodiazepines and 'Z-drugs' are a well-documented cause of falls and hip fractures in older adults, so knowing whether a newer alternative carries the same hazard directly affects how insomnia should be treated after age 65.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you take a sleeping pill and have fallen or worry about falling, this is a good reason to ask your doctor or pharmacist what class of drug you are on and whether a newer option might be safer for you — but don't stop or switch any sleep medication on your own, and remember the evidence here is still preliminary.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living staff and family caregivers, this adds nuance to blanket 'sedatives cause falls' policies: medication reviews should distinguish between drug classes rather than treating all sleep aids as equally risky, while still prioritizing non-drug sleep strategies.

**Caveats:** Meta-analysis mixing randomized and observational studies with inconsistent results between them; low event rates and failure to reach the optimal information size, meaning the findings could change with more data; limited data specifically in older adults, the group most at risk.

### 22. A Five-Question Checklist Predicts Who Will Recover Well After a Hip Fracture — Better Than Grip Strength

**Journal:** *J Am Med Dir Assoc* | **Published:** 2026 Oct
**PMID:** 42696807 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jamda.2026.106450

**The study:** Finnish researchers followed 430 adults aged 70 and older for one year after hip fracture surgery at a single university hospital, comparing three ways of screening for muscle loss: handgrip strength, calf circumference, and the SARC-F questionnaire (five questions about strength, walking, rising from a chair, climbing stairs, and falls). Only SARC-F consistently predicted outcomes — those flagged by it were far less likely to get moving in the first days after surgery (odds of impaired mobilization rose to 7.6-fold by day 3) and had worse walking ability and greater functional decline at four months. Notably, neither group showed meaningful functional improvement between 4 and 12 months, suggesting recovery largely plateaus after the first few months.

**Why it matters:** A free, five-question survey that takes a minute to complete can identify which hip fracture patients are headed for a poor recovery — and the finding that recovery stalls after four months argues for concentrating rehabilitation effort early.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or someone close to you is heading into hip fracture surgery, ask whether the team has screened you with SARC-F and what the plan is for intensive rehabilitation in the first four months — that appears to be the window when most recovery happens.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For rehab staff and senior living operators, SARC-F requires no equipment and could be used at admission to triage rehab intensity; the plateau after four months is also a sobering planning point for setting realistic family expectations about long-term care needs.

**Caveats:** Single-center study in Finland, so results may not generalize to other health systems or more diverse populations; observational design cannot prove that acting on SARC-F scores changes outcomes; one author has served on an Amgen advisory board.

### 23. Nursing Home Residents Who Just Broke a Bone Are Still Loaded With Fall-Risk Drugs — and Rarely Get Bone Treatment

**Journal:** *J Am Med Dir Assoc* | **Published:** 2026 Oct
**PMID:** 42632250 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jamda.2026.106430

**The study:** Researchers analyzed Medicare prescription data for 192,118 nursing home residents across 14,559 US facilities who had recently sustained a hip or other fracture (mean age 83.8; 75% female). Nearly three-quarters (72.8%) were taking at least one drug known to raise the risk of injurious falls — most commonly statins (31%), opioids (27%), newer anticonvulsants (22%), and loop diuretics (22%) — while only 4.2% had a prescription for an oral bisphosphonate to protect their bones. Osteoporosis was formally diagnosed in just 18.8% of residents despite all having had a fracture.

**Why it matters:** A fracture is the single strongest warning sign of another fracture to come, yet this snapshot shows the post-fracture period in nursing homes is routinely missing both halves of prevention: reducing fall-causing medications and treating fragile bones.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have broken a bone, that fracture itself is a reason to request two specific conversations: a full review of every medication that might make you dizzy or unsteady, and an honest discussion about whether bone-protecting treatment is right for you.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and long-term care professionals, a recent fracture should automatically trigger a structured medication review — opioids and newer anticonvulsants stand out as the most promising deprescribing targets — alongside an osteoporosis assessment, rather than treating the fracture as a closed episode.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional observational design that describes prescribing patterns but cannot establish causation or measure subsequent fracture outcomes; limited to Medicare fee-for-service Part D enrollees and heavily non-Hispanic White (86.5%); two authors receive royalties from an UpToDate chapter on falls, and one has received prior Sanofi funding.

### 24. Singapore Launches a 2,300-Person Study Using Wearables and Virtual Reality to Predict Who Will Fall

**Journal:** *JMIR Aging* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 25
**PMID:** 42789327 | **DOI:** 10.2196/89285

**The study:** The TARGET study enrolled 2,291 community-dwelling Singapore residents aged 60 and older between 2022 and 2024 for a comprehensive fall risk assessment combining home interviews, wearable motion sensors to analyze gait, bone density scans with 3D body scans, and a virtual reality test of spatial navigation. At baseline, 15% reported a fall in the past year; those fallers already showed poorer physical and psychosocial health, greater gait variability, and higher fracture risk than non-fallers. Participants are being followed for two years with linkage to national medical records to build and test new fall prediction models.

**Why it matters:** Current fall-risk screening tools miss many people who go on to fall, and this study is testing whether low-cost technology administered by non-clinical staff could make community-wide screening both more accurate and affordable.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Subtle variability in how you walk — not just obvious weakness — showed up as a marker distinguishing fallers from non-fallers, so it's worth mentioning any change in your walking rhythm, balance, or confidence to your doctor even if you haven't fallen yet.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living operators watching the fall-tech market, this study is a reminder that wearable gait sensors and VR assessments are still being validated; the two-year outcome data, not the baseline results, will tell you whether they outperform standard screening.

**Caveats:** This is a design-and-baseline paper — no predictive accuracy results yet; the cohort is relatively healthy and fully independent (83%), limiting generalizability to frailer populations; cross-sectional baseline comparisons cannot establish causation; Singapore-specific population.

### 25. Fear of Falling Affects Half of Older Adults — and It Can Cut Both Ways

**Journal:** *Nurs Older People* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 1
**PMID:** 42487554 | **DOI:** 10.7748/nop.2026.e1560

**The study:** This clinical review summarizes evidence on concerns about falling, which may affect up to half of people aged 60 and over. For some, that worry is productive — it motivates them to adopt prevention strategies and actually lowers their fall risk. For others, it triggers a downward spiral of lost confidence, reduced activity, and physical deconditioning that paradoxically raises fall risk and erodes quality of life. Guidelines recommend assessing fear of falling as part of every fall risk assessment, with multidisciplinary management addressing both physical and psychological factors.

**Why it matters:** Fear of falling is rarely asked about directly, yet withdrawing from activity because of it is one of the fastest routes to the weakness that causes real falls.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If worry about falling has led you to stop going out, skip stairs, or avoid activities you used to enjoy, say so at your next appointment — that avoidance itself is treatable and is making you weaker, not safer.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For caregivers and care home staff, well-meant restrictions on a resident's movement can reinforce the fear-deconditioning cycle; asking directly about confidence, and addressing it alongside physical strength, belongs in every fall risk assessment.

**Caveats:** This is a narrative clinical review rather than new original research, so it reports no new data and the prevalence figures are drawn from varied underlying studies.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**

- **Bone health and falls are still run as separate services.** "Broken Wrists and Shoulders Rarely Get Flagged as Osteoporosis — Especially in Men" (PMID 42448012) carries over from the previous digest's version of the same analysis (PMID 42448012), and is now joined by two new data points: "Nursing Home Residents Who Just Broke a Bone Are Still Loaded With Fall-Risk Drugs — and Rarely Get Bone Treatment" (PMID 42632250), where only 4.2% had a bisphosphonate prescription, and "After a Wrist or Shoulder Fracture, Fear of Falling Often Goes Untreated" (PMID 42358089), where patients did not know osteoporosis related to their fracture at all. Together they extend the previous digest's FRAXplus analysis (PMID 42689245) and Parkinson's bone-health prompt audit (PMID 42723140).
- **Fall risk stacks across domains rather than sitting in the legs.** "A Six-Part Health Check Predicts Who Lands in the ER After a Fall" (PMID 42575258) and "Hearing, Vision and Mood — Not Just Knees — Drive Fall Risk in People With Knee Arthritis" (PMID 42759165) both recur from the previous digest, and the new "Visual Hallucinations From Sight Loss Can Cause Falls" (PMID 42833804) and "Persistent Depression in Later Life Predicts Faster Decline Into Frailty" (PMID 42822543) add sensory and mood pathways to the same picture the previous digest drew with "Falls, Depression and Untreated Hearing Loss Track With Dementia" (PMID 42731172).
- **Frailty and simple questions beat equipment.** "Frailty, Not Muscle Loss, Was the Stronger Warning Sign for Falls in a 6-Year Study" (PMID 42600484) and "A Five-Part Health Check..." — more precisely, "A Five-Question Checklist Predicts Who Will Recover Well After a Hip Fracture — Better Than Grip Strength" (PMID 42696807) — both repeat from the previous digest, and "The Warning Signs Show Up Before Frailty Does" (PMID 42138269) now shows the measurable losses are already present at the prefrail stage.
- **Fear of falling as a clinical variable, not a mood.** "Fear of Falling Affects Half of Older Adults — and It Can Cut Both Ways" (PMID 42487554), PMID 42358089 and "Meals Plus Exercise at Home: What Homebound Older Adults Said Was Missing" (PMID 42847733) all land on the same point as the previous digest's "Worry About Falling Plus Slow Walking" (PMID 42708074) and "Fear of falling before hip surgery predicted who survived it" (PMID 42771096).

**Advances**

- **The prediction window moves decades earlier.** The previous digest's "In the Oldest Old, Slow Walking Predicts Falls" (PMID 42734897) is now bracketed at the other end by "How Fast You Walk in Your 50s May Predict Falls Decades Later" (PMID 42837653), where midlife gait speed — not grip, sit-to-stand or leg power — tracked recurrent falls years later.
- **Timing becomes its own variable, again.** Following the previous digest's well-timed stroke safety talk (PMID 42731691), "Hospital Fall Risk Changes by the Day — and a One-Time Admission Check May Miss It" (PMID 42842205) shows risk evolving measurably by hospital days 3 and 7, and PMID 42696807 reinforces the four-month post-hip-fracture plateau.
- **From whether you fall to how you fall.** "Which Falls End in a Head Injury — and the Movements That Seem to Protect You" (PMID 42832847) is genuinely new territory for this digest series, identifying potentially trainable protective movements rather than treating head impact as luck.
- **Sedative prescribing data gets a long view.** "Sedatives Are Finally Fading After Hip Fractures — But Opioid Prescribing Has Barely Budged" (PMID 42852585) puts 16 years of Ontario dispensing behind the deprescribing trend the previous digest tracked in Australian nursing homes (PMID 42786600) — and shows the opioid half of the problem has barely moved.
- **Policy framework arrives.** "A New National Blueprint Says Falls Prevention Can't Be Left to Doctors Alone" (PMID 42833801) gives the cross-sector argument from earlier runs a named US roadmap.

**Counterpoints**

- **Not all sleep medications behave alike.** "Newer Sleep Drugs Don't Appear to Raise Fall Risk — But the Evidence Is Still Thin" (PMID 42799573) complicates the blanket sedatives-cause-falls framing that sat behind the previous digest's Australian deprescribing analysis (PMID 42786600), while PMID 42852585 shows the class that has actually fallen out of use is benzodiazepines, not opioids.
- **A widely used safety device has little behind it.** "Bed and Chair Alarms Don't Prevent Falls — So Why Are Hospitals Still Using Them?" (PMID 42833806) extends the skepticism the previous digest applied to AI fall-prediction tools (PMID 42696799) to a low-tech practice families often read as a sign of good care — and "Singapore Launches a 2,300-Person Study Using Wearables and Virtual Reality to Predict Who Will Fall" (PMID 42789327) is still, by its own description, a baseline paper with no predictive accuracy results yet.
- **Mental rehearsal is not yet a substitute.** "Can Imagining Movement Improve Balance? The Evidence Is Thin but Intriguing" (PMID 42501905) tempers the enthusiasm generated by the previous digest's dual-task telerehabilitation trial (PMID 42468927).

**New themes**

- Falls as a possible symptom of undiagnosed seizures — "A Fall That Lands You in the ER May Be an Early Warning Sign of Seizures" (PMID 42848964).
- Identity and stigma as the barrier to fall prevention programs: "'It's Not for Me': Why Older Adults With Disabilities Avoid Fall Prevention Classes" (PMID 42831317).
- Real-time gait mechanics as an early warning in stroke recovery — "A Subtle Change in Stride Flags Stroke Survivors Who Are About to Stumble" (PMID 42696957).

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Across this batch, the fall or fracture itself is repeatedly treated as a closed episode when the evidence says it is an opening. An ER visit for a fall preceded first seizures by weeks to months (PMID 42848964); wrist and shoulder fractures went uncoded as osteoporosis (PMID 42448012); nursing home residents with a recent fracture were 72.8% likely to still be on a fall-risk drug and only 4.2% likely to be on a bisphosphonate (PMID 42632250); 44.6% of hip fracture patients were still filling opioids a year after discharge (PMID 42852585); and interviews after upper-limb fragility fractures described a fear-driven retreat from activity that no one addressed (PMID 42358089).

**Why pitch this now:** Each of these gaps is closable with a conversation, a scan, or a medication review that already exists in routine care — and the new National Falls Prevention Action Plan (PMID 42833801) is built on exactly the clinical-to-community handoffs these studies show are not happening.

**Angle:** A practical feature on the weeks after a fall or fracture — what should be triggered, what usually isn't, and the specific requests that close the gap — written for older adults and family caregivers, with a sidebar for care staff on turning a fracture into a standing checklist.

**Potential headlines:**
- After a Broken Wrist, Four Things That Should Happen Next — and Usually Don't
- The Fall Is Over. The Most Important Part Hasn't Started.
- A Fracture Is Your Best Warning You'll Have Another One

**Potential outlets:**
- **National Council on Aging** — their own team authored the national action plan in this batch, and the post-fall follow-up gap is precisely the clinical-to-community handoff the plan assumes will occur.
- **Caring.com** — families making care decisions after a parent's fracture need a concrete list of what to ask for during the discharge and rehab window.
- **McKnight's Long-Term Care News** — the Medicare nursing home prescribing data (PMID 42632250) and the 16-year Ontario opioid trend (PMID 42852585) speak directly to directors of nursing running post-fracture medication reviews.
- **HomeCare Magazine** — agencies serving clients home after a fracture are the ones positioned to catch lingering opioids, missing bone treatment, and activity withdrawal driven by fear.
