### 1. Doctors and nurses spot frailty readily — but often miss when it's time to talk about palliative care

**Journal:** *J Frailty Aging* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 5
**PMID:** 42700391 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.tjfa.2026.100196

**The study:** Researchers in the Netherlands and Belgium gave 15 written patient cases to health professionals chosen for their expertise in frailty and palliative care, and asked them to mark which features in each case signaled frailty, palliative care needs, or both — and how certain they felt. Professionals consistently used different features to reach the two judgments, and in no case were they more confident identifying palliative care needs than frailty. The authors concluded clinicians may overlook palliative care needs in patients who are already quite frail.

**Why it matters:** If clinicians are quicker to label someone "frail" than to recognize that person could benefit from palliative care, conversations about comfort, symptom relief and goals of care may be delayed until a crisis forces them.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or your spouse have been told you are "frail," it's reasonable to ask your clinician a follow-up question: could palliative care — which is about comfort, symptom control and planning, not giving up — add anything for me now? Research suggests clinicians are less confident spotting that need than spotting frailty itself.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living and home care teams, this points to a practical gap in interprofessional handoffs: frailty scores are widely recorded, but there's no shared language for flagging when frailty has progressed to palliative care need. Staff training and referral triggers may need to be explicit rather than left to clinical intuition.

**Caveats:** Not a study of patients — clinicians responded to written case vignettes, which may not reflect real-world decisions; number of respondents not reported in the abstract; limited to expert professionals in two countries, so findings may not generalize.

### 2. What nursing home staff and families actually value from a palliative care consultation — and it isn't just fewer hospital trips

**Journal:** *Gerontologist* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 12
**PMID:** 42742117 | **DOI:** 10.1093/geront/gnag220

**The study:** Researchers interviewed 33 key partners — nursing home clinicians and nurses, palliative care physicians and nurse practitioners, and nursing home and palliative agency directors — across 14 nursing homes in the Eastern United States about what counts as a meaningful outcome of a palliative care consultation. Participants described outcomes in four interconnected domains: residents (care that matches their goals, symptom relief, fewer hospitalizations, better understanding of choices and a sense of control), families (shared decision-making, understanding the illness trajectory, feeling supported), staff (capacity to deliver good care, emotional support), and the facility itself (filling care gaps, reputation, staff morale). The authors note current quality metrics capture little of this.

**Why it matters:** Palliative care in nursing homes is typically judged by hospitalization and cost data, yet the people delivering and receiving it say the real payoffs include feeling informed, supported and in control — outcomes families should know to expect and ask about.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you live in — or are choosing — a nursing home, you can ask whether the facility brings in palliative care consultants, and what that visit would actually give you: help with pain and breathlessness, a clear explanation of your options, and care that reflects what matters most to you, not just fewer ambulance rides.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For administrators and directors of nursing, this offers a ready-made framework for demonstrating the value of a palliative care partnership beyond utilization data — including staff morale and reduced moral distress, which bear directly on retention.

**Caveats:** Qualitative interview study with 33 participants in one U.S. region; no residents or family members were interviewed (their perspectives were reported second-hand); findings describe perceptions, not measured outcomes, and cannot show that consultations produce these results.

### 3. In a blinded trial, cancer patients preferred AI's empathic words over a palliative care doctor's — nearly three times out of four

**Journal:** *J Pain Symptom Manage* | **Published:** 2026 Oct
**PMID:** 42599269 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jpainsymman.2026.05.011

**The study:** This is a secondary analysis of the PALL-AI randomized survey trial, in which 105 patients with cancer read blinded empathic responses to fear and anxiety about treatment — some written by palliative care physicians, some generated by artificial intelligence using the same prompt and length limit. Patients chose the AI-written statement 72.4% of the time. Sentiment analysis found all three AI statements read as positive while all three physician statements read as negative, with AI using roughly twice as many positive words; "trust" and "joy" language correlated most strongly with patient preference.

**Why it matters:** As health systems begin drafting patient messages with AI, this suggests warmth and hopeful phrasing drive how supported patients feel — a signal both to clinicians about their own wording and to patients about who, or what, may be writing the note they receive.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If a message from your care team ever feels oddly polished or upbeat, it's fair to ask whether AI helped write it — and equally fair to tell your clinician plainly when their wording lands as bleak rather than honest. What you prefer to hear is legitimate clinical information.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For clinicians and care staff who deliver hard news, the takeaway isn't to hand the conversation to a chatbot: it's that positive, trust-building language measurably changes how patients receive the same information, and that's a teachable communication skill.

**Caveats:** Patients rated short written statements, not real conversations, so preference for warm phrasing may not reflect what serves patients in an actual goals-of-care discussion; only three statements per source; single health system; N=105; no assessment of accuracy or long-term trust.

### 4. A tape measure around the calf, plus one blood test, helps predict survival in advanced cancer

**Journal:** *Clin Nutr ESPEN* | **Published:** 2026 Aug 31
**PMID:** 42674364 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.clnesp.2026.105053

**The study:** Brazilian researchers followed 370 adult outpatients with incurable cancer at a specialized palliative care unit between 2021 and 2025, measuring calf circumference (a low-cost proxy for muscle mass) along with nutritional signs and inflammatory blood markers. Low calf circumference alone was independently associated with a roughly 58% higher risk of death within 90 days, but predictive accuracy improved substantially when it was paired with recent weight loss, low albumin, or elevated C-reactive protein — tripling the estimated mortality risk in those with both markers altered.

**Why it matters:** A tape measure and routine blood work can give patients, families and clinicians a more realistic sense of the months ahead, which is exactly the information needed to time hospice referrals, nutrition decisions and family planning.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're living with advanced cancer, shrinking calves and unintentional weight loss are not cosmetic details — they're prognostic signals worth raising with your team, and they can open a concrete conversation about what nutrition support, symptom care and planning make sense for you right now.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For home care and senior living staff, this validates a simple, inexpensive measure that non-physicians can track over time; a falling calf circumference alongside weight loss is a defensible reason to request a palliative care or dietitian review.

**Caveats:** Observational cohort — associations cannot establish cause, and the markers predict rather than explain risk; single center in Brazil, so findings may not generalize; predictive accuracy remained modest (area under the curve about 0.63) and should not be used to forecast an individual's survival; population was outpatients with incurable cancer, not older adults generally.

### 5. Pharmacy technicians visiting housebound elders at home cut falls and emergency contacts in a small pilot

**Journal:** *Int J Clin Pharm* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 12
**PMID:** 42730948 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11096-026-02211-2

**The study:** Two general practices in a deprived area of Glasgow tested home medication reviews by pharmacy technicians, supervised by a clinical pharmacist, for 25 housebound adults aged 60–89 (median age 81) taking at least five regular medications for two or more long-term conditions. Twenty-three of the 25 had collectively missed 91 routine long-term condition reviews since becoming housebound, and were taking a median of 14 regular medications (up to 27). Over 83 home encounters, 21 medications were stopped, 19 started and 15 adjusted; self-reported falls fell from 8 to zero over three months, unscheduled care contacts dropped from 56 to 21, and quality-of-life scores rose modestly from 57 to 62.

**Why it matters:** Older people who can no longer get to the clinic quietly fall out of medication review altogether — and this pilot shows bringing the review to the front door surfaces a large volume of fixable prescribing problems.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If leaving the house has become difficult, you may be missing the annual medication and long-term condition reviews you're entitled to. It's worth asking your practice or pharmacy whether someone can review your medicines at home — especially if you're taking five or more, or you've had a fall.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and care staff supporting a housebound person, keeping a running list of every medication and every fall gives a visiting pharmacist or technician the raw material to act on; this model also suggests advocating for home-based reviews rather than waiting for a clinic appointment that won't happen.

**Caveats:** Very small feasibility study (N=25), two practices, no control group, only three months of follow-up, and falls were self-reported — improvements could reflect chance, regression to the mean or extra attention rather than the medication changes. Notably, people receiving palliative care and those lacking capacity were excluded, so it does not tell us how the model works at end of life.

### 6. Written End-of-Life Wishes Do Cut Hospital Transfers From Care Homes — But Not Always

**Journal:** *J Hosp Palliat Nurs* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 1
**PMID:** 42714187 | **DOI:** 10.1097/NJH.0000000000001217

**The study:** Researchers reviewed 23 studies published between 2004 and 2024 across nine countries (15 quantitative, 7 qualitative, 1 mixed methods) examining why residential aged care residents get sent to the hospital in their final months — a transfer that happens to roughly 70% of residents. Advance care plans and directives that contained specific instructions, such as do-not-resuscitate or do-not-hospitalize orders, were associated with fewer hospital transfers than plans without them. But the review also surfaced troubling reports of residents being transferred to hospitals against wishes clearly documented in their own advance care plans, with transfer decisions driven by resident age, acuity of illness, perceived vulnerability, and family pressure.

**Why it matters:** A vague advance directive may do little to keep you out of an emergency department in your last weeks of life, while specific, written instructions measurably reduce transfers — and even those are sometimes overridden.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you live in or are moving into residential care, consider asking whether your advance care plan includes explicit instructions about hospitalization and resuscitation — not just general statements of values. You can also ask the facility directly: who makes the call at 2 a.m., where is my plan stored, and who reads it before an ambulance is called?
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and care home staff, the review's most uncomfortable finding is that transfers sometimes happen against documented wishes, often under acute pressure or family insistence. Facilities may want to examine their own after-hours decision pathways and make sure directives are accessible at the moment of crisis, not buried in a file.

**Caveats:** Scoping review of heterogeneous observational studies — associations between specific directives and fewer transfers cannot establish causation. Studies span 20 years and nine countries with differing aged care and legal systems, so findings may not generalize to any single setting.

### 7. Why Some Hospitals Get Dying Cancer Patients Into Hospice and Others Don't: It Comes Down to Relationships

**Journal:** *JAMA Intern Med* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 14
**PMID:** 42734951 | **DOI:** 10.1001/jamainternmed.2026.4511

**The study:** Investigators studied six US palliative care programs — three with high rates of hospice use among patients with metastatic cancer and three with low rates — conducting 93 interviews with palliative care clinicians, oncologists and patients plus 424 hours of on-site observation. The clearest difference was not staffing or protocols but the working relationship between the palliative care and oncology teams. High-performing programs showed mutual trust, physical proximity, real-time communication and a service-oriented palliative care team; low performers showed distrust, unfamiliarity and negative regard — and their patients got palliative care later and heard about hospice later.

**Why it matters:** Whether a seriously ill older adult gets timely symptom relief and an unhurried conversation about hospice may depend less on their diagnosis than on whether two teams down the hall from each other actually talk.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or a partner is being treated for advanced cancer, you can ask your oncologist how closely they work with the palliative care team and whether palliative care can be brought in now rather than later. Asking for a referral early is reasonable — palliative care is about symptom control and planning, not giving up on treatment.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living and health system leaders, this points at a fixable target: co-locating teams, building in real-time communication, and investing in relationship-building between specialties may move end-of-life outcomes more than another policy document.

**Caveats:** Qualitative study at just six programs in the Midwest, Upper South and Deep South — no US-wide representation, and the design identifies plausible mechanisms rather than proving that improving team relationships causes better hospice use.

### 8. Palliative Care Doctors Are Warming to AI Predictions of How Long Patients Have Left

**Journal:** *J Pain Symptom Manage* | **Published:** 2026 Oct
**PMID:** 42463061 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jpainsymman.2026.07.005

**The study:** A national survey of 2,500 US hospice and palliative medicine physicians (537 completed responses, January 2024 to March 2025) assessed attitudes toward artificial intelligence tools that predict life expectancy. Nearly 80% reported low knowledge of AI and 92% low knowledge of AI-based prognostication, and only 24% had ever used an AI-generated prognosis — yet attitudes were broadly positive: 71% thought AI could get patients to palliative care earlier, 78% that it could improve end-of-life planning, and 63% supported its use in hospice eligibility decisions. Notable reservations remained: 36% worried about legal liability and 38% that AI predictions could damage patients' sense of hope.

**Why it matters:** Algorithms embedded in electronic health records are starting to influence when patients are flagged for palliative care or hospice, and most of the physicians involved say they don't yet understand how these tools work.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If a clinician tells you what to expect about time remaining, it is fair to ask where that estimate comes from — clinical judgment, a statistical model, or an algorithm in the medical record — and how much uncertainty surrounds it. You also have the right to say how much prognostic detail you do and don't want to hear.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Caregivers and senior living staff should expect AI-triggered referrals for serious illness conversations to become more common, and should press for transparency: who reviews the algorithm's output before a family is contacted, and how are errors caught?

**Caveats:** Self-reported survey with a low response rate (537 of 2,500), respondents predominantly White and possibly skewed toward early technology adopters, so views may not represent the whole specialty. Measures attitudes only, not the accuracy or effects of AI prognostic tools.

### 9. An IV Drug Widely Hoped to Relieve Severe Cancer Pain Failed to Beat Placebo

**Journal:** *J Palliat Care* | **Published:** 2026 Oct
**PMID:** 42536632 | **DOI:** 10.1177/08258597261461797

**The study:** In a multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled feasibility trial across French palliative care units, 35 patients with cancer pain that had not responded to opioids received either a continuous low-dose intravenous lidocaine infusion (18 patients) or placebo (17 patients) for 48 hours. Neither group differed meaningfully on the primary endpoint — a 30% or greater drop in pain intensity — and pain fell in both groups. Lidocaine was generally well tolerated with mostly mild side effects, though 61% of patients needed a dose increase.

**Why it matters:** Intravenous lidocaine is sometimes used off-label as a last resort for opioid-refractory cancer pain; this trial offers no evidence that it works better than placebo, while also documenting how genuinely hard it is to run rigorous trials in dying patients.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If severe pain is not responding to opioids, it is worth asking your palliative care team what the actual evidence is behind any proposed add-on infusion, and what alternatives — nerve blocks, adjuvant medications, radiation — might be considered. Note that pain improved in the placebo group too, a reminder that attentive care itself matters.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For clinicians and families weighing aggressive interventions in the last weeks of life, this is a useful example of a plausible treatment that did not pan out — and an argument for asking about trade-offs in burden, monitoring and transfers before starting an infusion.

**Caveats:** Small sample (N=35), well below the planned enrollment, so the study was underpowered and cannot rule out a benefit; a negative feasibility trial is not proof of ineffectiveness. Very short 48-hour follow-up; conducted in French palliative care units, which may limit generalizability.

### 10. Caring for a Loved One With Advanced Cancer in a War Zone: Nearly Half of Family Caregivers Report Severe Anxiety

**Journal:** *Palliat Med* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 11
**PMID:** 42728769 | **DOI:** 10.1177/02692163261485102

**The study:** Researchers surveyed family caregivers of adults with advanced cancer at the Turkish-Palestinian Friendship Hospital in the Gaza Strip between May and August 2022, using the Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scale-21 and the Comprehensive Needs Assessment Tool for Cancer Caregivers. Of 400 caregivers approached, 97% completed the survey. Severe or extremely severe symptoms were common: anxiety in 44.1%, depression in 33.2%, and stress in 29.6%. Unmet needs for information (80.9%) and practical help (77.7%) were the most frequent gaps, and higher unmet needs tracked closely with higher distress; women reported markedly more distress than men.

**Why it matters:** It quantifies something families everywhere recognize — that not knowing what is coming next and not having hands-on help are themselves drivers of caregiver depression and anxiety, which means information and navigation support are not extras but part of the treatment plan.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If a family member is caring for you through a serious illness, their exhaustion and worry are part of your care picture too — you can ask your palliative or oncology team whether your caregiver has been formally assessed for distress, and request that written information, a care navigator, and practical home help be arranged for them alongside your own symptom management.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living and hospice staff, this study makes the case for routinely screening family caregivers, not just patients, and for recognizing that daughters, wives and other women in the caregiving role carry measurably heavier psychological loads and may need proactive outreach rather than waiting for them to ask.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional and observational design, so it cannot establish what causes what; single-center; all outcomes self-reported via questionnaires; conducted in a conflict-affected, resource-constrained setting, so distress levels and unmet need rates may not generalize to other health systems.

### 11. A Palliative Care Clinic That Drains Fluid Buildup — Without an Eight-Day Hospital Stay

**Journal:** *J Palliat Med* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 10
**PMID:** 42722989 | **DOI:** 10.1177/10966218261486546

**The study:** At Parkland Health in Dallas, clinicians first reviewed records of patients followed by palliative care with stage IV cancer or cirrhosis who were hospitalized for paracentesis (draining fluid from the abdomen) between 2018 and 2023: 493 patients accounted for 672 admissions, with a median stay of eight days. They then opened an outpatient palliative care procedure clinic; in its first 12 months, 19 patients underwent 44 drainage procedures (median age 50; 84% had malignant ascites). A median of 3,500 mL of fluid was removed per visit, with no immediate complications during a 20-minute observation period and none found on chart review within seven days. Baseline symptom burden was high (mean Palliative Care Outcome Scale score 17.3), and staff strongly supported continuing the clinic (4.8 out of 5).

**Why it matters:** Repeated fluid buildup from advanced cancer or cirrhosis is a leading reason people with serious illness end up admitted to the hospital, and this early experience suggests the relief can often be delivered in a clinic visit instead of a multi-day stay.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If abdominal fluid buildup keeps sending you to the hospital, it is worth asking your palliative care or specialty team whether the drainage could be done as an outpatient procedure, or whether an indwelling drain is an option — and what the local pathway is for getting it scheduled quickly when symptoms flare.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and care communities, this is a model worth asking local health systems about: an outpatient route for a common, recurring procedure can spare a resident days of hospitalization, disrupted routines and delirium risk, while keeping symptom relief on schedule.

**Caveats:** Very small pilot cohort (19 patients, 44 procedures) at a single safety-net center with only 12 months of experience and no control group; the retrospective design and short follow-up cannot show whether the clinic actually reduced hospitalizations or improved patient-reported outcomes; median patient age was 50, so findings are not specific to older adults; staff satisfaction was self-reported.

### 12. Palliative Care Referrals Came Just 23 Days Before Death in Hospital Study

**Journal:** *J Palliat Med* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 9
**PMID:** 42713708 | **DOI:** 10.1177/10966218261486532

**The study:** Researchers reviewed the electronic health records of 476 adults with cancer (median age 67; 52% women) referred to a newly established multidisciplinary supportive and palliative care team at a tertiary hospital in Zagreb, Croatia, during its first three years (March 2022 to March 2025). At the time of referral, 95% already had metastatic disease and 93% had moderate-to-severe functional impairment, with fatigue, pain, loss of appetite, and drowsiness rated as the most severe symptoms. The median time from hospital admission to palliative referral was six days, and the median time from referral to death was just 23 days.

**Why it matters:** Palliative care delivers the most benefit — better symptom control, fewer crises, clearer goals — when it starts months rather than weeks before death, yet this study shows referrals routinely arrive only after disease is far advanced and function has already collapsed.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or someone you love has advanced cancer or another serious illness, you don't have to wait until treatment options run out to ask about palliative care. Consider asking your oncologist directly: "Can I meet the palliative or supportive care team now, alongside my treatment?" Palliative care is about symptom relief and quality of life, and it can run in parallel with active treatment.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living and care teams, this is a referral-timing problem, not a patient-willingness problem. Consider auditing how late in the illness trajectory your residents or patients typically reach palliative services, and whether triggers — metastatic diagnosis, a sharp drop in function, repeated hospitalizations — could prompt automatic referral rather than a last-minute one.

**Caveats:** Retrospective, observational, single-center study at one hospital in Croatia; findings reflect that country's health system and a newly formed team, so timing patterns may not generalize to US hospitals or hospice systems. No comparison group and no direct measurement of whether earlier referral would have changed outcomes for these patients.

### 13. Fewer Pills at the End of Life: Review Finds Preventive Drugs Often Linger Past Their Usefulness

**Journal:** *J Hosp Palliat Nurs* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 1
**PMID:** 42519959 | **DOI:** 10.1097/NJH.0000000000001254

**The study:** Researchers conducted a rapid review of the literature on deprescribing — the deliberate, structured reduction of medications — for adults with serious illness and limited life expectancy, searching PubMed, CINAHL, and Scopus for studies published from January 2020 through December 2025. Of 494 records screened, 37 studies were included, spanning qualitative and observational research, pharmacist-led interventions, systematic reviews, and evaluations of tools such as STOPPFrail and OncPal. The authors report that long-term preventive drugs near the end of life — statins, blood pressure medications, diabetes drugs, blood thinners, and vitamins — frequently offer little short-term benefit while adding side effects and treatment burden, yet deprescribing remains inconsistent and usually reactive rather than planned.

**Why it matters:** Older adults on hospice or palliative care are often still swallowing a dozen pills a day aimed at preventing problems years away, which can cause nausea, dizziness, falls, and daily stress for the person and the caregiver managing the pillbox.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're living with a serious illness, it is reasonable to ask your clinician or hospice nurse to review every medication with one question in mind: "What is this doing for me right now?" Bring your full list, including vitamins and supplements, and ask which drugs are for comfort and which are for long-term prevention — then discuss whether stopping any would ease your day. Don't stop anything on your own.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Caregivers and nursing staff are often the first to notice medication side effects and refusals. The review positions nurses as central to medication reconciliation, spotting inappropriate drugs, and monitoring how the person does after a drug is withdrawn — so documenting observations after any change, and raising the topic proactively rather than waiting for a bad reaction, is part of the job.

**Caveats:** This is a rapid review rather than a systematic review or trial, so the search and appraisal were streamlined; included studies were largely qualitative and observational and cannot establish that deprescribing causes better outcomes. The authors themselves note that evidence on communication strategies, operational models, and nursing education remains thin.

### 14. Caregivers With Stronger Spiritual Well-Being Reported Lighter Burden in Palliative Care Study

**Journal:** *Geriatr Nurs* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 3
**PMID:** 42697036 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.gerinurse.2026.104340

**The study:** Researchers interviewed 85 family caregivers of patients receiving palliative care at a university hospital in southeastern Türkiye between April and July 2025, using the Zarit Burden Interview and the Spirituality Scale. Average caregiver burden was moderately high (46.56 out of a possible range on the Zarit), and married caregivers reported significantly higher burden than unmarried caregivers. Caregivers who scored higher on spirituality reported lower burden, a moderate negative correlation (r = -0.391, p < 0.001).

**Why it matters:** Family caregiving in serious illness carries real physical and emotional costs, and this study suggests that a caregiver's sense of meaning and spiritual support isn't a side issue but something linked to how heavy the load feels.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're the one caring for a seriously ill spouse or parent, take your own sense of meaning seriously — whether that comes from faith, community, ritual, or reflection. You can ask the palliative team whether a chaplain, spiritual care counselor, or support group is available to you, not just to the patient; support for caregivers is a legitimate part of palliative care.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For nurses and senior living staff, the practical takeaway is to screen the caregiver, not only the patient. Routinely asking about burden and about what sustains a caregiver spiritually — and connecting them to culturally appropriate chaplaincy or psychosocial support — is a low-cost intervention that this study suggests may map onto how burdened they feel.

**Caveats:** Small sample (N=85), single center, and conducted in one region of Türkiye, so the cultural context around spirituality and family caregiving may not generalize. The design is cross-sectional and correlational, so it cannot show that spirituality reduces burden — burdened caregivers may also feel less spiritually resourced. All measures were self-reported.

### 15. What People Actually Say When They Choose Assisted Dying — It's Rarely Just About Pain

**Journal:** *BMJ* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 15
**PMID:** 42744336 | **DOI:** 10.1136/bmj-2026-100610

**The study:** Swiss researchers conducted in-depth interviews with 30 adults who had decision-making capacity and had already contacted a right-to-die association to begin an assisted suicide request; 14 of them died by assisted suicide within weeks of the interview. Interviews took place in participants' homes or hospitals in French, German, or Italian between 2023 and 2025 and were analyzed thematically. Participants described their decision not as a reaction to one unbearable symptom but as the product of six interacting motivations — imagining and shaping their own death, viewing assisted suicide as a trusted and reliable option, seeking validation from others, reclaiming control from a medicalized dying process, crossing a personal threshold of unbearability, and acting before a narrowing window of physical or mental capacity closed.

**Why it matters:** It reframes requests for assisted dying as long-developing, values-driven deliberations rather than impulsive responses to suffering — which changes how clinicians, families, and palliative teams should respond when the subject is raised.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you've ever thought about how you want your death to go — including whether you'd want control over its timing — this research suggests those thoughts are usually part of a long process, not a crisis. Consider putting your values in writing through an advance directive and naming a health care proxy, and know that raising the topic with your doctor or a palliative care clinician is an invitation to a conversation, not a commitment to a course of action.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and care staff, the key finding is that a resident's mention of assisted dying often signals a wish for control, validation, and acknowledgment of a narrowing window of capacity — not necessarily uncontrolled pain. The practical response is curious, non-defensive exploration of what the person is trying to preserve, rather than immediate escalation or reassurance.

**Caveats:** Small qualitative sample (N=30), recruited largely through right-to-die associations so views may be unrepresentative of all seriously ill people; conducted in Switzerland, where assisted suicide is legal and culturally established, limiting generalizability; funded by the Swiss National Science Foundation and the Pallium Foundation, with no funder role reported.

### 16. Shame About a Liver Diagnosis Tracks With Worse Symptoms and More Distress

**Journal:** *J Pain Symptom Manage* | **Published:** 2026 Oct
**PMID:** 42362166 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jpainsymman.2026.06.021

**The study:** Researchers surveyed 218 outpatients with decompensated cirrhosis (median age 60, range roughly 51-65 for the middle half; 58% alcohol-related liver disease) using validated questionnaires measuring perceived stigma, anxiety and depression symptoms, and physical symptom burden. Average stigma scores were higher in people with more advanced liver disease and, notably, in younger patients. After adjusting for age, gender, cause of disease, and disease severity, greater perceived stigma was independently linked to more depression, more anxiety, and heavier physical symptom burden.

**Why it matters:** Advanced liver disease is a common and under-palliated serious illness in older adults, and this suggests that feeling judged — not just the illness itself — is tied to how badly patients feel day to day, making stigma a potential target for supportive care.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're living with advanced liver disease, the shame that often comes attached to the diagnosis may be compounding your anxiety, low mood, and physical symptoms — it's worth telling your hepatology or palliative care team how the diagnosis makes you feel, not just how your body feels, and asking what psychological and symptom support is available alongside liver treatment.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and senior living staff: language matters. Framing cirrhosis as a serious chronic illness rather than a self-inflicted one — and screening residents with liver disease for depression and anxiety, not just for physical decompensation — may be part of good symptom care.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional and observational, so it cannot show that stigma causes distress or symptoms (the reverse is equally plausible); all outcomes self-reported; single-center academic liver center population that may not reflect community or nursing home patients; 30% of eligible patients did not participate.

### 17. Routine Urine Drug Tests in Palliative Care: A Review Finds No Proof They Help

**Journal:** *J Pain Symptom Manage* | **Published:** 2026 Oct
**PMID:** 42362165 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jpainsymman.2026.06.020

**The study:** Two palliative medicine researchers systematically searched the literature through May 2025 and reviewed major guidelines (NCCN, ASCO, CDC) to assess the evidence for routinely urine-drug-testing patients with cancer or on palliative opioid therapy. They found no published study showing that routine screening prevents overdose, reduces diversion, improves pain control, or extends survival, and found that test results rarely change opioid management. They also documented harms: out-of-pocket costs, stigma, patients being dropped from care, and racially unequal application of testing.

**Why it matters:** Many seriously ill patients on opioids are now tested as a matter of policy rather than clinical judgment, and this analysis argues the practice carries real costs and stigma without demonstrated safety benefit.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're on opioids for cancer or serious-illness pain and are asked for a urine drug test, you can ask why it's being ordered, what will be done with the result, and what it will cost you — a specific clinical reason is reasonable, but blanket testing has no proven benefit for patients in your situation.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and senior living clinicians: unexpected or misread test results can trigger abrupt opioid tapering or discharge from a pain practice, leaving a seriously ill resident undertreated. Knowing that a single screen is a low-quality piece of evidence — and that confirmatory testing and context are essential — helps you advocate before pain control is disrupted.

**Caveats:** This is a narrative review and critical analysis, not new primary data or a formal systematic review with meta-analysis; conclusions reflect the authors' interpretation and the absence of evidence rather than evidence of absence; addresses mixed cancer and palliative populations rather than older adults specifically.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**

- *Referrals still arrive at the very end.* The new Croatian record review — "Palliative Care Referrals Came Just 23 Days Before Death in Hospital Study" (PMID 42713708) — lands squarely on top of the previous digest's Canadian study of consultations for adults 90 and older (PMID 42580255), where the median time from consultation to death or discharge was four days. Both describe patients whose function has already collapsed before anyone calls.
- *Body wasting as a prognostic signal.* "A tape measure around the calf, plus one blood test, helps predict survival in advanced cancer" (PMID 42674364) extends the previous digest's cachexia prevalence review (PMID 42674857) and the Japanese functional-ability study (PMID 42660629): cheap bedside measures of muscle, weight and function keep outperforming intuition for timing decisions.
- *Family caregivers are carrying measurable, under-assessed distress.* The Gaza caregiver survey (PMID 42728769) and the spiritual well-being study in Türkiye (PMID 42697036) reinforce the previous digest's findings on unmet social and psychological needs at end of life (PMID 42407095) and on skilled nursing residents' families, where family worry ran to 58% and unmet information needs to 46% (PMID 40820287).
- *What families and staff actually want is broader than utilization data.* "What nursing home staff and families actually value from a palliative care consultation" (PMID 42742117) echoes the previous digest's diverse caregiver interviews describing hospital palliative care as "uncoordinated and reactive" (PMID 41247831) and the Belgian comfort-rating study showing clinicians and families do not see the same dying patient (PMID 42530751).
- *Home-based, non-physician models keep delivering.* Pharmacy technicians doing home medication reviews for housebound elders (PMID 42730948) sits alongside the previous digest's nurse-practitioner home dementia care review (PMID 42343547) and the community health worker pilot (PMID 42681826): bring the review to the door, and problems surface.

**Advances**

- Advance care planning moves from "does it work" to "what has to be in it." The previous digest's umbrella review found structured planning reduces unwanted aggressive treatment (PMID 42085825); the new scoping review, "Written End-of-Life Wishes Do Cut Hospital Transfers From Care Homes — But Not Always" (PMID 42714187), specifies that explicit do-not-hospitalize or do-not-resuscitate instructions are what track with fewer transfers — and documents residents moved against clearly documented wishes.
- The coordination story gains a mechanism. Where the previous digest's English professional interviews described unclear ownership of coordination (PMID 42663268), the new JAMA Internal Medicine study (PMID 42734951) names a concrete driver: trust, proximity and real-time talk between palliative care and oncology teams separated high- from low-performing programs.
- Recognition gets a finer edge. The previous digest established that most older adults with palliative needs don't have cancer (PMID 42366281); the new vignette study (PMID 42700391) shows clinicians confidently label frailty while feeling less certain about the palliative care need sitting inside it.
- Medication burden becomes its own target. The deprescribing rapid review (PMID 42519959) turns the previous digest's general symptom-management focus toward the pillbox itself, and pairs naturally with the home medication review pilot (PMID 42730948).

**Counterpoints**

- Plausible palliative interventions don't always survive testing. The previous digest's virtual reality review looked promising for drug-free symptom relief (PMID 42490252); the new randomized lidocaine trial (PMID 42536632) found no benefit over placebo for opioid-refractory cancer pain — and pain fell in both arms, a caution about enthusiasm ahead of evidence. The urine drug testing review (PMID 42362165) makes the same point about entrenched routine practice.
- The perception gap cuts both ways. The previous digest showed doctors rating patients as more comfortable than families did (PMID 42530751); the new blinded PALL-AI analysis (PMID 42599269) found patients preferred AI-written empathic statements to physicians' nearly three times out of four, with clinician wording reading as uniformly negative. Clinicians' words may be landing differently than they believe in both directions.

**New themes**

- *Artificial intelligence arrives at the bedside.* Two studies with no counterpart in the previous digest: patients' preference for AI-written empathy (PMID 42599269) and a national survey finding palliative physicians broadly positive about AI life-expectancy prediction while reporting little understanding of it (PMID 42463061).
- *Stigma as a symptom driver.* Shame attached to a cirrhosis diagnosis tracked with worse anxiety, depression and physical symptoms (PMID 42362166), while routine urine drug testing was linked to stigma and unequal application without proven benefit (PMID 42362165).
- *Assisted dying as deliberation, not crisis.* Swiss interviews with people who had begun an assisted suicide request describe six long-developing motivations centered on control and a narrowing window of capacity (PMID 42744336) — a theme absent from prior runs.
- *Moving procedures out of the hospital.* An outpatient palliative care procedure clinic for abdominal fluid drainage replaced stays with a median of eight days (PMID 42722989).

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Three studies in this batch, taken together, show that the most delicate judgments in serious illness — how long someone has, and how that news is worded — are quietly being handed to machines and to cheap bedside measures. Cancer patients chose AI-written empathic responses over a palliative physician's 72.4% of the time (PMID 42599269); a national survey found palliative physicians broadly favorable toward AI prognosis tools, including for hospice eligibility, while nearly 80% reported low knowledge of AI (PMID 42463061); and a tape measure around the calf plus routine blood work predicted 90-day survival in advanced cancer (PMID 42674364). Meanwhile referrals in one hospital still arrived a median of 23 days before death (PMID 42713708), which is the vacuum these tools are being sold into.

**Why pitch this now:** Algorithms are already embedded in medical records and beginning to flag patients for palliative care or hospice, and the clinicians using them say they don't yet understand how they work — a gap patients and families will feel before anyone regulates it.

**Angle:** A reader-facing feature on where a prognosis actually comes from and who wrote the kind message about it, built around a handful of questions an older adult or family caregiver can ask out loud — with the tape-measure study as the reminder that some of the most useful tools cost nothing and are still underused. Aimed at older adults living with serious illness and the families making decisions with them.

**Potential headlines:**
- What Your Doctor Is Using to Guess How Much Time You Have
- Patients Liked the Computer's Kind Words Better Than the Doctor's
- The Prognosis Question You Have Every Right to Ask

**Potential outlets:**
- **Next Avenue** — regularly runs practical, dignified pieces on aging and serious illness; the "questions to ask about your prognosis" spine is exactly their register.
- **Hospice News** — the survey's findings on AI in hospice eligibility decisions and on physicians' low AI literacy are a direct operational and regulatory concern for their provider readership.
- **AARP** — national reach with consumer-rights framing; the idea that a warm-sounding message may have been drafted by software is a concrete, checkable thing readers can act on.
- **NPR** — the blinded finding that patients preferred AI empathy to a palliative physician's is the kind of counterintuitive result their health desk builds a segment around.

## Story Ideas by Study

- **Doctors and nurses spot frailty readily — but often miss when it's time to talk about palliative care (PMID 42700391):** "You've been called frail — now ask the follow-up question," a short script for turning a frailty label into a conversation about comfort and planning, for a caregiver-advice column.
- **What nursing home staff and families actually value from a palliative care consultation (PMID 42742117):** Four things a palliative care visit should give a resident and family beyond fewer ambulance rides — usable as a checklist when touring or choosing a facility, for a consumer explainer or a senior-living trade brief on demonstrating program value.
- **In a blinded trial, cancer patients preferred AI's empathic words over a palliative care doctor's (PMID 42599269):** The uncomfortable finding that warmth, not accuracy, drove preference — and what that means for clinicians who think bluntness equals honesty, for a national health desk or a clinician-facing communication feature.
- **A tape measure around the calf, plus one blood test, helps predict survival in advanced cancer (PMID 42674364):** Why shrinking calves and unintended weight loss deserve a place on the appointment agenda, with careful handling of what a modest predictive tool can and cannot say about one person, for a consumer health explainer.
- **Pharmacy technicians visiting housebound elders at home cut falls and emergency contacts in a small pilot (PMID 42730948):** Twenty-three of 25 housebound patients had collectively missed 91 routine reviews — a story about the appointments people silently stop attending, and how to request a review at home, for a caregiver-advice column or local-news health segment.
- **Written End-of-Life Wishes Do Cut Hospital Transfers From Care Homes — But Not Always (PMID 42714187):** "Who reads my plan at 2 a.m.?" — the specific questions to ask a care home about where directives are stored and who decides in a crisis, for a consumer explainer, with a companion trade brief on after-hours decision pathways.
- **Why Some Hospitals Get Dying Cancer Patients Into Hospice and Others Don't (PMID 42734951):** Whether you get timely palliative care may depend on whether two teams down the hall trust each other — a system story with a patient-side action (ask your oncologist how closely they work with palliative care), for a health-policy section or senior-living trade piece on referral culture.
- **Palliative Care Doctors Are Warming to AI Predictions of How Long Patients Have Left (PMID 42463061):** Physicians favor tools most of them say they don't understand, including for hospice eligibility — a trade brief on governance and review of algorithm output, plus a consumer sidebar on asking where an estimate came from.
- **An IV Drug Widely Hoped to Relieve Severe Cancer Pain Failed to Beat Placebo (PMID 42536632):** How to weigh a proposed add-on infusion near the end of life — including what it costs in monitoring, transfers and time — for a consumer health explainer on asking about evidence and trade-offs.
- **Caring for a Loved One With Advanced Cancer in a War Zone (PMID 42728769):** Unmet needs for information and practical help tracked with caregiver depression and anxiety — the case that navigation and written information are treatment, not extras, and that women in the role carry more of the load, for a caregiving feature or hospice trade piece on routine caregiver screening.
- **A Palliative Care Clinic That Drains Fluid Buildup — Without an Eight-Day Hospital Stay (PMID 42722989):** For anyone whose recurring abdominal fluid keeps triggering admissions, the questions to ask about outpatient drainage or an indwelling drain, for a consumer health explainer or a local health-system segment.
- **Palliative Care Referrals Came Just 23 Days Before Death in Hospital Study (PMID 42713708):** "Can I meet the supportive care team now, alongside treatment?" — one sentence patients can use, paired with the referral triggers facilities could automate, for a caregiver column and a senior-living trade brief on auditing referral timing.
- **Fewer Pills at the End of Life (PMID 42519959):** A guided "what is this doing for me right now?" medication review — what to bring, what to ask, and why not to stop anything alone, for a consumer health explainer or a nursing-focused trade piece on who monitors after a drug is withdrawn.
- **Caregivers With Stronger Spiritual Well-Being Reported Lighter Burden in Palliative Care Study (PMID 42697036):** Chaplains and spiritual care counselors serve the caregiver too, not just the patient — a permission-giving piece for family caregivers, for a faith-and-aging or caregiving section.
- **What People Actually Say When They Choose Assisted Dying (PMID 42744336):** Requests reflected long-held wishes for control and validation more than uncontrolled pain — guidance on responding with curiosity rather than alarm when a parent or resident raises the subject, for a thoughtful caregiving feature or clinician-facing piece.
- **Shame About a Liver Diagnosis Tracks With Worse Symptoms and More Distress (PMID 42362166):** How the language around cirrhosis — self-inflicted versus serious chronic illness — may be adding to a patient's pain and low mood, for a consumer health explainer or a staff-training trade brief.
- **Routine Urine Drug Tests in Palliative Care: A Review Finds No Proof They Help (PMID 42362165):** What to ask when a seriously ill patient on opioids is handed a cup — why it's ordered, what happens with the result, what it costs — and how misread results can disrupt pain control, for a patient-advocacy column or hospice trade brief.
