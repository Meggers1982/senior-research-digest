### Study 1: Hearing Aids Didn't Prevent Dementia in a Large Trial — but Some Users' Cognitive Scores Got Better
**PMID:** 42804222 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Caveat detail not in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "dementia conversion rates were lower than expected"
- **Abstract says:** "The abstract reports conversion rates of 3.09% (intervention) vs 4.74% (control) but makes no statement about whether these were lower than anticipated."
- **Problem:** Unverifiable from abstract only — the claim about expected event rates may come from the full text or discussion but cannot be confirmed here.
- **Suggested fix:** "Either drop the phrase or attribute it (e.g., 'event rates in both groups were very low, limiting statistical power')."

### Study 6: An Alzheimer's Drug Boosted Word Recognition in Cochlear Implant Users — In a Very Small Pilot Trial
**PMID:** 42776967 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Device-use framing overstates what the trial showed** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the most proven lever is still the one both groups in this trial pulled: wearing the device more hours per day"
- **Abstract says:** ""Both groups were encouraged to increase their daily device use during the study... All study participants increased their device use, with a mean increase of 3.1 hr per day in both groups.""
- **Problem:** The trial recorded increased wear time but did not test or demonstrate that increased wear time improved outcomes; calling it 'the most proven lever' attributes evidence to this study that it does not contain.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to note that both arms increased wear time by about 3 hours daily, which confounds interpretation, and that wear-time benefit comes from other evidence, not this trial."

**Issue B. Imaging laterality omitted** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "brain imaging showed increased frontal cortex activation during listening in noise"
- **Abstract says:** ""DPZ increasing left frontal cortex activation during a speech-in-noise task (β = 3.721, p < 0.001)""
- **Problem:** The abstract specifies left frontal cortex; the digest generalizes to 'frontal cortex'.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add 'left' before frontal cortex."

### Study 7: When Hearing Fades, the Aging Brain Appears to Lean Harder on Vision
**PMID:** 41317239 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Omits counter-direction P1/N1 finding** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Adults with more hearing loss showed faster later-stage visual brain responses (shorter P2 latency) — a pattern consistent with the brain compensating by boosting visual processing"
- **Abstract says:** ""Participants with more hearing loss tended to have longer cortical P1 and N1 latencies in the visual evoked potential. However, the later cortical P2 response latency decreased with more hearing loss in agreement with compensatory plasticity.""
- **Problem:** The study found a mixed picture: earlier responses (P1, N1) were slower with more hearing loss, which the authors interpret as sensitivity to sensory decline rather than compensation. Reporting only the P2 result makes the compensation story look cleaner than it is.
- **Suggested fix:** "Note that earlier responses (P1, N1) were actually slower with more hearing loss, while only the later P2 response showed the compensatory pattern."

**Issue B. Temporal-cortex recruitment stimulus specificity dropped** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "along with evidence that the temporal cortex, normally devoted to sound, was being recruited for visual input"
- **Abstract says:** ""P2 responses for the non-speech stimulus showed greater cross-modal recruitment of the temporal cortex.""
- **Problem:** Cross-modal temporal recruitment was observed specifically for the non-speech stimulus, not generally — which slightly undercuts the digest's lip-reading framing.
- **Suggested fix:** "Specify that the temporal-cortex recruitment was seen for the non-speech visual stimulus."

### Study 8: Talking "clearly" helps people with hearing loss understand — but slowing down too much makes you sound robotic
**PMID:** 42757902 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline asserts an untested intelligibility benefit** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Talking "clearly" helps people with hearing loss understand — but slowing down too much makes you sound robotic"
- **Abstract says:** "The study measured perceived naturalness (rated by 28 normal-hearing adults) and acoustic variables only; intelligibility for listeners with hearing loss was not tested. The abstract refers to an "intelligibility-naturalness trade-off" as interpretation."
- **Problem:** The first half of the headline states a benefit this study did not measure; readers may assume it was demonstrated here.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reframe, e.g., 'Clear-speech training made talkers much slower — and listeners rated the result far less natural.'"

**Issue B. 'Patronizing' is an editorial addition** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "over-coaching the technique produces exaggerated, unnatural-sounding speech that can feel patronizing"
- **Abstract says:** ""A 30-min clear speech training reduced speech naturalness compared to HS and UCS"; nothing about perceived condescension or patient reactions."
- **Problem:** Listeners rated naturalness, not how patronizing or respectful the speech felt; the inference goes beyond the data.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say the trained speech was rated markedly less natural, and note that how it is received by listeners with hearing loss was not tested."

### Study 11: Think One Ear Is Worse? You're Probably Wrong, Hearing Study Finds
**PMID:** 42467662 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

**Issue A. 62% statistic misdescribed** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "62% misidentified which ear was worse — almost always exaggerating how bad the 'bad' ear was"
- **Abstract says:** "Sixty-two percent of patients misclassified the worse hearing ear, with a strong bias toward overestimation (94%). In contrast, 48% misclassified the better hearing ear, with a modest skew toward underestimation (58%)."
- **Problem:** In context (the survey quantified 'subjective individual ear hearing loss severity' for each ear), 'misclassified the worse hearing ear' means misestimating that ear's severity category, not picking the wrong ear. The digest converts a severity-estimation error into an ear-identification error, which is a different and stronger claim, and the second half of the sentence then describes severity overestimation, mixing the two.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to: '62% misjudged the severity of their worse ear, almost always overestimating how bad it was (94% of those errors), while 48% misjudged their better ear.'"

### Study 13: Hospitals Stock Pocket Hearing Amplifiers — and Barely Anyone Uses Them
**PMID:** 42677253 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Specific harms not in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "missed communication in hospital contributes to confusion, poor comprehension of discharge instructions and worse outcomes"
- **Abstract says:** "Hearing loss is experienced by two-thirds of hospitalized older adults and is associated with poor access to healthcare and negative health outcomes."
- **Problem:** The abstract supports 'poor access to healthcare and negative health outcomes' but does not mention confusion or discharge-instruction comprehension. Unverifiable from abstract only.

### Study 14: Stem Cell Infusions Didn't Change Older Adults' Hearing — and the Study Can't Say They Would
**PMID:** 42819495 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. 'Best current data' overstates a single exploratory substudy** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "know that the best current data show essentially no change in hearing thresholds over six months"
- **Abstract says:** "In this exploratory single-arm substudy, mean PTA-4 changed little over short-term follow-up... Without a concurrent audiologic comparator, the observed trajectory cannot be attributed causally to hUC-MSC administration."
- **Problem:** Calling one exploratory, single-arm, uncontrolled substudy 'the best current data' implies a broader evidence base than the abstract supports.
- **Suggested fix:** "Rephrase as 'the only published hearing data from such an infusion study — an uncontrolled substudy — showed essentially no change in thresholds over six months.'"

### Study 16: Hearing Loss Linked to Faster Cognitive Decline in 10-Year Study of 6,650 Older Adults
**PMID:** 42753934 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Interaction finding generalized** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Notably, a strong baseline cognitive score was less protective among those with hearing loss."
- **Abstract says:** "The MMSE-by-hearing interaction was significant for moderate decline (RRR 1.252, 95% CI 1.079-1.453), indicating a weaker inverse association between baseline MMSE and moderate-decline membership among participants with hearing loss."
- **Problem:** The interaction was significant only for moderate-decline membership; the digest presents it as a general effect across trajectories.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add the qualifier: 'a strong baseline cognitive score was less protective against moderate decline among those with hearing loss.'"

### Study 17: How Hard You Feel You're Working to Listen Tracks With How Well You Actually Hear
**PMID:** 42348455 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Strength of association overstated** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "lower self-reported listening workload was consistently associated with better speech recognition scores"
- **Abstract says:** "In general, lower levels of self-reported workload were associated with better speech recognition scores, including for audibility-adjusted and non-audibility-adjusted speech recognition tasks"
- **Problem:** The abstract says 'in general,' implying the pattern did not hold uniformly across all five outcomes; 'consistently' implies it did.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change to 'was generally associated with better speech recognition scores.'"

**Issue B. Timing of workload ratings** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "rate their perceived performance, effort, and frustration (using a modified NASA Task Load Index) immediately after completing standard speech recognition tests"
- **Abstract says:** "participants ... who completed a modified version of the ... NASA-TLX following the Northwestern University Auditory Test No. 6 (NU-6; n = 635), Speech Perception in Noise test (SPIN; n = 440), and Staggered Spondaic Word test (SSW; n = 455)"
- **Problem:** The abstract says the ratings followed each test but does not state they were taken 'immediately' after. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Drop 'immediately' and simply say the ratings were given after each speech recognition test."
