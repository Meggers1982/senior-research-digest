### 1. Loneliness in Macular Degeneration Isn't One Problem — It Follows Several Different Paths

**Journal:** *Innov Aging* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 16
**PMID:** 42847076 | **DOI:** 10.1093/geroni/igag102

**The study:** Researchers analyzed baseline data from 210 adults with age-related macular degeneration (average age 80) who were enrolled in a randomized trial, using a decision-tree machine-learning method rather than standard regression to map who ends up lonely. Satisfaction with one's social life was the single strongest dividing factor: those content with their social lives reported much less loneliness. When social satisfaction was low, optimism acted as a buffer, while severity of vision loss, anxiety, and emotional reactions to losing vision shaped other branches — and depressive symptoms appeared to override otherwise protective factors.

**Why it matters:** Loneliness among older adults with vision loss is common and tied to poorer health, and this analysis suggests there is no single fix — the right support depends on which pathway a person is on.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If vision loss is shrinking your world, it may be worth paying attention to how satisfied you feel with your social life — not just how many people you see — and discussing persistent low mood or anxiety with your clinician, since depression in particular appeared to swamp other protective factors in this analysis.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living staff and family caregivers: a one-size-fits-all social program may miss residents whose loneliness is driven by anxiety, depression, or grief over vision loss rather than lack of opportunity; screening for emotional response to vision loss could help target support.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional baseline data from a trial (observational, cannot establish causation); self-reported loneliness, mood, and social satisfaction; modest sample (N=210) drawn from trial volunteers with AMD, which may not generalize to all older adults with vision impairment.

### 2. Eye Injections for Diabetic Vision Loss: Patients Describe a Cycle of Hope and Dread

**Journal:** *Diabet Med* | **Published:** 2026 Nov
**PMID:** 42008767 | **DOI:** 10.1111/dme.70334

**The study:** Swedish researchers interviewed people with diabetic macular edema at 4 and 12 months after starting intravitreal anti-VEGF injections, analyzing their emotional, physical, and social responses. An overarching theme of 'health, hope, and worry' emerged: participants cycled between hope and anxiety around each injection, and experiences diverged sharply depending on whether vision improved. Trust in providers, continuity of care, and clear information were described as central to coping.

**Why it matters:** Anti-VEGF injections are among the most common ongoing treatments in older adults with diabetes, and this work shows the burden is emotional as much as clinical — something care teams can address directly.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're facing repeated eye injections, it's reasonable to ask your team exactly what visual outcome is realistic for you, what each visit will feel like, and who you can call when anxiety spikes — patients in this study said consistent information and a familiar clinician mattered most to getting through the course.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and diabetes care teams: injection days are a predictable stress point. Arranging the same escort, preparing for post-injection discomfort, and setting expectations that vision may stabilize rather than improve can reduce distress and help maintain adherence.

**Caveats:** Small qualitative study with no control group; self-reported experiences; single-country (Sweden) sample whose care context may not generalize to other health systems; findings describe experience, not treatment effectiveness.

### 3. Low-Contrast Obstacles Make Everyone Step More Dangerously — Even People With Good Vision

**Journal:** *Gait Posture* | **Published:** 2026 Oct
**PMID:** 42501620 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.gaitpost.2026.110296

**The study:** Researchers had 29 older adults — 15 with normal vision (average 78) and 14 with central vision loss (average 79) — walk a black-carpeted path and step over an obstacle that was either 1 cm or 19 cm tall and either low or high contrast, using motion capture to track their feet. Both groups lifted their lead foot lower over low-contrast obstacles than high-contrast ones, a riskier pattern that raises trip potential. Adults with central vision loss showed bigger adjustments in speed, foot clearance, and head angle for tall obstacles and tilted their heads down to compensate with short obstacles.

**Why it matters:** It points to a cheap, concrete fall-prevention lever: making thresholds, curbs, steps, and cords visually contrast with the floor rather than blending into it.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Look around your home for trip hazards that blend into the floor — a dark threshold on dark carpet, a low step, a cord — and add contrasting tape or paint; this study found even people with normal vision cleared low-contrast obstacles more dangerously without realizing it.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living operators and designers: contrast marking on stair edges, door thresholds, and level changes is a measurable safety intervention, not a decorating preference — and residents with macular degeneration may be compensating by looking down, which carries its own balance risk.

**Caveats:** Very small sample (N=29), single-center laboratory setting with artificial obstacles rather than real-world falls data, short-term measurement only; one author reports funding from the American Academy of Optometry Foundation and a consulting relationship with Ray Therapeutics, Inc.

### 4. Tamoxifen Can Damage the Retina — But Almost No One Gets Their Eyes Checked

**Journal:** *JAMA Ophthalmol* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 29
**PMID:** 42955022 | **DOI:** 10.1001/jamaophthalmol.2026.4646

**The study:** Using the US All of Us research database, researchers tracked 2,327 adults (mean age 54; 96% female) for a year after starting tamoxifen, a widely used breast cancer drug known to cause retinal toxicity. Only 7% had any eye care encounter within 12 months, and just 1.7% received retinal imaging (OCT or fundus photography). People with preexisting glaucoma were nearly four times as likely to be seen, and rates of eye care were no higher than among women taking aromatase inhibitors, which do not carry the same retinal risk.

**Why it matters:** There is no formal US screening guideline for tamoxifen retinopathy, and this shows that in practice, eye monitoring is essentially not happening for long-term users — including older women on multi-year regimens.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you are taking or starting tamoxifen, it's worth asking your oncologist and an eye doctor whether a baseline eye exam makes sense for you and what visual symptoms — blurred central vision, distortion, color changes — should prompt a prompt call, since routine monitoring is currently rare.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and care coordinators: medication reviews for older women on long-term breast cancer therapy should flag tamoxifen as a drug with known eye toxicity, and new visual complaints shouldn't be written off as ordinary aging or cataracts.

**Caveats:** Retrospective observational cohort using billing and encounter codes — it measures eye care use, not actual retinopathy rates, and cannot establish that screening would improve outcomes; All of Us participants may not represent the general US population; a coauthor reports extensive industry payments from ophthalmic pharmaceutical companies outside this work.

### 5. Beyond AREDS: Experts Weigh What's Actually Proven for Supplements and Aging Eyes

**Journal:** *Front Aging* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 12
**PMID:** 42936152 | **DOI:** 10.3389/fragi.2026.1912337

**The study:** Clinicians from Mass Eye and Ear and collaborators reviewed preclinical and clinical evidence for metabolic supplements — NAD+ boosters, citicoline, pyruvate, and omega-3 fatty acids — in macular degeneration, diabetic retinopathy, and glaucoma, framing these diseases as forms of accelerated retinal aging. They conclude that the AREDS formulations remain the main supplement with demonstrated benefit, and only in selected AMD populations. Newer metabolic agents show biological plausibility and early biomarker or functional signals, particularly in glaucoma and diabetic eye disease, but long-term efficacy is unproven.

**Why it matters:** Eye-health supplements are heavily marketed to older adults, and this review draws a clear line between the one formulation with trial evidence and a growing shelf of products that don't yet have it.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Before spending money on an eye supplement, ask your eye doctor whether you actually fit the AREDS criteria — and treat NAD+ boosters, citicoline and similar products as unproven for now, not as substitutes for monitoring and treatment of glaucoma or diabetic eye disease.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and senior living wellness staff: supplement purchases are a common source of out-of-pocket spending among residents with vision concerns; a medication and supplement review with the eye care provider can separate evidence-based use from marketing.

**Caveats:** This is a narrative review, not new original data; it synthesizes studies of varying quality including animal work, and the authors themselves emphasize that long-term efficacy of the newer agents remains unproven.

### 6. Glaucoma Surgery Linked to Lower Dementia Risk Than Eye Drops Alone in Huge Patient Database

**Journal:** *Int J Geriatr Psychiatry* | **Published:** 2026 Sep
**PMID:** 42693831 | **DOI:** 10.1002/gps.70252

**The study:** Researchers used the TriNetX multicenter health records database to compare two groups of glaucoma patients aged 40 and older — those whose first definitive treatment was surgery versus those who started long-term medication — matching 51,923 people in each group (mean age 64, 55.3% female) on background characteristics. Over follow-up through July 2026, the surgical group had a 19% lower rate of all-cause dementia (HR 0.81) and a 47% lower rate of vascular dementia (HR 0.53); the reduction in Alzheimer disease was not statistically significant. The surgical group also had more recorded visual loss (HR 1.52), which the authors attribute to those patients having more advanced glaucoma to begin with.

**Why it matters:** The 2024 Lancet Commission named visual impairment a modifiable dementia risk factor, and this is among the first very large analyses to ask whether how glaucoma is treated — not just whether it's treated — tracks with later cognitive outcomes.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have glaucoma and your pressures aren't well controlled on drops, this is a reasonable thing to raise at your next appointment: ask your ophthalmologist where you stand on the medication-versus-surgery decision and what your options are. Don't read this as proof that surgery protects your brain — the people who got surgery differed from those who didn't in ways records can't fully capture.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and senior living staff: this adds eye care to the list of specialty appointments worth protecting on a resident's calendar, and it's a reminder to track whether glaucoma drops are actually being instilled correctly — a common and quiet failure point in assisted living that can drive disease progression.

**Caveats:** Observational database study — cannot establish causation; patients selected for surgery likely differ systematically from those kept on drops (confounding by indication, visible in the higher visual-loss rate); diagnoses derived from coded health records rather than direct examination; no information on adherence to eye drops.

### 7. Two Decades After Radiation Plaque Treatment for Eye Melanoma, Most Patients Still Have Useful Vision

**Journal:** *Ophthalmology* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 14
**PMID:** 42874810 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.ophtha.2026.10.009

**The study:** A retrospective cohort at a single New York eye cancer center followed 170 patients with choroidal melanoma treated with palladium-103 plaque brachytherapy for at least 10 continuous years. Local tumor control was 100%, and only one eye (0.6%) was removed. Vision of 20/200 or better was retained by 92.7% at 12 years, 87.0% at 15 years, and 63.7% at 20 years; tumors sitting close to the fovea or the optic nerve head produced the worst visual outcomes, and radiation retinopathy was managed with anti-VEGF injections, steroids, and laser.

**Why it matters:** Choroidal melanoma is diagnosed most often in older adults, and the fear of losing the eye or going blind drives many treatment conversations — this gives patients and clinicians concrete 10-, 15-, and 20-year numbers to work with.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you've been diagnosed with a melanoma inside the eye, ask specifically where the tumor sits relative to your central vision and optic nerve — location was the strongest predictor of how vision held up decades later, and it should shape both your expectations and your plan for long-term monitoring of radiation retinopathy.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For caregivers: survivorship here is measured in decades, which means years of follow-up appointments and possible repeated intravitreal injections. Building that into transportation planning and care coordination matters more than the initial treatment decision alone.

**Caveats:** Retrospective, single-center cohort at a highly specialized referral practice led by the technique's proponents — results may not generalize to other settings; survivors with 10+ years of continuous follow-up are a selected group; no comparison arm against other treatments such as enucleation or proton therapy.

### 8. A Three-Surgery Combination Keeps Eye Pressure Down for Three Years in Advanced Angle-Closure Glaucoma

**Journal:** *Br J Ophthalmol* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 5
**PMID:** 42833871 | **DOI:** 10.1136/bjo-2026-329527

**The study:** A multicenter prospective study across Chinese eye centers followed 70 eyes from 58 patients with advanced primary angle-closure glaucoma who still had clear lenses and underwent a combined procedure — surgical peripheral iridectomy, goniosynechialysis, and goniotomy — for 36 months. Mean eye pressure fell from 29.4 to 16.4 mm Hg (a 40.7% drop) and the number of glaucoma medications fell from 2.0 to 0.7. Visual field loss stayed stable, with no vision-threatening complications; 21.4% of eyes lost two or more Snellen lines, mostly because of cataract developing afterward. Notably, older age predicted higher three-year surgical success.

**Why it matters:** Angle-closure glaucoma is a major cause of irreversible blindness in older adults, especially of East Asian ancestry, and this offers a lens-sparing alternative for patients who don't yet need cataract surgery — while cutting the daily drop burden by about two-thirds.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have advanced angle-closure glaucoma and are struggling with multiple daily eye drops, it's worth asking your specialist whether angle-based combined surgery is an option for you — and asking directly about the chance of needing cataract surgery afterward, since that was the main reason vision slipped in this study.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living staff and family caregivers: a procedure that reduces a resident from two medications to less than one meaningfully reduces the daily administration load and the risk of missed doses — a practical argument for being present at the surgical consultation.

**Caveats:** Small sample (58 patients, 70 eyes), observational with no control group or comparison surgery, conducted entirely in Chinese centers in a population with high angle-closure prevalence — findings may not generalize; some outcomes depend on surgeon expertise at specialized centers.

### 9. Newly Identified Gene Explains Some Cases of Adult-Onset Retinitis Pigmentosa

**Journal:** *JAMA Ophthalmol* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 29
**PMID:** 42955015 | **DOI:** 10.1001/jamaophthalmol.2026.4692

**The study:** An international team screened roughly 4,500 people with genetically unexplained retinal disease and found six variants in a gene called PRKCZ in 13 affected individuals from 10 unrelated families, inherited from both parents. Affected people developed night blindness and shrinking peripheral vision starting between ages 18 and 49, eventually losing central vision; four also developed adult-onset sensorineural hearing loss. Laboratory work in patient cells showed the variants knock out a long, retina-specific version of the gene, disrupting cell structures and mitochondria.

**Why it matters:** A substantial share of people with inherited retinal degeneration never get a genetic answer, and naming a new gene opens the door to accurate diagnosis, family counseling, and eligibility for future gene-targeted therapies and trials.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you've lost night vision and peripheral vision as an adult and were told the cause was unknown, genetic testing may be worth revisiting — the list of known genes keeps growing, and a re-analysis of older test results sometimes produces an answer years later. Mention any hearing loss, since it may be part of the same condition.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families: this is an inherited condition requiring two copies of the variant, so a diagnosis has implications for siblings and for genetic counseling across the family — and the hearing-loss link means a combined vision-and-hearing plan may be needed sooner than expected.

**Caveats:** Case series of only 13 individuals with no control group; no treatment exists for this gene variant yet; several authors report ties to RNA-therapy companies and vision-research foundations, including one who is a founder and shareholder of a retinal-therapy spin-off; findings require independent replication.

### 10. Trouble Reading Up Close Linked to Weaker Memory Scores in Large Rural Study

**Journal:** *Br J Ophthalmol* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 9
**PMID:** 42716809 | **DOI:** 10.1136/bjo-2025-329310

**The study:** Researchers in the Handan Eye Follow-up Study examined 3,650 adults aged 50 and older in rural China who completed both vision testing and cognitive assessment with the Mini-Mental State Examination. Near vision impairment was strikingly common (92%) and distance vision impairment affected 27.5%. After full adjustment for demographic and health covariates, near vision impairment remained associated with roughly 3.4-fold higher odds of poorer cognitive scores, while the distance-vision association faded once all covariates were accounted for.

**Why it matters:** Near vision loss is usually correctable with a simple pair of reading glasses or cataract surgery, yet it was nearly universal in this population — raising the possibility that an inexpensive fix could support both daily function and cognitive engagement.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you find yourself avoiding reading, puzzles, recipes or your phone because the print is blurry, get your near vision checked — uncorrected reading vision is extremely common, easy to test, and often fixable with glasses. This study cannot prove better glasses protect memory, but there is little downside to being able to see what you are doing.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living staff and family members: cognitive screening results can be distorted when a resident literally cannot see the test. Consider confirming that reading glasses are current and available before interpreting a low MMSE score as decline.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional observational design — cannot establish that vision loss causes cognitive impairment, and reverse causation or shared risk factors are plausible. Cognitive testing by MMSE partly depends on near vision, which may inflate the association. Rural Chinese population with very high uncorrected refractive error may not generalize to settings with routine eye care.

### 11. Ten Common Health Risks — Including Vision and Hearing Loss — Add Up in the Aging Brain

**Journal:** *Geroscience* | **Published:** 2026 Aug 26
**PMID:** 42645764 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11357-026-02473-8

**The study:** Using data from 38,414 older adults in the National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center database, researchers built a simple score counting ten modifiable risk factors: hypertension, diabetes, high cholesterol, alcohol misuse, smoking, depression, obesity, hearing loss, vision loss and low education. Each additional risk factor was associated with worse global cognition, poorer delayed recall and semantic fluency, greater clinical severity, and higher odds of white matter hyperintensities, brain infarcts and hippocampal atrophy on MRI. Hippocampal atrophy showed the strongest indirect pathway linking risk burden to cognitive performance.

**Why it matters:** It reframes sensory loss as a brain-health issue rather than a standalone inconvenience, and suggests that addressing several modest risks together may matter more than perfecting any one of them.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Think of your brain-health risks as a running tally rather than a pass/fail test: blood pressure, blood sugar, cholesterol, mood, drinking, smoking, weight — and your hearing and vision. Ask your doctor which items on that list are currently unaddressed in your case, since each one you can subtract appears to count.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For care teams and families, this supports a multidomain checklist approach at annual reviews — pairing cardiometabolic management with hearing aid and eyeglass checks — rather than treating sensory care as cosmetic or optional.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional observational data from a memory-clinic-based research registry; it cannot establish causation or show that reducing risk factors changes brain outcomes. The NACC population is enriched for cognitive concerns and is not a representative community sample. Several risk factors were self-reported binary variables.

### 12. After Wet-AMD-Like Lesions Clear, Half of Eyes Stay Quiet for Two Years — and Half Come Back

**Journal:** *JAMA Ophthalmol* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 17
**PMID:** 42752539 | **DOI:** 10.1001/jamaophthalmol.2026.3927

**The study:** In a prospective cohort at four Thai university hospitals, 81 previously untreated patients (mean age 64) with polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy — a major global cause of vision loss — received three monthly aflibercept injections, with three more if needed. Only 40% achieved complete polypoidal regression; among 26 of those eyes followed, half had a recurrence of fluid within two years. Among recurrences, median vision change was a loss of 3.5 letters, but 21% lost 10 or more letters and 36% developed new bleeding.

**Why it matters:** It quantifies the real risk of pausing injections once the eye looks dry, information patients weary of monthly needles need when discussing whether and how to stretch treatment intervals.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If your retina specialist says your eye looks dry and suggests moving to as-needed injections, ask what your personal recurrence risk looks like and how often you will still need monitoring scans — in this study, stopping treatment did not mean stopping follow-up, and about half of eyes flared again within two years.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families arranging transportation: a switch to 'as-needed' injections reduces procedures but not appointments. Monthly-to-bimonthly OCT monitoring visits remained essential to catching recurrences before vision was lost, so transportation support should not be scaled back.

**Caveats:** Small N (81 enrolled, only 26 regressed eyes analyzed for recurrence), yielding very wide confidence intervals; no control group receiving continued fixed dosing; single-country, four-center Asian cohort where this condition is most prevalent, limiting generalizability. Industry funding and conflicts: grants from Bayer during the study, with multiple authors reporting fees from Bayer, Roche, Novartis, AbbVie and Alcon.

### 13. Eye Damage Is Common and Often Missed in a Widely Used Breast Cancer Drug Class

**Journal:** *Br J Ophthalmol* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 22
**PMID:** 42331364 | **DOI:** 10.1136/bjo-2026-329526

**The study:** Researchers prospectively followed 21 patients with HER2-positive breast cancer receiving HER2-targeted antibody-drug conjugates, using detailed ocular surface testing including confocal microscopy and, in selected cases, electron microscopy. Ocular surface toxicity appeared in 86% of patients, at an average of 28 days after starting therapy, presenting as dry eye, blurred vision and a distinctive swirl-shaped corneal deposit pattern along with loss of corneal nerve fibers and reduced corneal sensitivity. Damage was dose- and time-related and partially reversible, with corneal structure trending toward recovery after 12 treatment cycles.

**Why it matters:** Many older women on these cancer drugs may attribute blurry, gritty eyes to aging or chemotherapy generally, when the cause is a recognized, monitorable and partly reversible drug effect.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you are starting a HER2-targeted antibody-drug conjugate for breast cancer, ask your oncologist about a baseline eye exam and tell the team promptly about dry, gritty or blurry eyes — symptoms typically began about a month in, and because corneal nerves go numb, your eyes may be more damaged than they feel.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For caregivers accompanying someone through cancer treatment: add eye symptoms to the side-effect list you report at each cycle, and flag reduced reading ability or new difficulty driving, since numbed corneal nerves mean discomfort is an unreliable warning sign.

**Caveats:** Small N (21 patients), single-center, no control group, and no randomization; follow-up limited to the treatment course so long-term reversibility is unknown. Findings describe a specific cancer-drug population and do not apply to ocular surface disease generally.

### 14. Childhood Hardship May Shape Late-Life Memory — Partly Through Untreated Vision Loss

**Journal:** *Gerontologist* | **Published:** 2026 Aug 5
**PMID:** 42371784 | **DOI:** 10.1093/geront/gnag147

**The study:** Researchers analyzed data from 20,010 Chinese adults aged 40 and older in the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study, including its 2014 Life History Survey, using structural equation modeling to trace how 11 different adverse childhood experiences relate to later-life cognitive function. Emotional abuse and exposure to community violence were directly linked to poorer cognition decades later. Physical neglect and parental divorce or death affected cognition indirectly — through lower educational attainment, depression, and untreated vision loss.

**Why it matters:** It identifies untreated vision loss as one of the modifiable links between early hardship and later cognitive decline, meaning something as ordinary as getting an eye exam and the right glasses or cataract surgery sits on the pathway to brain health.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you grew up with hardship, that history does not seal your cognitive fate — but it may raise your risk. One of the links researchers found runs through vision that was never properly corrected, so keeping up with eye exams, updated glasses, and discussing cataract evaluation with your doctor addresses a factor you can actually act on.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For care staff and family members, a resident's childhood history is rarely in the chart — but this analysis suggests it can be a marker of cognitive risk worth noting, and that untreated sensory impairment in that group deserves particular scrutiny during intake and annual reviews.

**Caveats:** Observational design cannot establish causation; childhood experiences were recalled retrospectively by older adults, which invites recall bias; vision loss and other risk factors were largely self-reported; findings come from a Chinese cohort and may not generalize elsewhere; two ACE categories showed unexplained positive associations with cognition, suggesting measurement problems.

### 15. A Routine Eye Scan May Flag Which Diabetic Eyes Are Headed for Trouble

**Journal:** *Invest Ophthalmol Vis Sci* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 1
**PMID:** 42752554 | **DOI:** 10.1167/iovs.67.11.35

**The study:** Researchers followed 190 eyes from 121 patients with diabetic retinopathy but no macular swelling, measuring inner and outer retinal volume from baseline macular OCT scans and tracking outcomes for two years. About a quarter of eyes (25.3%) progressed — meaning worsening on angiography, loss of 10 or more letters of vision, vitreous hemorrhage, or the need for laser or anti-VEGF injections. Higher outer retinal volume at baseline independently predicted progression even after adjusting for retinopathy severity, hypertension, age, and lens status, while inner retinal volume predicted nothing.

**Why it matters:** OCT scanning is already standard in most eye clinics, so this measurement could help identify which people with diabetes need closer monitoring or earlier treatment before vision is lost.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have diabetes, ask your eye doctor what your OCT scan shows beyond whether there's swelling — this research suggests the thickness of the outer retina carries information about your two-year risk, and that may be a reason to come back sooner rather than waiting for symptoms.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families coordinating diabetes care, this supports keeping dilated eye exams and OCT imaging on the calendar even when vision seems fine and there is no macular edema — a quarter of these eyes progressed within two years.

**Caveats:** Observational study with no control group; fewer than 200 eyes and only 121 patients; required manual correction of scan segmentation, which may limit real-world reproducibility; senior author discloses consulting relationships with Roche/Genentech, Boehringer Ingelheim, RegenXbio, and 3Helix; two-year follow-up is relatively short for a slowly progressive disease.

### 16. Night Vision Fades First in a Rare Retinal Disease That Mirrors Macular Degeneration

**Journal:** *Invest Ophthalmol Vis Sci* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 1
**PMID:** 42734305 | **DOI:** 10.1167/iovs.67.11.24

**The study:** Swiss and UK researchers tracked 25 patients with genetically confirmed Malattia Leventinese (Doyne honeycomb retinal dystrophy), median age 60, over two years using detailed retinal imaging and microperimetry testing of light sensitivity. Total retinal thickness appeared stable — but only because deposits were building up at the same rate that photoreceptor layers were thinning underneath. Dim-light (scotopic) sensitivity declined measurably while standard mid-light testing stayed flat, with the sharpest functional losses occurring early in the disease.

**Why it matters:** Because this inherited condition closely resembles dry age-related macular degeneration, the finding that dim-light vision deteriorates before conventional measures change could reshape how both diseases are monitored and how new treatments are tested.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you notice you are struggling more in dim restaurants, stairwells, or at dusk while your standard eye chart results look unchanged, that is worth raising — this study suggests dim-light sensitivity can decline well before routine tests register a problem.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living operators and caregivers, this is a practical argument for treating complaints about dim-light difficulty as real clinical information, and for auditing lighting in hallways, bathrooms, and stairways rather than assuming good chart vision means safe navigation.

**Caveats:** Very small sample (N=25) of a rare inherited disease; findings about age-related macular degeneration are inferred by analogy, not directly tested; two-year follow-up; multiple authors disclose funding or consulting ties to Bayer, Roche/Genentech, iCare/CenterVue, Apellis, and Johnson & Johnson.

### 17. A Chemotherapy Drug Injected Into the Cornea Shrinks Sight-Threatening Blood Vessels

**Journal:** *Br J Ophthalmol* | **Published:** 2026 Aug 20
**PMID:** 42215273 | **DOI:** 10.1136/bjo-2026-329997

**The study:** Surgeons at Moorfields Eye Hospital reviewed 39 procedures using mitomycin C to shut down abnormal corneal blood vessels — a condition that causes scarring, fat deposits, and vision loss — delivered either directly into the vessels (MICE) or injected into the corneal tissue (MIST), with a median 12 months of follow-up. Direct vessel cannulation succeeded in only 15% of cases, so the simpler intrastromal injection was used alone in 70%. Vessel regression was documented in 59% to 81% of cases depending on the assessment method, and four patients went on to successful corneal transplants without vessel recurrence.

**Why it matters:** Corneal neovascularization has long been considered difficult to treat and is a major reason corneal transplants fail; an accessible injection technique could open the door to transplant for patients previously considered too high risk.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have been told abnormal blood vessels in your cornea make you a poor candidate for a corneal transplant, it may be worth asking a corneal specialist whether mitomycin C injection could stabilize your eye first — this is still an emerging technique, but it is being used at major centers.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families supporting someone with long-standing corneal disease, this is a reminder that a past 'not a transplant candidate' verdict is not necessarily permanent, and a fresh specialist opinion may be warranted.

**Caveats:** Retrospective case series with no control group, single-center, and small (39 procedures); outcome measures disagreed substantially with each other, with surgeon-reported results most favorable; two serious complications occurred involving drug migration into the anterior chamber in thinned or previously perforated corneas; median follow-up only 12 months.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**
- Nine studies carry over from the previous run, and none of them reverse. The vision–cognition cluster holds: **Ten Common Health Risks — Including Vision and Hearing Loss — Add Up in the Aging Brain** (PMID 42645764), **Trouble Reading Up Close Linked to Weaker Memory Scores in Large Rural Study** (PMID 42716809), and **Childhood Hardship May Shape Late-Life Memory — Partly Through Untreated Vision Loss** (PMID 42371784) repeat the same conclusions as their counterparts last run (PMIDs 42645764, 42716809, 42371784), with near vision again the association that survives full adjustment.
- The fall-prevention and treatment-monitoring threads also recur intact: **Low-Contrast Obstacles Make Everyone Step More Dangerously — Even People With Good Vision** (PMID 42501620), **After Wet-AMD-Like Lesions Clear, Half of Eyes Stay Quiet for Two Years — and Half Come Back** (PMID 42752539), **A Routine Eye Scan May Flag Which Diabetic Eyes Are Headed for Trouble** (PMID 42752554), **Glaucoma Surgery Linked to Lower Dementia Risk Than Eye Drops Alone in Huge Patient Database** (PMID 42693831), **Eye Damage Is Common and Often Missed in a Widely Used Breast Cancer Drug Class** (PMID 42331364), and **A Chemotherapy Drug Injected Into the Cornea Shrinks Sight-Threatening Blood Vessels** (PMID 42215273) all restate the findings reported in the previous digest under the same PMIDs.

**Advances**
- The drug-toxicity thread that entered the digest last run as a single item now has a second, much larger data point. **Tamoxifen Can Damage the Retina — But Almost No One Gets Their Eyes Checked** (PMID 42955022) adds 2,327 patients and a hard monitoring number — 1.7% received retinal imaging in a year — to the small prospective cornea series in **Nearly 9 in 10 Breast Cancer Patients on Newer Targeted Drugs Developed Eye Surface Damage** (PMID 42331364, repeated this run as PMID 42331364). Together they move the question from "does this drug class hurt eyes" to "why is nobody looking."
- **After Wet-AMD-Like Lesions Clear, Half of Eyes Stay Quiet for Two Years — and Half Come Back** (PMID 42752539) is framed more conservatively this run, leading with the fact that only 40% of eyes achieved complete regression at all — a denominator the earlier write-up (PMID 42752539) left in the background.
- **Eye Injections for Diabetic Vision Loss: Patients Describe a Cycle of Hope and Dread** (PMID 42008767) puts patient voice behind the logistics-heavy pipeline review from the previous digest, **Beyond the Needle: What's Coming Next for Macular Degeneration and Diabetic Eye Disease** (PMID 41076606): the injection burden is emotional as well as a transportation problem.

**Counterpoints**
- Last run's optimism about alternatives to the needle meets a sober correction. **Beyond AREDS: Experts Weigh What's Actually Proven for Supplements and Aging Eyes** (PMID 42936152) concludes that outside selected AMD patients taking AREDS formulations, the supplement shelf has no demonstrated benefit — a useful brake on the "something new is coming" framing in **Beyond the Needle** (PMID 41076606).

**New themes**
- Emotional and social consequences of vision loss get their own evidence base this run: **Loneliness in Macular Degeneration Isn't One Problem — It Follows Several Different Paths** (PMID 42847076) and **Eye Injections for Diabetic Vision Loss: Patients Describe a Cycle of Hope and Dread** (PMID 42008767). The previous digest's closest relative was the audiobook study (PMID 42500929), which does not appear this run.
- Surgical and survivorship outcomes over long horizons are new: **Two Decades After Radiation Plaque Treatment for Eye Melanoma, Most Patients Still Have Useful Vision** (PMID 42874810) and **A Three-Surgery Combination Keeps Eye Pressure Down for Three Years in Advanced Angle-Closure Glaucoma** (PMID 42833871).
- Also new: inherited disease genetics in **Newly Identified Gene Explains Some Cases of Adult-Onset Retinitis Pigmentosa** (PMID 42955015), and functional measurement beyond the eye chart in **Night Vision Fades First in a Rare Retinal Disease That Mirrors Macular Degeneration** (PMID 42734305).
- Dropped out of this batch: the global burden analyses on women's blindness and diabetic retinopathy (PMIDs 42521465, 42476825), overlapping eye disease (PMID 41249002), and the American Indian dementia risk study (PMID 42635108).

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Three studies in this batch put the eye squarely inside cancer care, and show a monitoring gap running through it. **Tamoxifen Can Damage the Retina — But Almost No One Gets Their Eyes Checked** (PMID 42955022) found that only 7% of people starting a drug with known retinal toxicity had any eye care encounter in a year and 1.7% had retinal imaging; **Eye Damage Is Common and Often Missed in a Widely Used Breast Cancer Drug Class** (PMID 42331364) found ocular surface damage in 86% of patients on HER2-targeted antibody-drug conjugates, arriving around a month in and partly hidden because the corneal nerves go numb; and **Two Decades After Radiation Plaque Treatment for Eye Melanoma, Most Patients Still Have Useful Vision** (PMID 42874810) shows the flip side — cancer survivorship measured in decades, with radiation retinopathy managed by years of injections and follow-up.

**Why pitch this now:** Cancer treatment for older adults is increasingly long-term and outpatient, and these studies together show the eye is the organ most likely to be quietly harmed and least likely to be checked — a gap that no single oncology or eye visit currently owns.

**Angle:** A reported feature on who is supposed to be watching an older cancer patient's eyes, built around the practical questions a patient or family member can ask at the start of treatment and at each cycle, with a second strand on survivors whose eye follow-up outlasts their cancer care. Written for older adults in or past cancer treatment and the family members who go to appointments with them, with a shorter trade read for care coordinators and medication-review staff.

**Potential headlines:**
- The Eye Exam That Almost No One on This Breast Cancer Drug Gets
- Your Eyes Can Be Hurt by Cancer Treatment Before They Start to Hurt
- When Cancer Treatment Ends and the Eye Appointments Don't

**Potential outlets:**
- Next Avenue — the piece is fundamentally about navigating a gap between two specialists over years, which matches their human-centered reporting on managing care in later life.
- AARP — a large share of readers are on, or caring for someone on, multi-year breast cancer therapy, and "ask for a baseline eye exam" is clean service journalism.
- Cure — written for cancer patients and survivors specifically, and this side-effect-and-surveillance story is exactly the kind of under-covered treatment detail its audience seeks.
- McKnight's Senior Living — medication review and symptom reporting at the community level is where a flagged drug-eye interaction would actually be caught; frames tamoxifen and ADCs as items for the resident med review.

## Story Ideas by Study

- **Loneliness in Macular Degeneration Isn't One Problem — It Follows Several Different Paths (PMID 42847076):** Why bussing someone to bingo doesn't fix loneliness — screening for grief, anxiety, and low mood around vision loss instead of just counting social opportunities, for a caregiver-advice column or a senior-living activities and wellness trade brief.
- **Eye Injections for Diabetic Vision Loss: Patients Describe a Cycle of Hope and Dread (PMID 42008767):** A first-person-driven piece on injection day — what it actually feels like, why the same escort and the same clinician matter, and the questions that set realistic expectations, for a consumer health explainer or a diabetes-focused patient publication.
- **Low-Contrast Obstacles Make Everyone Step More Dangerously — Even People With Good Vision (PMID 42501620):** A walk-the-house checklist on contrast tape for thresholds, stair edges, and flooring transitions, paired with an operator version arguing contrast belongs in the fall-risk audit, for a consumer home-safety feature plus a senior-living trade companion.
- **Tamoxifen Can Damage the Retina — But Almost No One Gets Their Eyes Checked (PMID 42955022):** The specific symptoms that should prompt a call — blurred central vision, distortion, color change — and how to ask an oncologist for a baseline exam when no guideline requires one, for a consumer health explainer or women's health section.
- **Beyond AREDS: Experts Weigh What's Actually Proven for Supplements and Aging Eyes (PMID 42936152):** A buyer's-guide piece separating the one formulation with trial evidence from the NAD+ and citicoline products marketed to people with glaucoma and diabetic eye disease, for a consumer health or money-and-spending section.
- **Glaucoma Surgery Linked to Lower Dementia Risk Than Eye Drops Alone in Huge Patient Database (PMID 42693831):** Lead with the quiet failure point — drops that never reach the eye because of tremor, arthritis, or a missed med pass — and what a frank pressure-control conversation sounds like, for a caregiver-advice column or senior-living clinical brief.
- **Two Decades After Radiation Plaque Treatment for Eye Melanoma, Most Patients Still Have Useful Vision (PMID 42874810):** Concrete 10-, 15-, and 20-year vision numbers for a diagnosis people assume means losing the eye, plus what decades of follow-up asks of a family, for a consumer health explainer or cancer-survivorship outlet.
- **A Three-Surgery Combination Keeps Eye Pressure Down for Three Years in Advanced Angle-Closure Glaucoma (PMID 42833871):** Cutting a resident from two daily drops to less than one is a measurable reduction in med-pass workload and missed doses — plus the cataract trade-off patients should ask about, for a senior-living trade brief with a consumer sidebar.
- **Newly Identified Gene Explains Some Cases of Adult-Onset Retinitis Pigmentosa (PMID 42955015):** Why a negative genetic test from years ago is worth re-analyzing, and why hearing loss belongs in the conversation, for a consumer health explainer or a patient-community publication for inherited retinal disease.
- **Trouble Reading Up Close Linked to Weaker Memory Scores in Large Rural Study (PMID 42716809):** Check the reading glasses before you interpret the memory test — a practical warning for anyone administering or receiving cognitive screening, for a caregiver-advice column or senior-living clinical brief.
- **Ten Common Health Risks — Including Vision and Hearing Loss — Add Up in the Aging Brain (PMID 42645764):** Pitch the bundled annual brain-health review that books vision and hearing checks alongside blood pressure, blood sugar, and mood, rather than four separate errands, for a consumer health explainer or a wellness-program trade brief.
- **After Wet-AMD-Like Lesions Clear, Half of Eyes Stay Quiet for Two Years — and Half Come Back (PMID 42752539):** "A pause in injections is not a discharge" — what to ask about scan intervals and which symptoms warrant an urgent call, for a consumer health explainer or care-coordination brief.
- **Eye Damage Is Common and Often Missed in a Widely Used Breast Cancer Drug Class (PMID 42331364):** A "nobody warned me" piece on gritty, blurry eyes about a month into HER2-targeted therapy, with the counterintuitive detail that numbed corneal nerves make comfort an unreliable gauge, for a consumer health explainer or an oncology-nursing trade brief.
- **Childhood Hardship May Shape Late-Life Memory — Partly Through Untreated Vision Loss (PMID 42371784):** For care managers and social workers: a difficult early-life history is a prompt to check the fixable links first, starting with whether the person has ever had a proper eye exam, for a care-management or social work trade publication.
- **A Routine Eye Scan May Flag Which Diabetic Eyes Are Headed for Trouble (PMID 42752554):** The scan you already get may carry two-year risk information — a case for keeping dilated exams on the calendar when vision feels fine, for a consumer diabetes explainer or care-coordination brief.
- **Night Vision Fades First in a Rare Retinal Disease That Mirrors Macular Degeneration (PMID 42734305):** Take "I can't see in dim light" seriously even when the eye chart is unchanged — and audit hallway, bathroom, and stairwell lighting accordingly, for a senior-living operations brief with a consumer sidebar on home lighting.
- **A Chemotherapy Drug Injected Into the Cornea Shrinks Sight-Threatening Blood Vessels (PMID 42215273):** For people told they are poor corneal transplant candidates, why a fresh specialist opinion may be worth it — and why thin or previously perforated corneas change the risk math, for a consumer health explainer or patient-community outlet.
