### 1. A popular anti-aging supplement made Parkinson's slightly worse in a large trial

**Journal:** *JAMA* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 8
**PMID:** 42848371 | **DOI:** 10.1001/jama.2026.18828

**The study:** In the NOPARK trial, 410 people with Parkinson disease diagnosed within the previous two years (mean age 65.6, 34% women) at 11 Norwegian centers were randomized to 500 mg of nicotinamide riboside twice daily or an identical placebo for 52 weeks. Nicotinamide riboside is an NAD+ precursor sold widely as an over-the-counter anti-aging supplement and had been promoted as a possible disease-modifying therapy based on lab and early-phase work. After a year, the supplement group's total MDS-UPDRS score worsened by 2.45 points while the placebo group's barely changed, an adjusted difference of 2.72 points favoring placebo, and nonmotor symptom burden also worsened more with the supplement. Serious adverse events were actually less common in the supplement group (8.3% vs 14.1%).

**Why it matters:** This is a rigorous, phase 3 answer on a supplement many older adults are already buying for brain and energy health, and the answer is not just "no benefit" but a signal of slightly worse symptoms in early Parkinson disease.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have Parkinson disease and are taking nicotinamide riboside, NAD+ boosters, or similar "cellular energy" supplements, this trial gives you a concrete reason to bring the bottle to your next neurology appointment and ask whether it's worth continuing — the best evidence to date shows no benefit and a modest worsening of symptoms.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and senior living staff who often don't know what over-the-counter supplements a resident takes, this is a reminder to include supplements in medication reconciliation — a product with no prescription and a wellness label just failed a major trial in the disease it was marketed to help.

**Caveats:** The average worsening (2.72 points) was smaller than the threshold usually considered clinically meaningful; the trial was conducted entirely in Norway in people with early-stage disease, so results may not generalize to advanced Parkinson disease or to supplement use by healthy older adults.

### 2. Newer insomnia drugs linked to far fewer hip fractures than "Z-drugs" in people over 65

**Journal:** *J Am Geriatr Soc* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 8
**PMID:** 42850802 | **DOI:** 10.1111/jgs.70754

**The study:** Researchers used health insurance and claims databases from Tochigi Prefecture, Japan, to emulate a trial comparing newer orexin-blocking sleep medications with older Z-drugs (zolpidem and relatives) in adults aged 65 and over starting treatment for insomnia. Among 22,655 people in the lemborexant comparison, lemborexant users had roughly a 69% lower rate of hospitalization for hip fracture within a year (HR 0.31). In a parallel comparison of 22,377 people, suvorexant showed a lower rate too, but the difference was not statistically significant (HR 0.57).

**Why it matters:** Sleeping pills are among the most common fall-and-fracture culprits in older adults, and this is one of the largest head-to-head comparisons suggesting the newer class may be meaningfully safer for the hips.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you take zolpidem or another Z-drug for sleep, this is a reasonable thing to raise with your prescriber — ask whether a different class, or a non-drug approach to insomnia, might lower your fracture risk, rather than stopping on your own.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For care communities tracking falls, sleep medication class belongs on the review checklist alongside blood pressure drugs and sedatives; this study gives staff a specific, evidence-based question to put to prescribing physicians during medication reviews.

**Caveats:** Observational database study — it cannot prove the drug choice caused the difference, and residual confounding is likely (people prescribed newer drugs may differ in frailty or prescriber caution); conducted entirely in one Japanese prefecture, so prescribing patterns and generalizability to other countries are uncertain; hip fracture counts were small, producing wide confidence intervals.

### 3. Osteoporosis patients with a stubbornly low lab value may have a missed genetic bone disease

**Journal:** *Osteoporos Int* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 7
**PMID:** 42844428 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s00198-026-08227-8

**The study:** A systematic review of 27 observational studies published between 2015 and 2025 examined how often hypophosphatasia (HPP), a rare inherited bone and mineral disorder, turns up among patients with persistently low alkaline phosphatase (ALP) on routine blood work. Among studies at low risk of bias, genetically confirmed HPP was found in 10.4–26.2% of general clinical populations with low ALP, 46.4–56.5% of rheumatology populations, and 57.1–87.5% of osteoporosis populations. Overall population prevalence was estimated at roughly 18 to 36 cases per 100,000.

**Why it matters:** HPP is routinely mistaken for osteoporosis, and the distinction matters because standard bone-building drugs such as bisphosphonates are generally inappropriate in HPP — yet the clue, a low ALP, sits on blood panels most older adults have already had.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you've been treated for osteoporosis and keep having fractures, stress fractures, unexplained bone or muscle pain, or lost adult teeth, it's fair to ask your clinician to look back at your alkaline phosphatase results and whether a persistently low value has ever been explained.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For clinicians and staff reviewing residents' lab panels, a repeatedly low ALP is a flag that is easy to overlook because low values rarely trigger alerts the way high ones do — worth noting in records for anyone with recurrent fractures on osteoporosis therapy.

**Caveats:** Industry-funded and authored — several authors are employees of or consultants to Alexion/AstraZeneca Rare Disease, which markets HPP therapy, and most co-authors report payments from the company; the included studies were observational and heterogeneous, and the osteoporosis-population figures come from selected, already-screened groups, so they do not represent risk for the average older adult.

### 4. Why trials of taking older adults off fall-risk medications keep stalling

**Journal:** *J Gerontol A Biol Sci Med Sci* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 8
**PMID:** 42848531 | **DOI:** 10.1093/gerona/glag248

**The study:** Investigators running DeFRID — an ongoing multicenter randomized trial in India testing a structured program to withdraw fall risk-increasing drugs in adults aged 60 and over — combined their own process-evaluation data with a scoping review of global deprescribing trials. They identified four recurring categories of obstacle: ethical discomfort among clinicians about stopping drugs, difficulty recruiting and retaining participants, inconsistent methods of counting falls, and local context issues such as normalized polypharmacy and fragmented care. Internationally, trials shared the same weaknesses: low clinician buy-in, short follow-up, and eligibility rules that exclude the frail and cognitively impaired patients most likely to benefit.

**Why it matters:** It explains a frustrating gap for families and clinicians — everyone agrees older adults are on too many fall-causing medications, but the trial evidence to guide safe withdrawal remains thin because the studies themselves are so hard to run.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you've wondered why no one will give you a straight answer about stopping a sedative, blood pressure drug, or antidepressant that may be making you unsteady, part of the reason is that the research is genuinely unsettled — which makes it reasonable to ask for a scheduled, monitored trial reduction with your own doctor rather than a yes-or-no verdict.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living and care-coordination teams, the paper's most usable finding is that fall counting itself is inconsistent; standardizing how your facility defines and records a fall is a prerequisite to evaluating whether any medication change actually helped.

**Caveats:** This is a narrative review with scoping elements, not a trial result — it reports no patient outcomes and the underlying DeFRID trial is still ongoing; much of the context is specific to Indian low- and middle-income-country health systems and may not transfer directly to US or European settings.

### 5. Nurses can spot a palliative care patient's decline before symptoms worsen, review finds

**Journal:** *Palliat Support Care* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 9
**PMID:** 42850936 | **DOI:** 10.1017/S1478951526103964

**The study:** A systematic review screened over 1,100 records and included 14 studies — randomized trials, cohorts, predictive modeling studies, and telehealth interventions — on nurse-led approaches that combine routine blood markers with clinical judgment to anticipate symptom flare-ups in cancer patients receiving palliative care. Markers including C-reactive protein, the neutrophil-lymphocyte ratio, albumin, ferritin, and electrolytes predicted symptom worsening and clinical decline. Programs pairing those markers with nursing assessment improved symptom control, cut emergency department visits, and strengthened continuity of care, though formal predictive models were rarely embedded in real practice.

**Why it matters:** Most symptom management in serious illness is reactive — pain or breathlessness escalates and only then gets treated — and this points toward a nurse-driven model that gets ahead of the crisis and keeps frail patients out of the emergency department.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or your spouse are receiving palliative or hospice care, you can ask whether the nursing team reviews routine lab trends to anticipate rough patches, and request a clear plan for who to call when symptoms start to shift rather than after they become unbearable.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For hospice and palliative programs, the review makes a case for giving nurses a formal role in acting on lab trends — the evidence suggests payoff in fewer emergency transfers, which is both a quality and a cost argument for staffing models that support proactive nurse-led review.

**Caveats:** Only 14 heterogeneous studies were included, with varied designs and outcomes and no pooled effect estimate; most predictive models have not been validated in routine care, and the evidence is drawn from oncology palliative populations, so it may not extend to dementia or organ-failure trajectories common in senior living.

### 6. Telephone calls that ask older adults to tell their life story eased loneliness for a full year

**Journal:** *Int Psychogeriatr* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 8
**PMID:** 42850158 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.inpsyc.2026.100299

**The study:** Researchers in Hong Kong randomized 287 older adults (average age 76, 72% women) to one of three telephone programs delivered by trained lay counselors — not clinicians: wisdom-enhancement narrative therapy (Tele-NT), which draws out and reframes a person's own life story and accumulated life lessons; an empathy-focused calling program (Tele-EP); or an active control. On one standard loneliness measure (De Jong Gierveld), the narrative therapy group showed significantly less loneliness at both 6 and 12 months, along with reduced social loneliness at 6 months and better sleep quality at 12 months; both active programs reduced emotional loneliness at 12 months. On a second measure, the widely used revised UCLA Loneliness Scale, no significant overall group differences emerged.

**Why it matters:** Loneliness is common and health-damaging in later life, and this trial suggests a low-cost, phone-based program delivered by non-professionals can produce benefits that hold up a year later — making it plausible to scale through senior centers, community agencies and volunteer corps.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you feel isolated, the content of a check-in call may matter as much as the fact of one — programs that invite you to narrate your own life and the wisdom you've gained outperformed simple friendly conversation here. Ask your local senior center or community agency whether they offer structured telephone befriending or life-story programs, and consider volunteering for one yourself.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For providers and volunteer coordinators: this is evidence that trained lay counselors — not licensed therapists — can deliver a structured loneliness intervention by phone with durable results, which lowers the cost and staffing barrier to running one. Note that results depended on which loneliness scale was used, so build in your own outcome tracking rather than assuming a uniform effect.

**Caveats:** Findings were inconsistent across the two loneliness scales (significant on De Jong Gierveld, not on UCLA-LS); several reported benefits were secondary outcomes; single-region trial in Hong Kong, so cultural transferability is untested; sleep and loneliness outcomes are self-reported.

### 7. A 12-week home exercise program with video coaching lengthened the stride of older adults living with knee and back pain

**Journal:** *JMIR Aging* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 8
**PMID:** 42849064 | **DOI:** 10.2196/97414

**The study:** Japanese researchers randomized 59 community-dwelling adults aged 65 and older with mild to moderate knee and/or low back pain to a 12-week hybrid video-guided multicomponent exercise program with staff assistance or to an education-only control, then measured real-world walking with an ankle sensor worn during two 7-day windows. The exercise group improved the quality of their everyday walking — step length increased by about 2.4 cm (a moderate effect), with a borderline gain in walking speed — but total daily steps, distance and energy expenditure did not differ between groups. Sensitivity analyses showed the same direction of effect but with less precision, and the authors call the results suggestive rather than definitive.

**Why it matters:** Step length and gait speed are among the strongest predictors of future mobility, fall risk and independence, and this suggests structured exercise can improve how older adults with chronic joint pain walk in daily life even when it doesn't make them walk more.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If joint pain has changed how you walk, a guided exercise program may improve the quality of your steps — a longer stride and slightly faster pace — even if your daily step count stays the same, so don't judge a program solely by what your pedometer says. Ask a physical therapist about multicomponent programs combining strength, balance and mobility work that can be done at home with periodic staff check-ins.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living and rehab staff: a hybrid model — video-guided exercise at home plus staff support — was feasible for older residents with chronic pain, and wearable sensors captured gains in walking quality that activity-count metrics completely missed. Consider what you measure when evaluating your own wellness programs.

**Caveats:** Small sample (N=59, 52 analyzed), single-country single-program trial, secondary analysis of a randomized trial, effects attenuated in per-protocol sensitivity analysis and after accounting for multiple comparisons, short 12-week follow-up with no data on falls or long-term function.

### 8. Qigong improved memory and thinking in people with mild cognitive impairment, a review of 21 trials finds

**Journal:** *Appl Neuropsychol Adult* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 8
**PMID:** 42847483 | **DOI:** 10.1080/23279095.2026.2743827

**The study:** A systematic review and meta-analysis pooled 21 randomized trials covering 1,429 older adults with mild cognitive impairment to test whether qigong — a slow, meditative Chinese movement practice — improves cognition. Across trials, qigong was associated with significant improvements in global cognition (on both the MoCA and MMSE), memory (Wechsler Memory Scale and Auditory Verbal Learning Test) and executive function (Stroop test), with effect sizes described as large. The author rated overall study quality as low risk of bias but notes that long-term follow-up data are lacking.

**Why it matters:** Mild cognitive impairment affects a large share of older adults and has few proven treatments, so a gentle, low-cost, low-injury-risk group exercise that may support memory and executive function is directly actionable for individuals and for activity programming in senior living.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or your doctor have noted mild memory changes, qigong is a low-impact option worth asking about — it requires no equipment, is easy on the joints, and pooled trial evidence links it to better memory and thinking scores. Look for classes at senior centers, YMCAs or community colleges, and treat it as a complement to, not a replacement for, medical evaluation of memory concerns.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For activity directors and caregivers: qigong is cheap to run, adaptable to seated participation, and this evidence base supports prioritizing it in programming for residents with mild cognitive impairment. Bear in mind that most included trials come from Chinese research settings, so participant engagement in other cultural contexts may differ.

**Caveats:** Meta-analysis of heterogeneous trials, many from a single research region (searches included four Chinese-language databases), raising generalizability questions; unusually large pooled effect sizes in behavioral trials warrant caution; no long-term follow-up; blinding is inherently difficult in exercise trials; publication bias not described in the abstract.

### 9. Dose matters: higher-dose brexpiprazole eased agitation in Alzheimer's even in severe dementia, lower dose fell short

**Journal:** *J Alzheimers Dis* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 8
**PMID:** 42847294 | **DOI:** 10.1177/13872877261493010

**The study:** Researchers re-analyzed data from Japanese participants in a 10-week randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of brexpiprazole for agitation in Alzheimer's dementia, splitting patients by dementia severity (mild-to-moderate, MMSE 11–22, versus severe, MMSE 1–10). The 2 mg daily dose reduced agitation scores significantly more than placebo in both severity groups (differences of −7.0 and −7.3 points on the Cohen-Mansfield Agitation Inventory), while the 1 mg dose helped only the mild-to-moderate group and showed no significant benefit in severe dementia. Global clinical severity ratings followed the same pattern.

**Why it matters:** Agitation is one of the most distressing symptoms for people with Alzheimer's and their caregivers, and this analysis suggests that how well a commonly prescribed drug works depends on dose and on how advanced the dementia is — information relevant to any family weighing medication for agitation.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If a loved one's agitation isn't improving on brexpiprazole, dose may be part of the picture — ask the prescribing clinician whether the current dose is appropriate for the stage of dementia, and discuss the known risks of antipsychotic-class medications in older adults alongside non-drug approaches first.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For care staff and clinicians: dementia severity appears to modify treatment response, which argues for documenting MMSE stage and tracking agitation with a structured tool like the CMAI rather than impressions alone, so under-dosing in advanced dementia can be spotted. This analysis does not address safety trade-offs, which remain central for this drug class.

**Caveats:** Post hoc subgroup analysis — hypothesis-generating, not confirmatory; funded and largely authored by Otsuka Pharmaceutical, the drug's manufacturer; Japanese patients only, limiting generalizability; short 10-week follow-up; safety and mortality outcomes not reported in this analysis.

### 10. Sleeping pills and dementia risk: huge Korean study finds the link is far weaker than feared

**Journal:** *J Alzheimers Dis* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 9
**PMID:** 42853249 | **DOI:** 10.1177/13872877261492672

**The study:** Researchers used South Korea's national health insurance database to compare 132,805 benzodiazepine users with 132,805 people of the same age and sex who did not use them, tracking new diagnoses of dementia and Alzheimer's disease. Before adjusting for other health conditions, benzodiazepine users appeared to have a 43% higher dementia risk — but after accounting for their much heavier burden of other illnesses (42.5% had a Charlson Comorbidity Index of 7 or more, versus 26.5% of controls) and psychiatric conditions such as anxiety, the excess risk shrank to 15%. Among people who already had dementia, benzodiazepine use was not associated with faster progression to severe dementia, and there was no dose or duration effect.

**Why it matters:** Benzodiazepines are among the most commonly prescribed drugs for anxiety and insomnia in later life, and fear of dementia has driven many older adults and their doctors to agonize over them; this analysis suggests much of the alarming association reported previously reflects how sick benzodiazepine users already are, not the drug itself.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you take a benzodiazepine such as lorazepam, alprazolam or diazepam, this study is reassuring news about dementia specifically — but it is not a green light to keep taking one indefinitely. These drugs still carry well-established risks of falls, fractures, confusion and dependence in older bodies, so the conversation to have with your prescriber is about whether you still need it and at what dose, not about dementia alone.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and prescribers, the finding reframes the clinical question: rather than withdrawing benzodiazepines purely over dementia fear, focus on the sedation, fall and interaction risks that drive real harm — and recognize that a resident on long-term benzodiazepines is likely carrying substantial multimorbidity that deserves its own review.

**Caveats:** Observational insurance-claims study — cannot establish causation; diagnoses were defined from billing codes, not clinical assessment; residual confounding by indication remains likely (anxiety and insomnia can themselves be early dementia symptoms); findings come from a single national population and may not generalize.

### 11. An 8-week exercise program older adults actually stuck with — but it didn't make them stronger

**Journal:** *J Aging Phys Act* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 7
**PMID:** 42849527 | **DOI:** 10.1123/japa.2025-0453

**The study:** In a cluster randomized controlled trial, 186 low-income community-dwelling older adults (88% women, average age 74) were assigned either to a program combining a one-on-one session reviewing their own body composition and physical test results, weekly 60-minute group classes and 60 minutes of weekly home exercise, or to a control group. After eight weeks, the program did not produce meaningful gains in physical function (Short Physical Performance Battery, handgrip strength) or body composition beyond the control group, though it did appear to blunt declines in two bioelectrical measures of cell health (phase angle and impedance ratio). Adherence was notably high: 81% attendance at classes and 72% completion of home exercises.

**Why it matters:** It is a useful corrective to the assumption that any group exercise class will improve strength and balance — dose and intensity matter, and eight weeks of once-weekly class plus an hour of home work may simply not be enough stimulus.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you are joining a community exercise class hoping to get stronger and steadier on your feet, ask how often it meets and whether the resistance work progresses over time. Showing up reliably is necessary but not sufficient — programs that only meet once a week may maintain you rather than improve you, and you may need to add a second session or heavier work at home to see change.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living activity directors and community partners: the striking result here is the adherence, not the outcome. Low-income older adults will show up and do homework when the program is built around their own feedback — the design lever to pull next is intensity and frequency, not recruitment or engagement.

**Caveats:** Short follow-up (8 weeks); largely female sample from one US region's low-income community-dwelling older adults, which may limit generalizability; the positive bioimpedance findings are secondary, surrogate measures rather than function or falls.

### 12. Hyperbaric oxygen chambers are marketed as anti-aging therapy. Here's what the evidence actually shows.

**Journal:** *J Aging Phys Act* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 7
**PMID:** 42849526 | **DOI:** 10.1123/japa.2025-0308

**The study:** Researchers systematically reviewed all studies of hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT) in people aged 50 and over, finding just seven studies covering 502 participants aged 51 to 85. Two high-quality studies in healthy older adults reported improvements in global cognition, attention, executive function, processing speed, aerobic capacity and lung function; moderate-quality studies in people with Parkinson's disease, cerebral small vessel disease and vascular dementia suggested HBOT added to medication produced greater cognitive gains than medication alone. Side effects were reported in four studies and were generally mild and short-lived, but follow-up ranged only from about four to twelve weeks.

**Why it matters:** HBOT is aggressively promoted in wellness and longevity clinics at considerable out-of-pocket cost, and this review gives older adults and families a realistic measure of how thin — though not negative — the underlying evidence base is.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have seen ads promising hyperbaric oxygen will sharpen your memory or reverse aging, know that the entire worldwide evidence in people over 50 rests on seven studies and roughly 500 people, with no data on whether any benefit lasts beyond about three months. Before paying for a course of sessions, ask the provider what trial evidence supports their specific protocol and what it costs in total.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families fielding questions about expensive longevity treatments, this review is a usable reference point: the signal is genuine enough to keep studying but far too preliminary to justify major spending, and physical benefits in particular were often inferred indirectly from quality-of-life scores rather than measured directly.

**Caveats:** Systematic review of only seven heterogeneous studies with 502 total participants; protocols varied widely in pressure, duration and frequency; mostly moderate methodological quality; short follow-up leaves durability unknown; no pooled meta-analysis.

### 13. A second, simple question may help doctors spot which patients need palliative care — and it works the same across Europe

**Journal:** *Am J Hosp Palliat Care* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 9
**PMID:** 42853125 | **DOI:** 10.1177/10499091261489538

**The study:** The long-used "Surprise Question" asks clinicians, "Would I be surprised if this patient died in the next 12 months?" — a screen with known accuracy problems. Researchers tested the "Double Surprise Question," which adds "Would I be surprised if this patient were still alive after 12 months?", by having 250 general practitioners across six European countries respond to 20 case vignettes. Responses tracked sensibly with patient factors — estimated chance of dying, lower functional status, and a cancer rather than organ-failure diagnosis — while the GPs' own characteristics (such as experience or country) had no influence on their answers.

**Why it matters:** A screening tool whose results do not depend on which doctor happens to be asking is far more trustworthy for triggering timely palliative care conversations, which too often happen only in the final days.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** You do not have to wait for your doctor to raise palliative care. If you are living with advanced cancer, heart failure, COPD or kidney disease, you can ask directly: "Would you be surprised if I were still here in a year?" — and use the answer to open a conversation about symptom control, goals of care, and what support is available now rather than later.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For primary care teams and senior living clinical staff, this supports adding the second question to routine reviews as a low-cost prompt — though note that GPs were more likely to flag cancer patients than those with organ failure, a bias worth guarding against when identifying residents who would benefit from palliative support.

**Caveats:** Vignette study rather than real patients — clinicians' responses to hypothetical cases may not match real-world judgments; no link to actual patient deaths, so predictive accuracy remains untested; participating GPs were volunteers and may not represent general practice broadly.

### 14. Your osteoporosis result may depend on which scanner you stood on

**Journal:** *Osteoporos Int* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 7
**PMID:** 42841935 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s00198-026-08245-6

**The study:** Researchers analyzed hip and femoral neck bone density results from 6,086 German women scanned on two DXA machine models from the same manufacturer, then compared the values against major international reference populations from the US (NHANES III), Ireland and Italy. One device (Prodigy Pro) consistently produced higher readings than the other (iDXA), and when compared to international norms, iDXA values came out lower in all 36 comparisons — by as much as 19% — while the other device differed by at most roughly 9%. The authors conclude the data should not be treated as scanner-independent normative values.

**Why it matters:** Bone density scores determine who is diagnosed with osteoporosis and who gets offered fracture-preventing treatment, so systematic differences between machines could mean the same woman is labeled differently depending on where she was scanned.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have had repeat bone density scans, ask whether they were done on the same machine at the same facility — comparing results across different scanners can be misleading. When you get a result near the treatment threshold, it is reasonable to ask your doctor how your T-score was derived and which reference population it was compared against.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For clinicians and care facilities arranging bone health screening, the practical takeaway is continuity: book follow-up DXA scans on the same device wherever possible, and treat cross-site comparisons of absolute bone density values with caution.

**Caveats:** Retrospective analysis of a clinical (not population-representative) cohort of women referred for scanning, so it likely overrepresents those at risk; women only; single manufacturer's devices; comparisons with international cohorts were calculated from published summary statistics rather than individual data; generative AI was used in drafting and statistical syntax, per the authors' disclosure.

### 15. Older adults on prescription opioids report markedly worse quality of life — especially physical function

**Journal:** *J Am Geriatr Soc* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 9
**PMID:** 42853040 | **DOI:** 10.1111/jgs.70708

**The study:** Researchers analyzed baseline data from 9,971 community-dwelling older Australians enrolled in the STAREE statin trial, comparing 479 people taking prescribed opioids with 9,492 who were not, using the 36-item Short Form Survey. Opioid users scored far lower on health-related quality of life, and although adjusting for demographics, medical conditions and psychosocial factors cut the gap roughly in half, it remained significant (about 7.6 points lower overall). Deficits appeared across every domain measured — physical functioning, social functioning, vitality, bodily pain, mental health — with physical functioning scores running about 20 to 25 points lower.

**Why it matters:** Opioids are commonly prescribed to older adults for persistent pain, yet this suggests the people taking them are living with substantially reduced function and well-being that pain-focused prescribing may not be addressing.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you take a prescription opioid for ongoing pain, it's reasonable to ask your doctor at your next review how well it's actually working — not just for pain scores, but for what you can do, how you sleep, your mood and your energy. Ask whether non-drug options like physical therapy have been tried, and never stop or taper an opioid on your own.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For care staff and family, this is an argument for tracking function and mood, not just pain ratings, in residents and relatives on long-term opioids — a decline in walking, socializing or vitality may be as important a signal as the pain itself.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional observational design — it cannot establish whether opioids reduce quality of life or whether people with worse health and pain are more likely to be prescribed them; opioid use and quality of life were both self-reported; participants were relatively healthy older Australians enrolled in a clinical trial, who may not represent frailer populations.

### 16. Nearly 4 in 5 older adults with new back pain got low-value care, Medicare Advantage data show

**Journal:** *J Am Geriatr Soc* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 9
**PMID:** 42852584 | **DOI:** 10.1111/jgs.70779

**The study:** Using a 20% sample of 2021 Medicare Advantage claims, researchers followed 181,980 beneficiaries aged 66 and older with a newly diagnosed episode of low back pain for 90 days. Among the 87,305 who received follow-up care, only 21.6% got guideline-concordant high-value care — most commonly physical therapy — while 78.4% received low-value care, most often imaging without any physical therapy. Lower odds of high-value care were seen among the oldest patients, Hispanic beneficiaries, those with multiple chronic conditions, people in rural or socioeconomically deprived counties, and those whose first visit was with a surgeon or pain specialist.

**Why it matters:** Guidelines put non-drug treatment first for back pain, but this first look at Medicare Advantage enrollees suggests most older adults are routed to scans and injections instead — with the gap widest for rural, lower-income and Hispanic patients.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If new back pain sends you to the doctor, it's worth asking whether an X-ray or MRI will actually change your treatment, and whether physical therapy should come first — guidelines say it usually should, unless you have red-flag symptoms like weakness, numbness or loss of bladder control.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living operators and care navigators, the findings point to a concrete quality lever: building easy referral pathways to physical therapy for residents with new back pain, particularly in rural communities and lower-rated plans where high-value care is least likely to happen on its own.

**Caveats:** Retrospective claims analysis — billing codes cannot capture clinical reasoning or whether imaging was appropriate in individual cases; observational design cannot establish causation; limited to a single year (2021), when care patterns were still affected by the pandemic; no patient-reported pain or function outcomes.

### 17. Memory center shrinks faster in women and in people with two copies of the APOE ε4 gene

**Journal:** *Neuroradiology* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 9
**PMID:** 42853241 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s00234-026-04210-6

**The study:** Japanese researchers analyzed brain MRI from 9,453 adults in the population-based Tohoku Medical Megabank study, including 5,774 who had a second scan an average of 4.7 years later. A single snapshot showed no difference in hippocampal volume by APOE genotype — but over time, people carrying two copies of the ε4 allele lost hippocampal volume faster than ε3/ε3 or ε3/ε4 carriers, as did women compared with men. Total brain volume loss did not differ, meaning the shrinkage was specific to the memory-critical hippocampus, and the effect held even after accounting for cognitive test scores.

**Why it matters:** It shows that genetic and sex-related risk for Alzheimer's-type brain change may be invisible on a one-time scan and only emerges when the same brain is measured years apart — relevant as blood tests and anti-amyloid drugs push risk assessment earlier.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you've had a brain scan that looked normal, understand that a single image is a weak guide to future risk — repeat measurements over years are what reveal change. Focus on what you can influence: blood pressure, hearing, activity, sleep and social engagement all affect brain aging regardless of your genes.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families weighing direct-to-consumer genetic testing, this adds nuance: carrying one ε4 copy did not accelerate hippocampal loss here — only the much rarer double-copy genotype did, and even then the result is a population average, not a personal forecast.

**Caveats:** Observational cohort — cannot establish causation or predict individual outcomes; participants were all from one region of Japan and may not generalize to other populations; cognition was assessed only with a brief screening test (MoCA) at the second scan; no dementia diagnoses were reported as outcomes; relatively short interval (mean 4.7 years) for a disease that develops over decades.

### 19. Who you first see for palliative care predicts how much care you actually get

**Journal:** *J Pain Symptom Manage* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 8
**PMID:** 42849667 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jpainsymman.2026.09.039

**The study:** Researchers linked Medicare claims to the National Palliative Care Registry to examine 33,677 people who died of poor-prognosis cancer at 276 hospitals with palliative care programs. Overall, 72.8% had at least one palliative care encounter in their last six months. Patients whose first encounter was with an independent physician were more likely to have it within 30 days of death (69% vs. 48% for an independent advanced practice provider) but were far more likely to have only that single visit (61% vs. 30%); first encounters with a team-based physician were associated with nearly three times the odds of having three or more encounters.

**Why it matters:** It suggests palliative care is not one uniform service — whether a patient gets sustained support or a single late consultation depends heavily on how the program is staffed and who makes the first contact.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or a loved one is facing a serious cancer diagnosis, it's fair to ask the hospital whether palliative care is delivered by a dedicated team and whether follow-up visits are routine — one consultation in the final weeks is not the same thing as ongoing symptom and goals-of-care support. You can request a palliative care referral yourself; it does not mean stopping treatment.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For hospital and hospice administrators, the data offer a benchmarking signal: team-based staffing models were associated with repeat engagement, while nearly a third of eligible decedents had no palliative encounter at all despite being treated at hospitals with established programs.

**Caveats:** Retrospective claims analysis — billing data cannot capture encounter content, quality or patient preference; observational design cannot establish that clinician type causes differences in follow-up; limited to Medicare decedents with poor-prognosis cancer at hospitals with registered palliative care programs; no patient-reported or survival outcomes examined.

### 21. Drugs already on pharmacy shelves linked to lower Alzheimer's rates in a huge patient records study

**Journal:** *Nat Neurosci* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 8
**PMID:** 42850312 | **DOI:** 10.1038/s41593-026-02472-0

### 22. Stroke survivors with aphasia face nearly three times the risk of developing epilepsy

**Journal:** *Neuroepidemiology* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 8
**PMID:** 42848724 | **DOI:** 10.1159/ned/adwag049

**The study:** Using Taiwan's national health insurance records, researchers followed 23,431 adults who had language impairment (aphasia) after a first stroke and 130,058 stroke survivors without aphasia for a median of roughly 8 years. Post-stroke epilepsy occurred at 13.02 per 1,000 person-years in the aphasia group versus 4.56 in the non-aphasia group — an excess of about 3 cases per 1,000 person-years after adjustment. The gap was largest in the first year after stroke and held across age groups, sexes, and both ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If a stroke left you with difficulty speaking or understanding words, ask your neurologist what seizure warning signs you and your household should know, particularly during the first year after the stroke. Brief staring spells, sudden confusion, unexplained falls, or jerking movements are worth reporting rather than dismissing as part of recovery.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and rehabilitation staff: a resident with aphasia may not be able to describe an aura or a postictal fog, so documented observation matters more than usual. Consider making seizure-symptom education part of the discharge conversation for every stroke survivor with language impairment.

**Caveats:** Observational insurance-claims study — it shows association, not causation, and aphasia may simply mark more extensive brain injury rather than independently causing epilepsy. ### 23. Glasses are costing older Americans the most — and seniors are the likeliest to pay the whole bill themselves

### 24. Pain relief from platelet injections for knee arthritis faded within months, small study finds

**Journal:** *J Back Musculoskelet Rehabil* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 8

### 25. Who gets rehab after a hip or knee replacement depends partly on your neighborhood's transportation and housing

**Journal:** *Phys Ther* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 8
**PMID:** 42667652 | **DOI:** 10.1093/ptj/pzag090

**The study:** Researchers analyzed 100% Medicare fee-for-service claims for 74,937 beneficiaries aged 66 and older who had a hip or knee replacement in 2018, examining physical therapy visits in the 90 days after discharge. - **About older adults and seniors:** For discharge planners and senior living staff: this is evidence that transportation and housing vulnerability are shaping rehabilitation setting, not just rehabilitation amount. Building a transportation question into standard discharge assessment is a concrete equity step.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional analysis of administrative claims — it shows associations, not causation, and cannot tell whether more visits meant better outcomes. ### 26. Five routine blood tests, combined into one score, predicted three-year memory decline

**Journal:** *J Alzheimers Dis* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 8
**PMID:** 42847329 | **DOI:** 10.1177/13872877261494123

**The study:** Researchers used data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study to build an "Aging Discordance Index" — a measure of how unevenly a person's body systems are aging, calculated from five standard blood tests (creatinine, total cholesterol, C-reactive protein, mean corpuscular volume, and HbA1c). - **To older adults and seniors:** This is early research, not a test you should request yet, but it reinforces something actionable: the ordinary blood markers your doctor already tracks — inflammation, blood sugar, kidney function, cholesterol — appear to relate to brain health as a package. - **About older adults and seniors:** For clinicians and care managers, the concept worth watching is that imbalance across organ systems may carry information beyond any single abnormal value. No change in practice is warranted from one cohort study.

**Caveats:** Observational design that cannot establish causation; short follow-up of three years; cognitive measures were brief screening tasks rather than full neuropsychological testing; substantial loss to follow-up (addressed with mixed models but not eliminated). ## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**

- **Palliative care as a delivery-system problem, not a clinical one.** Last week opened this thread with *A trained guide helped Black older adults plan end-of-life care — a self-serve workbook worked just as well for White patients* (PMID 42842242) and *When advanced cancer blocks the bowel, chemotherapy relieves it in only one patient in three — and the toxicity is heavy* (PMID 42843988). This week it widens into four studies, all pointing at structure rather than science: *Who you first see for palliative care predicts how much care you actually get* (PMID 42849667), *A second, simple question may help doctors spot which patients need palliative care — and it works the same across Europe* (PMID 42853125), *Nurses can spot a palliative care patient's decline before symptoms worsen, review finds* (PMID 42850936), and *Pancreatic cancer patients and caregivers describe delays, financial strain — and widespread confusion about palliative care* (PMID 42842360). Last week's lesson was that people act when someone asks; this week's is that who asks, and how the team is staffed, determines what they get.
- **Marketed longevity treatments keep meeting thin or negative evidence.** Last week's *Brief sessions of thin-air breathing proved safe in Parkinson's — and shifted stress and metabolic hormones* (PMID 42574864) and *A cancer drug restored muscle strength in aged mice by clearing a metabolic traffic jam* (PMID 42841610) were the "promising, not ready" entries. This week delivers the harder version: *A popular anti-aging supplement made Parkinson's slightly worse in a large trial* (PMID 42848371), *Hyperbaric oxygen chambers are marketed as anti-aging therapy. Here's what the evidence actually shows.* (PMID 42849526), and *Pain relief from platelet injections for knee arthritis faded within months, small study finds* (PMID 42847270). Notably, hyperbaric oxygen is now the second lab-adjacent oxygen therapy in two weeks being sold to people with Parkinson's ahead of the evidence.
- **Measurement remains the weak link.** Last week's *The exercise research guiding senior fitness programs is measuring activity with tools never designed for older bodies* (PMID 41638194) has three successors. *Your osteoporosis result may depend on which scanner you stood on* (PMID 42841935) finds up to 19% divergence between devices on a test that decides who gets treated; *Telephone calls that ask older adults to tell their life story eased loneliness for a full year* (PMID 42850158) was significant on one loneliness scale and not the other; and *A 12-week home exercise program with video coaching lengthened the stride of older adults living with knee and back pain* (PMID 42849064) found real gains in walking quality that step counts missed entirely.
- **Back pain, from prognosis to practice.** Last week's *Back pain that stops you from doing things predicts frailty seven years later* (PMID 42844230) argued for treating functional limitation. This week, *Nearly 4 in 5 older adults with new back pain got low-value care, Medicare Advantage data show* (PMID 42852584) shows that is largely not what happens — physical therapy was the exception, imaging the rule.

**Advances**

- **Parkinson's: what you do versus what you take.** Last week's *People with Parkinson's who stay the most active decline the slowest — and total time moving matters more than intensity* (PMID 42842855) put the modifiable lever on movement. This week's NOPARK result (PMID 42848371) closes the door on the most-purchased alternative, with a phase 3 answer that is not merely "no benefit" but a small worsening.

**Counterpoints**

- **Adherence was last week's problem; this week it isn't.** *Older adults quit their dizziness exercises within a year — and most never tell anyone* (PMID 42844416) framed drop-off as the barrier. But in *An 8-week exercise program older adults actually stuck with — but it didn't make them stronger* (PMID 42849527), 81% attended classes and 72% did the homework — and still got no functional gain. Together they suggest two different failure points: fading habits in one setting, insufficient dose in the other.
- **Scary associations keep shrinking under adjustment.** Last week's *Brain white-matter damage grows faster with age — and lower blood pressure didn't predict slower progression* (PMID 42843461) warned against reading group averages as personal risk. ## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Six studies this week converge on the medicine cabinet — and they mostly move the worry, rather than confirming it. *Sleeping pills and dementia risk: huge Korean study finds the link is far weaker than feared* (PMID 42853249) shrinks the fear that drives most benzodiazepine arguments, while *Newer insomnia drugs linked to far fewer hip fractures than "Z-drugs" in people over 65* (PMID 42850802) and *Older adults on prescription opioids report markedly worse quality of life — especially physical function* (PMID 42853040) point to the harms that are actually measurable: broken hips and lost function. *Why trials of taking older adults off fall-risk medications keep stalling* (PMID 42848531) explains why no one can give a clean answer about stopping, *Dose matters: higher-dose brexpiprazole eased agitation in Alzheimer's even in severe dementia, lower dose fell short* (PMID 42847294) shows the question is sometimes the dose rather than the drug, and *A popular anti-aging supplement made Parkinson's slightly worse in a large trial* (PMID 42848371) is a reminder that the bottle bought without a prescription belongs on the same list.

**Why pitch this now:** Older adults and families are routinely told to "take fewer pills," but this batch says something more useful and more specific — the fear most people act on may be the wrong one, and the review that matters is about falls, function and dose, not a headline diagnosis.

**Angle:** A practical walkthrough of the modern medication review for older adults and the family members who sit in on appointments: which drugs this evidence puts on the list, what to ask about fracture risk and daily function rather than dementia alone, why "we don't really know" is an honest answer about stopping, and what senior living staff can standardize on their end.

**Potential headlines:**
- New Research Points to Hip Fractures, Not Memory Loss, as the Bigger Risk From Sleeping Pills
- The Dementia Worry About Anxiety Medication Just Got Smaller. The Fall Risk Didn't.
- Why No One Will Give You a Straight Answer About Stopping Your Medication

- **National Council on Aging** — falls prevention is central to their programming, and the lemborexant-versus-Z-drug fracture comparison plus the deprescribing-evidence gap gives their audience something concrete to put into a medication review toolkit.
