### 1. Big trial finds carotid surgery doesn't rescue thinking skills in people with symptom-free artery narrowing

**Journal:** *JAMA* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 16
**PMID:** 42747834 | **DOI:** 10.1001/jama.2026.13384

**The study:** Within the large CREST-2 randomized trial, researchers enrolled 392 adults aged 35-86 (mean age 71) who had no symptoms but at least 70% narrowing of one internal carotid artery, and measured blood flow delay to the brain with perfusion imaging. Participants were randomly assigned to carotid surgery or stenting plus intensive medical management, or intensive medical management alone, and took five cognitive tests over a year. Among the 51 participants who already had reduced cognition at baseline, there was no cognitive advantage from revascularization, and no benefit even in those with the worst brain blood-flow impairment (adjusted difference -0.15 SD; interaction P = .40).

**Why it matters:** Carotid procedures are sometimes pitched to older patients as a way to protect memory and thinking as well as prevent stroke; this is the strongest randomized evidence to date that the cognitive rationale doesn't hold up.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If a clinician raises carotid surgery or stenting for a narrowed artery that hasn't caused symptoms, ask specifically what outcome the procedure is expected to improve — this trial found no thinking or memory benefit over a year, even in patients with reduced blood flow to the brain, so the conversation should center on stroke risk and your own priorities.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families weighing an elective vascular procedure for a relative with early cognitive changes: the hope that restoring blood flow will sharpen thinking is not supported here, which reframes the risk-benefit discussion and may reduce pressure to proceed quickly.

**Caveats:** The key subgroup with reduced baseline cognition was small (n=51 of 291 analyzed); follow-up was only one year; participants were dementia-free with at least an eighth-grade education, which may limit generalization; several investigators report industry consulting fees, though funding was from the NIH.

### 2. Three weeks of pre-surgery conditioning didn't keep frail older patients independent a year later

**Journal:** *Lancet Healthy Longev* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 15
**PMID:** 42743945 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.lanhl.2026.100899

**The study:** The German PRAEP-GO trial randomized 1,382 patients aged 70 and older (median age 78) with frailty or prefrailty facing elective surgery at 19 hospitals to either usual care or a 3-week individualized prehabilitation program of 30 supervised sessions covering exercise, nutrition, cognitive and psychological support, and practical help. Adherence was high — a median of 29.5 of 30 sessions completed. Twelve months after surgery, change in long-term care dependency did not differ between groups (between-group difference -0.1; p=0.26).

**Why it matters:** Prehabilitation has been widely promoted as a way for frail older adults to 'train' for surgery, and this is the largest test of whether it preserves independence — a result that should temper expectations and redirect resources toward longer, behavior-change-focused programs.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're frail and facing planned surgery, a short burst of supervised exercise and nutrition sessions beforehand is safe and you may value how it feels — but this large trial found it did not reduce your odds of needing more care a year later, so ask your surgical team what else in your recovery plan actually moves that needle.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For health systems and senior living operators budgeting for prehabilitation pathways: the authors explicitly conclude that short supervised programs should not be adopted as a universal preoperative route, and that longer, engagement-focused models and better targeting of who benefits deserve the investment instead.

**Caveats:** Conducted entirely in Germany with no race or ethnicity data collected, which may limit generalization; screening was interrupted by the COVID-19 pandemic; about 13% of randomized participants lacked 12-month outcome data; funded by the German Federal Joint Committee's Innovation Fund, with several authors reporting extensive industry ties outside the trial.

### 3. Botox injections sharply cut the foot pain that torments many people with Parkinson's

**Journal:** *Neurol Clin Pract* | **Published:** 2026 Dec
**PMID:** 42753227 | **DOI:** 10.1212/CPJ.0000000000200659

**The study:** In a single-center, double-blind randomized trial, 33 adults with Parkinson disease and painful one-sided foot dystonia that had not responded to medication adjustments received either 100 units of onabotulinumtoxinA or placebo, injected into three specific foot and leg muscles. At 6 weeks, the botulinum group had a large reduction in dystonia-related pain (mean difference -3.60 on the King's Parkinson's Disease Pain Scale item; Hedges' g -1.33), sustained at 12 weeks. Three-quarters of treated patients achieved at least 30% pain reduction versus under 12% on placebo, with no worsening of motor function and only mild side effects.

**Why it matters:** Painful foot cramping and curling is a common, disabling and often overlooked Parkinson's symptom, and this is rare randomized evidence that a targeted, well-tolerated treatment works when medication changes have failed.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If your Parkinson's brings painful foot cramping or toe curling — especially during 'off' periods — and adjusting your medications hasn't helped, it's worth asking a movement disorders specialist whether targeted botulinum toxin injections are an option for you.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For caregivers and care staff: foot dystonia pain is often described vaguely as 'cramps' or dismissed as arthritis; documenting when the pain occurs relative to medication doses can help a specialist recognize it as a treatable dystonia rather than general Parkinson's discomfort.

**Caveats:** Small sample (N=33), single-center, short follow-up (12 weeks blinded plus 12-week open-label extension), primary outcome was patient-reported pain, and results need replication in larger multicenter trials.

### 4. Vitamin D and fish oil didn't shift Alzheimer's blood markers over four years

**Journal:** *Alzheimers Dement (N Y)* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 14
**PMID:** 42746141 | **DOI:** 10.1002/trc2.70312

**The study:** Researchers analyzed 929 participants (mean age 65) from the in-clinic subgroup of the large VITAL randomized trial, who took 2,000 IU/day of vitamin D3, 1 g/day of marine omega-3s, both, or placebo. Blood was tested at baseline and at 2 and 4 years for four Alzheimer's-related biomarkers including neurofilament light and GFAP. Neither supplement significantly changed any biomarker over four years, though exploratory subgroup signals appeared among men and Black participants.

**Why it matters:** Vitamin D and fish oil are among the most commonly taken supplements by older adults hoping to protect their brains, and this randomized evidence finds no measurable effect on the blood markers that track Alzheimer's-related brain change.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're taking vitamin D or fish oil specifically to ward off dementia, this randomized trial found no effect on blood markers of Alzheimer's-related brain change over four years — worth factoring in when you review your supplement list and its cost with your doctor.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and senior living wellness programs: supplement regimens marketed for 'brain health' continue to lack randomized support, and this study is a useful reference point when residents or relatives ask what's actually proven.

**Caveats:** Secondary analysis of a trial designed for cardiovascular and cancer outcomes; participants were relatively young (mean age 65) and only 8.2% were Black, so subgroup findings are fragile; subgroup analyses were not corrected for multiple testing and the authors call them hypothesis-generating only; several authors report industry research support and honoraria (including Mars Edge, Nordic Naturals, Haleon).

### 5. Online support for dementia caregivers helped most those juggling a job alongside care

**Journal:** *J Gerontol A Biol Sci Med Sci* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 15
**PMID:** 42745561 | **DOI:** 10.1093/gerona/glag232

**The study:** This secondary analysis of the Australian Draw Care randomized trial examined 93 adult family carers from diverse language backgrounds who received either an online, culturally adapted caregiver support program or were waitlisted. Forty-two carers reported work-care role conflict — missing work or losing productivity because of caregiving demands. Carers with that conflict saw significantly larger reductions in caregiver burden from the intervention at both 6 weeks (-10.00) and 12 weeks (-9.56) than carers without it.

**Why it matters:** Working family caregivers are a fast-growing and heavily strained group, and this suggests low-cost online support may deliver its biggest payoff precisely to the people with the least time to attend in-person programs.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you are caring for a spouse or parent with dementia while still working, this trial suggests flexible online caregiver support may ease your burden more than it does for carers without job pressures — worth asking your dementia service or employer whether such a program is available to you.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For employers, aged care providers and community organizations: targeting online caregiver programs at employees who are missing work or struggling with productivity because of care duties may be a more efficient use of limited program places than blanket offerings.

**Caveats:** Small sample (N=93) and an exploratory secondary analysis not pre-specified as the trial's main question; outcomes were self-reported; waitlist control rather than active comparator; short 12-week follow-up; conducted in Australia among linguistically diverse carers, which may limit generalization.

### 6. Medicare data reveal four very different hospital paths after a heart failure diagnosis

**Journal:** *J Gerontol A Biol Sci Med Sci* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 16
**PMID:** 42750129 | **DOI:** 10.1093/gerona/glag233

**The study:** Researchers followed 84,597 fee-for-service Medicare beneficiaries (mean age 77.4) for up to five years after a first heart failure diagnosis made during a hospital stay, using data from 2010 to 2020. They identified four distinct hospitalization patterns: consistently low risk (22.9%), high risk right after discharge then tapering (57.9%), low risk that climbed later (9.8%), and persistently high risk (9.4%). Routinely collected clinical characteristics available before discharge predicted membership in the highest-risk group reasonably well (C-statistic 0.79).

**Why it matters:** It shows that the year-by-year hospital burden of heart failure varies enormously between older adults, and that the highest-risk patients can be flagged before they even leave the hospital — enabling earlier, targeted support at home.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or a partner has just been diagnosed with heart failure in the hospital, ask the discharge team where you fall on risk of readmission and what follow-up is being arranged — for most patients the riskiest window is the weeks right after going home, but a sizable minority face rising risk years later and need continued monitoring.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For discharge planners and senior living clinical staff: this supports using routinely available admission data to sort new heart failure patients into follow-up intensities rather than applying a uniform 30-day readmission playbook, and flags the 'low-to-high risk' group whose deterioration comes late and may be missed.

**Caveats:** Retrospective observational analysis of administrative claims — it identifies patterns and predicts risk but cannot establish cause or prove that targeted interventions will help; limited to fee-for-service Medicare beneficiaries, so Medicare Advantage and uninsured older adults are not represented; prediction models were not tested in an external population.

### 7. Clot-busting drug helped stroke patients over 70 even when treatment started up to 24 hours later

**Journal:** *Stroke Vasc Neurol* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 16
**PMID:** 42749350 | **DOI:** 10.1136/svn-2025-004834

**The study:** Researchers reanalyzed the HOPE randomized trial, focusing on the 198 participants older than 70 (median age 80) who had an ischemic stroke and still showed salvageable brain tissue on imaging 4.5 to 24 hours after symptoms began. Half received intravenous alteplase and half received standard medical care. At 90 days, 38.4% of the alteplase group had little or no disability versus 18.2% of the standard-care group; symptomatic bleeding in the brain was more common with alteplase (4.0% vs 1.0%), but death rates were essentially identical (17.2% vs 16.2%).

**Why it matters:** Older stroke patients have often been considered too frail or too late for clot-dissolving drugs, yet this analysis suggests age alone — and a delay well beyond the traditional 4.5-hour window — should not automatically rule out treatment when imaging shows tissue worth saving.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or someone with you has stroke symptoms, get to a hospital immediately — but also know that arriving late, or being in your 80s, does not necessarily mean the window for treatment has closed. Ask whether advanced brain imaging can determine if clot-dissolving treatment is still an option, and ask about the bleeding risk alongside the potential benefit.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living staff and family caregivers, this strengthens the case for documenting the exact time symptoms were last known normal and transporting residents to stroke centers capable of perfusion imaging, rather than assuming a late presentation means nothing can be done.

**Caveats:** Post hoc secondary analysis of a subgroup (N=198) not designed to answer this question; conducted entirely in China, so results may not generalize to all populations; symptomatic brain bleeding was roughly five times more likely with treatment; short 90-day follow-up.

### 8. Mediterranean-style eating linked to 38-45% lower risk of three more cancers

**Journal:** *Geroscience* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 15
**PMID:** 42744960 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11357-026-02525-z

**The study:** A systematic review and meta-analysis pooled 34 observational studies covering 4,286,835 participants to compare cancer risk in people with the highest versus lowest adherence to a Mediterranean dietary pattern. High adherence was associated with a 45% lower risk of head and neck cancer, a 38% lower risk of gastric cancer, and a 41% lower risk of liver cancer. The protective pattern echoes previously established findings for colorectal cancer.

**Why it matters:** Cancers of the digestive tract and upper airway rise sharply with age, and this analysis suggests a familiar, affordable eating pattern — vegetables, fruit, whole grains, legumes, fish, and olive oil — tracks with substantially lower risk across several of them.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** You don't need a specialty diet to act on this: shifting toward more vegetables, legumes, fish, and olive oil while cutting back on processed and red meat is the pattern these millions of participants followed. Talk to your doctor or a dietitian about adapting it to your appetite, chewing ability, budget, and any medication or kidney restrictions.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For dining directors and food service teams in senior living, this is ammunition for menu redesign — but the practical challenge is palatability and texture modification for residents with swallowing or dental issues, which the research does not address.

**Caveats:** Observational studies only — cannot prove the diet causes lower cancer risk; dietary intake was self-reported; substantial heterogeneity between studies for head and neck (I²=90%) and gastric cancer (I²=76%); two authors hold senior editorial positions at the publishing journal.

### 9. Loneliness in later life tracks with income, housing and internet access — not just personality

**Journal:** *J Gerontol B Psychol Sci Soc Sci* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 15
**PMID:** 42742397 | **DOI:** 10.1093/geronb/gbag195

**The study:** Researchers followed 8,164 adults aged 60 and over across three waves (2014–2019) of the English Longitudinal Study on Ageing, measuring the gap between how lonely people felt and how socially isolated they actually were. People with university education, higher household income, and higher perceived social standing tended toward "social resilience" — feeling less lonely than their thin networks would predict. Renting privately or in social housing, living in a deprived or densely populated area, being unemployed, and rarely or never using the internet were all independently linked to "social vulnerability" — feeling lonelier than the size of their network would suggest.

**Why it matters:** It reframes later-life loneliness as partly a structural problem of money, housing security and digital access, meaning individual advice to "join a club" may miss what is actually driving the distress.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you feel lonely despite having people around you, the problem may not be your social skills or attitude. Practical supports — reliable internet and the skills to use it, secure housing, transportation, help with income supports — appear to matter as much as the number of people in your life.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living operators and community programs, the finding on rare or no internet use points to digital inclusion — devices, Wi-Fi and hands-on coaching — as a loneliness intervention, and suggests targeting outreach to renters and residents in deprived areas rather than offering the same activity calendar to everyone.

**Caveats:** Observational cohort — cannot establish causation; loneliness measured by self-report (UCLA-3); English population only, so housing and internet findings may not transfer to other countries; the loneliness–isolation "asymmetry" score is a constructed measure whose clinical meaning is still being established.

### 10. A blood test for Alzheimer's proteins predicted worsening mood and behavior changes in frail older adults

**Journal:** *Alzheimers Dement (Amst)* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 14
**PMID:** 42745934 | **DOI:** 10.1002/dad2.70392

**The study:** In a secondary analysis of the COGFRAIL study, researchers tracked 174 pre-frail and frail older adults with mild cognitive impairment (mean age 82.9) for two years, measuring plasma phosphorylated tau (p-tau181) at the start. Participants in the highest third of p-tau181 levels went on to develop more domains of mild behavioral impairment — things like apathy, irritability, anxiety and disinhibition — and greater severity than those in the lowest third. Analyzed as a continuous measure rather than by thirds, the trend pointed the same way but fell short of statistical significance.

**Why it matters:** It supports the idea that new personality, mood or motivation changes in later life can be an early manifestation of Alzheimer's biology rather than a separate psychiatric problem — which changes how such changes should be investigated.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you notice a genuine shift in your own motivation, irritability or anxiety that isn't like you, mention it to your doctor as a cognitive symptom, not just a mood one. Blood-based Alzheimer's markers are still a research tool for this purpose, so treat this as a reason to be evaluated, not a reason to seek a specific test.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and care staff, the message is that apathy or uncharacteristic irritability in a frail resident deserves the same attention as memory complaints — and documenting when the change started can help clinicians distinguish emerging neurodegeneration from depression, pain or medication effects.

**Caveats:** Small sample (N=174), single-center secondary analysis; behavioral impairment was approximated with an algorithm applied to a caregiver questionnaire (NPI-Q) rather than formally diagnosed; continuous analysis was not significant, and the finding held only for the highest tertile; participants were pre-frail or frail with mild cognitive impairment, so results may not generalize; several authors report extensive pharmaceutical industry ties.

### 11. A routine liver-enzyme ratio tracked faster cognitive decline — especially in people carrying the APOE ε4 gene

**Journal:** *Alzheimers Dement (Amst)* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 13
**PMID:** 42741023 | **DOI:** 10.1002/dad2.70484

**The study:** Using data from 2,709 participants in the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative, researchers examined whether the AST/ALT ratio — a liver enzyme index already reported on standard blood panels — predicted cognitive trajectories. Higher baseline AST/ALT was associated with faster decline on cognitive tests and faster thinning of Alzheimer's-signature brain cortex, with stronger associations in APOE ε4 carriers. However, the ratio added almost nothing to predicting who would clinically progress beyond what APOE status and baseline diagnosis already told researchers.

**Why it matters:** It hints that liver and metabolic health may interact with genetic risk in brain aging, while also honestly showing that a cheap existing blood value is not a useful standalone screening test for dementia risk.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Don't ask for your AST/ALT ratio as a dementia test — it added essentially no predictive value here. The more useful takeaway is that liver and metabolic health appear intertwined with brain aging, which makes the ordinary advice about alcohol, weight and metabolic conditions worth revisiting with your doctor.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For care professionals, this is a caution against over-interpreting incidental lab values as dementia risk signals in resident records; the authors themselves note the added discriminative value was negligible.

**Caveats:** Observational cohort — cannot establish causation; ADNI participants are volunteers who are disproportionately white, educated and research-motivated, limiting generalizability; the authors state findings require external validation; interaction effects were significant in some models but multiplicative and additive interactions for clinical progression were null.

### 12. Eye scans after a mild stroke signaled who would lose cognitive ground over the next year

**Journal:** *Neurology* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 13
**PMID:** 42743445 | **DOI:** 10.1212/WNL.0000000000218502

**The study:** Researchers in Edinburgh gave optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA) eye scans, brain MRIs and cognitive testing to 189 patients (mean age 64.8) after a lacunar or mild cortical ischemic stroke, then repeated the assessments a year later in 154 of them. Declines in retinal vessel density over the year tracked with growth of white matter hyperintensities — a marker of cerebral small vessel disease — and with falling scores on the Montreal Cognitive Assessment. Lower retinal vessel density at baseline was associated with worse cognitive scores one year later.

**Why it matters:** Because the retina's tiny blood vessels can be photographed non-invasively in minutes, this raises the prospect of using an eye scan to flag people whose small brain vessels are deteriorating and whose thinking may be at risk.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** This is early-stage research, not a test to request yet — but if you've had a stroke or have high blood pressure or diabetes, it reinforces why keeping regular eye appointments matters and why controlling blood pressure protects the same small vessels in your eyes and your brain.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For rehabilitation teams and families supporting someone after a mild stroke, the practical point is that cognitive decline can continue quietly over the following year even when physical recovery looks good — repeat cognitive checks at 12 months are worth requesting.

**Caveats:** Modest sample (N=189, 154 at follow-up) from a single hospital stroke service; observational design cannot establish causation; participants were mostly younger than the typical senior living population (mean age 64.8) and only 37% female; some retinal findings were inconsistent in direction (increased vessel density linked to increased perivascular space volume); one-year follow-up; authors call for validation in larger cohorts.

### 13. When everyday tasks start slipping, depression may be part of what shortens life

**Journal:** *Geroscience* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 17
**PMID:** 42753032 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11357-026-02536-w

**The study:** Researchers followed 6,033 South Korean adults aged 45 and older, tracking their ability to handle instrumental activities of daily living — things like managing money, shopping, taking medications, using transportation — from 2006 to 2014, then recorded deaths through 2024. About 4.6% of participants fell into an 'increasing limitation' group, and they were more than twice as likely to die during follow-up as those who stayed independent (adjusted hazard ratio 2.13). Depressive symptoms accounted for roughly 16% of that excess mortality risk.

**Why it matters:** Losing the ability to run errands or manage medications is not just an inconvenience — it tracks with a doubled death rate, and the emotional fallout of that loss appears to be one modifiable piece of the picture.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If tasks you once handled easily — bills, groceries, prescriptions, getting around town — are becoming harder, treat it as a health signal worth raising with your doctor, and mention your mood too. Difficulty with these tasks often comes bundled with low mood, and depression is treatable even when the underlying physical limitation is not.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and senior living staff: functional decline assessments are often done for care-planning purposes only. This study argues for pairing them with depression screening, since the emotional response to losing independence may be a treatable contributor to worse outcomes, not just an understandable reaction.

**Caveats:** Observational cohort — cannot establish causation; depression may be both cause and consequence of functional decline. Only 276 people were in the increasing-limitation group. Single-country (South Korea) sample beginning at age 45 may not generalize to other health systems or older-old populations.

### 14. A brain scan changed the diagnosis for one in three patients with unclear parkinsonism

**Journal:** *J Clin Neurosci* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 17
**PMID:** 42753528 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jocn.2026.112305

**The study:** Researchers pooled 75 sets of observations covering 2,232 patients whose parkinsonian symptoms — tremor, stiffness, slowness — could not be confidently diagnosed on clinical examination alone, then looked at what happened after they received a DaTscan (a specialized SPECT imaging test of the brain's dopamine system). The diagnosis was revised in about 32% of cases overall. Revision was most common when doctors had initially suspected vascular parkinsonism (53%) or drug-induced parkinsonism (38%), and still occurred in 22% of those initially labeled as having Parkinson's disease.

**Why it matters:** Older adults are frequently given a tentative Parkinson's label based on exam findings alone, which can lead to years of dopaminergic medication — or to missed treatment — and this analysis quantifies how often imaging overturns that initial impression.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have been told your tremor or slowed movement 'might be Parkinson's' and the diagnosis still feels uncertain after months of treatment, it is reasonable to ask your neurologist whether a DaTscan could clarify things — particularly if your symptoms were attributed to small strokes, or began after starting a new medication.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For care staff and families: a sizable share of parkinsonism in older residents is drug-induced or vascular rather than degenerative. That distinction changes prognosis, medication plans and fall-risk management, so it is worth knowing whether a resident's diagnosis was confirmed by imaging or is still presumptive.

**Caveats:** Meta-analysis of observational studies with substantial heterogeneity (I² up to 92%); 'diagnostic revision' does not by itself prove the new diagnosis was correct, as few studies had pathological confirmation. No data on whether revised diagnoses improved patient outcomes.

### 15. Smartphone brain-training apps showed cognitive gains in nearly every trial reviewed

**Journal:** *J Alzheimers Dis* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 17
**PMID:** 42754558 | **DOI:** 10.1177/13872877261488814

**The study:** Researchers screened 3,140 abstracts and identified 19 randomized controlled trials, published between 2010 and 2025, testing mobile app-based interventions in older adults with subjective cognitive decline or mild cognitive impairment. Seven trials focused on people who simply felt their memory slipping; most of the rest enrolled patients with diagnosed mild cognitive impairment. All but one trial reported improvement on at least one cognitive measure, with memory showing the most consistent benefit.

**Why it matters:** Subjective cognitive decline and mild cognitive impairment each affect up to roughly a quarter of older adults, and app-delivered programs require no travel — a real advantage for people who have stopped driving or live far from a memory clinic.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have noticed your memory slipping, app-based cognitive training is a low-risk option worth discussing at your next appointment — but ask specifically about programs that have been tested in trials rather than picking a commercial 'brain game' off the app store, and be realistic that the measured gains were mostly on memory tests, not proven prevention of dementia.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living communities weighing technology purchases: this review supports building structured, evidence-based app programs into wellness offerings, and pairs naturally with the digital-skills support residents say they want but rarely receive. Staff time for onboarding and troubleshooting is the usual bottleneck.

**Caveats:** Systematic review with narrative synthesis — individual trials were small with short follow-up, no pooled effect size reported, and 'improvement in at least one cognitive outcome' is a lenient benchmark vulnerable to selective reporting. No evidence that test-score gains translate into delayed dementia or better daily functioning. Publication bias toward positive results is likely.

### 16. Gum disease keeps turning up in Alzheimer's patients — and treating it moved one brain marker

**Journal:** *J Alzheimers Dis* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 17
**PMID:** 42754555 | **DOI:** 10.1177/13872877261488806

**The study:** Spanish researchers systematically reviewed 11 studies from the past five years examining the biological link between periodontitis and Alzheimer's disease, eight of which analyzed brain tissue, blood, saliva, gum fluid or cerebrospinal fluid. Patients with Alzheimer's consistently showed higher levels of gum-disease bacteria including Porphyromonas gingivalis, Fusobacterium nucleatum and Prevotella intermedia, along with links between bacterial load and systemic inflammatory markers. Notably, periodontal treatment was associated with a reduction in an imaging-based marker of preclinical Alzheimer's, though findings were inconsistent and some studies found no significant difference.

**Why it matters:** Dental care is one of the first things to lapse in later life and in dementia care, yet this line of evidence suggests oral inflammation may be more than a mouth problem.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Keeping up with dental cleanings and treating bleeding or receding gums is worth doing for reasons that are already well established — and this review adds a possible brain-health rationale. It does not show that treating gum disease prevents dementia, so approach it as good general health maintenance rather than a memory intervention.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and care communities: oral hygiene is chronically under-resourced in dementia care, and residents who can no longer brush effectively or report tooth pain are at particular risk. This review strengthens the case for making dental access and daily mouth care a tracked quality measure, not an optional extra.

**Caveats:** Systematic review of heterogeneous observational studies — cannot establish causation, and reverse causation is plausible since cognitive decline itself degrades oral hygiene. Findings were inconsistent, with some studies showing no significant association. The treatment finding rests on a surrogate neuroimaging marker, not clinical outcomes; the authors explicitly say causality cannot be established.

### 17. An AI reads aging in your retina — and diabetes and smoking, not blood pressure, showed up

**Journal:** *Geroscience* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 17
**PMID:** 42753030 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11357-026-02538-8

**The study:** Researchers trained a deep learning model on 29,530 retinal photographs from 7,535 people at a Korean health screening center to estimate a 'retinal age,' then compared that estimate with each person's actual age to produce a retinal age gap. Diabetes was linked to retinas that looked 2.5 years older, smoking to about half a year older, cataract to 1.9 years and macular degeneration to 0.6 years older — while hypertension and high cholesterol showed no association at all. Married participants had slightly younger-looking retinas. The authors stress the tool currently suits population-level research, not individual risk prediction.

**Why it matters:** Retinal photographs are cheap, quick and already part of routine eye exams, making them an attractive window on biological aging — but this analysis also shows the signal is narrower than the hype suggests.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Don't expect your optometrist to hand you a biological age score yet — the researchers themselves say this tool isn't ready for individual predictions. The more useful takeaway is that diabetes left the largest aging imprint on the retina of any factor measured, which is one more reason to keep blood sugar and annual dilated eye exams on your calendar.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living and clinical staff: expect 'retinal age' and similar AI biological-age products to be marketed to residents and families. This paper from the developers themselves is useful ammunition for asking vendors whether their tool has been validated longitudinally against real health outcomes.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional and observational — no follow-up to show whether a higher retinal age gap predicts disease or death, which the authors identify as the key missing validation. Single-center health screening cohort in South Korea, a self-selected and likely healthier and wealthier group than the general population; results may not generalize across ethnicities. Effect sizes for most factors were under one year, near the model's own 2.5-year margin of error.

### 18. Apathy — not depression or anxiety — signals who will progress from mild cognitive impairment to Alzheimer's

**Journal:** *Int Psychogeriatr* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 16
**PMID:** 42749536 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.inpsyc.2026.100275

**The study:** Researchers pooled 22 longitudinal cohort studies covering 346,185 people with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) to test whether three common mood and behavior symptoms at baseline predicted later conversion to clinical Alzheimer's dementia. Apathy — loss of motivation, initiative and interest — was associated with roughly 2.6 times the risk of progression. Depression was not associated with progression overall (and only reached significance in studies following people three or more years), and anxiety showed no significant association at all.

**Why it matters:** Apathy is easy to dismiss as 'just getting older' or mistaken for depression, but this analysis suggests it is the single most informative behavioral warning sign in people already diagnosed with mild cognitive impairment.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have been told you have mild cognitive impairment and you have lost the drive to do things you used to enjoy — not sadness, but a flatness and lack of initiative — mention that specifically at your next appointment. Ask your clinician to distinguish apathy from depression, because this research suggests they carry very different prognostic weight.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and memory-care staff: the resident who has stopped signing up for activities may be flagging cognitive risk, not simply becoming antisocial. Documenting withdrawal and loss of initiative as distinct from low mood could improve how care teams stratify who needs closer cognitive monitoring.

**Caveats:** Meta-analysis of observational cohort studies — associations cannot establish that apathy causes progression, and it may simply be an early manifestation of the disease process. Substantial heterogeneity between studies required statistical adjustment; the depression subgroup difference did not reach conventional significance. Neuropsychiatric symptom measurement varied across the included cohorts.

### 19. Autopsies of Alzheimer's trial volunteers find most had multiple brain diseases, not just one

**Journal:** *J Prev Alzheimers Dis* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 15
**PMID:** 42743907 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.tjpad.2026.100669

**The study:** Researchers at the University of Kentucky reviewed 614 brain autopsies from a community-based cohort and identified 67 people who had enrolled in therapeutic dementia trials — prevention trials in cognitively normal adults, treatment trials for mild cognitive impairment or early dementia, and vascular cognitive impairment trials. Every intervention had been designed to target a single disease mechanism, yet only 10% to 29% of participants (depending on trial type) turned out to have that pathology alone. On average, trial participants had 2.54 distinct brain pathologies each, rising to 3.2 in the mild cognitive impairment and dementia group, with amyloid, tau, alpha-synuclein, TDP-43 and cerebrovascular disease commonly coexisting.

**Why it matters:** It helps explain why so many single-target dementia drugs have produced modest results, and it means an older adult with memory problems most likely has several overlapping brain diseases rather than pure Alzheimer's.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you are considering enrolling in a dementia trial or weighing a new amyloid-targeting drug, it is fair to ask how much of your own cognitive decline is likely driven by the target the treatment addresses — and whether biomarker testing could help answer that before you commit.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and senior living clinicians: 'mixed dementia' is the norm, not an unusual diagnosis. That reframes care planning — vascular risk management, sleep, and Parkinson-related symptoms may all be in play alongside Alzheimer's, and expecting one drug to fix everything sets families up for disappointment.

**Caveats:** Small trial-engaged subgroup (n=67), single-center cohort from one region of Kentucky, retrospective and observational. Autopsy cohorts are self-selected and may not represent all trial participants nationally; participants had marginally higher education than trial-naive peers.

### 20. RSV shots look effective for older adults on immune-suppressing drugs — but protection may fade faster

**Journal:** *Rheumatology (Oxford)* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 17
**PMID:** 42755043 | **DOI:** 10.1093/rheumatology/keag513

**The study:** Because people with systemic autoimmune rheumatic diseases — rheumatoid arthritis, lupus and related conditions — were excluded from the original RSV vaccine trials, researchers systematically reviewed 49 studies to piece together what is known about RSV risk, vaccine effectiveness and safety in this group. RSV vaccines showed 60-73% first-season effectiveness in immunocompromised adults, but protection appeared weaker and waned more quickly than in people with normal immune systems, and antibody responses were reduced. No consistent autoimmune flare or safety signal emerged, though only one study reported actual RSV outcomes in rheumatic disease patients and none analyzed effectiveness by type of immune-suppressing drug.

**Why it matters:** Millions of older adults take DMARDs or biologics for arthritis and related conditions, and this is the clearest summary yet of an evidence gap that leaves them making vaccination decisions without data tailored to them.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you take methotrexate, a biologic or another immune-suppressing drug for arthritis or lupus, the available evidence supports RSV vaccination for you — but ask your rheumatologist about timing relative to your medication doses and whether your protection may need revisiting sooner than for other older adults.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For care teams and senior living staff: residents on immunosuppressants may be both higher-risk for RSV hospitalization and less durably protected by vaccination, which argues for keeping RSV in the differential during respiratory season rather than assuming a vaccinated resident is covered.

**Caveats:** Systematic review of mostly observational data and pharmacovigilance reports, not new trial data. Only one study reported RSV outcomes specifically in rheumatic disease patients; no drug-class-specific effectiveness data exist. ### 21. Lower blood levels of the 'anti-aging' molecule NAD+ tracked with frailty in 529 Japanese seniors — with a catch

**Journal:** *Geroscience* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 16
**PMID:** 42744962 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11357-026-02545-9

**The study:** Researchers measured whole-blood NAD+ — a metabolic molecule heavily marketed in supplement form — in 529 community-dwelling Japanese adults aged 65 and older from the Itabashi Longitudinal Study on Aging. The 32 participants (6%) who met frailty criteria had lower median NAD+ levels than the rest (24.0 versus 25.0 µmol/L), and each 1 µmol/L increase was associated with about 13% lower odds of frailty after adjusting for age, sex and fasting time. Notably, the association weakened and lost statistical significance once the researchers adjusted for hematocrit, suggesting blood cell composition may partly explain the link.

**Why it matters:** NAD+ boosters are among the fastest-growing supplements aimed at older adults, and this is among the first human studies to connect circulating NAD+ with frailty — while also showing how fragile that connection is.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Before you spend money on NAD+ precursor supplements, know that this study found only a modest association that largely disappeared when researchers accounted for red blood cell concentration — and it cannot show that raising NAD+ would prevent or reverse frailty.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and senior living professionals fielding questions about NAD+ products: this is early, cross-sectional human data, not evidence of benefit. The proven levers against frailty — resistance exercise, adequate protein, medication review — still have far stronger support.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design cannot establish causation or direction. Only 32 frail participants, making estimates imprecise. The key association was attenuated after hematocrit adjustment, hinting the signal may reflect blood composition rather than cellular NAD+ metabolism. Single Japanese urban cohort of community-dwelling volunteers may not generalize.

### 22. Where it hurts may predict how depression unfolds in midlife and later life

**Journal:** *J Affect Disord* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 16
**PMID:** 42749164 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jad.2026.122520

**The study:** Using seven years of data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study, researchers followed 1,047 adults aged 45 and older who already had depressive symptoms at baseline and mapped how those symptoms changed. Three paths emerged: mild and stable (13%), moderate and worsening (58%), and high and persistent (29%). Pain in specific locations — particularly head, neck, arm, finger, wrist, toe and stomach — was associated with the worse trajectories, while self-reported sleep duration explained only a small share of the link (at most about 9%).

**Why it matters:** It suggests that among older adults with depression, asking precisely where the pain is may carry prognostic information, and that treating pain — not just sleep — deserves attention in depression care.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you live with both persistent pain and low mood, it is worth describing to your doctor exactly which body sites hurt rather than just saying 'I ache' — this research suggests pain location tracks with how depression plays out over years, and treating the pain may be part of treating the mood.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For caregivers and care staff: a resident whose depression is not improving may have under-treated pain in less obvious places — hands, feet, head, stomach — rather than the back or knees clinicians ask about by default. Sleep support alone appears unlikely to break the pain-depression cycle.

**Caveats:** Observational cohort — cannot establish causation, and pain and depression likely influence each other. Pain and sleep duration were self-reported. Sample was Chinese adults aged 45 and older, so findings include but are not specific to those over 65 and may not generalize elsewhere. Indirect-effect analyses were explicitly exploratory.

### 23. A common anesthesia add-on may cut post-surgery confusion in older patients by nearly half

**Journal:** *BMC Geriatr* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 14
**PMID:** 42750012 | **DOI:** 10.1186/s12877-026-08201-3

**The study:** Researchers pooled 17 randomized controlled trials involving 2,914 surgical patients aged 60 and older to test whether adding esketamine — a ketamine derivative used for pain and sedation — to the anesthesia plan reduced postoperative delirium. Regimens containing esketamine were linked to about a 43% lower odds of delirium (OR 0.57) and less postoperative nausea and vomiting, with no increase in psychiatric side effects such as hallucinations or agitation. A smaller set of four trials hinted at less postoperative cognitive dysfunction, but the authors rated that evidence as very low certainty.

**Why it matters:** Postoperative delirium is one of the most feared complications of surgery in later life, often triggering longer hospital stays, functional loss and a move to higher levels of care — so a low-cost drug already stocked in operating rooms that might reduce it is a meaningful lead.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have surgery scheduled, delirium prevention is a fair topic to raise at your pre-op anesthesia consultation: ask what the team's plan is for reducing postoperative confusion, and whether medications like esketamine are part of their protocol. The evidence is still preliminary, so treat it as a question to explore with your anesthesiologist rather than a drug to request.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and senior living staff, the practical takeaway is preparation regardless of drug choice: know your resident's or relative's baseline mental status before surgery, so any post-op change is recognized fast, and build in familiar faces, glasses, hearing aids and sleep-wake routines that independently lower delirium risk.

**Caveats:** Meta-analysis of trials with substantial heterogeneity in anesthesia and perioperative management; moderate statistical heterogeneity for the main outcome (I²=45.8%); cognitive dysfunction findings rest on only four trials and were graded very low certainty; most included trials come from single centers, and the review was retrospectively registered.

### 24. Ketogenic diets modestly improved memory and language in older adults with cognitive impairment — but raised LDL cholesterol

**Journal:** *Arch Gerontol Geriatr* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 12
**PMID:** 42753449 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.archger.2026.106409

**The study:** A systematic review and meta-analysis pooled 12 randomized controlled trials with 707 older adults who had mild cognitive impairment or Alzheimer's disease and who followed ketogenic diets or related strategies such as ketone-producing supplements. Overall cognitive function improved modestly compared with control diets (SMD 0.34), with significant gains specifically in memory and language but no measurable benefit for attention or executive function. Blood ketone levels rose as expected, confirming the diets worked biologically — but total cholesterol and LDL cholesterol also went up, while triglycerides and HDL were unchanged.

**Why it matters:** Diet is one of the few dementia levers families can pull themselves, and this is among the clearest pooled signals that ketogenic approaches may help some cognitive domains — while flagging a cardiovascular trade-off that matters a great deal in a population already at risk for stroke and heart disease.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you are considering a ketogenic diet or ketone drink for memory concerns, know that the measured benefits were real but modest and concentrated in memory and word-finding — and that cholesterol rose in these trials, so ask your physician about checking your lipid panel before and after any switch rather than going it alone.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For dining directors and care staff, ketogenic eating is operationally demanding — high fat, very low carbohydrate, and hard to sustain in congregate dining — so the honest conversation with families is about feasibility, palatability and monitoring, not just headline cognitive numbers.

**Caveats:** Pooled sample is small (707 across 12 trials) with variable interventions lumped together as "ketogenic strategies"; effect sizes are modest and outcome measures differ across trials; follow-up durations were generally short, so the long-term cognitive and cardiovascular balance is unknown.

### 25. Ashwagandha improved sleep and menopausal symptoms in a 12-week randomized trial — but the study was small

**Journal:** *Menopause* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 15
**PMID:** 42742071 | **DOI:** 10.1097/GME.0000000000002894

**The study:** In a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial at a single center in Amritsar, India, 66 postmenopausal women aged 45–65 took either 600 mg/day of Withania somnifera (ashwagandha) root extract or placebo for 12 weeks. The ashwagandha group showed substantially greater improvement in sleep quality on the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (−5.2 vs −0.8 points), in menopausal symptom scores (−8.1 vs −1.2), and a small reduction in body fat percentage, with quality-of-life gains across several domains. Bone mineral density did not differ between groups, and no serious adverse events occurred.

**Why it matters:** Poor sleep and persistent menopausal symptoms trouble many women well past midlife, and options that are neither hormonal nor sedative are in short supply — but supplement claims rarely come with placebo-controlled data, so a trial like this deserves attention along with a hard look at its limits.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you are weighing an over-the-counter supplement for sleep or hot flashes, this is one of the few with a real placebo-controlled trial behind it — but bring it up with your clinician and pharmacist first, because ashwagandha can interact with thyroid, sedative and immune-affecting medications and has been linked in rare cases to liver injury.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For care staff and family members managing a long medication list, the lesson is broader than one herb: supplements residents buy on their own belong on the medication reconciliation list, since even well-tolerated botanicals can interact with prescriptions.

**Caveats:** Small sample (N=66, 63 completers), single center in one region of India so results may not generalize; sleep and symptom outcomes are self-reported questionnaires; 12-week follow-up is short; bone density was only an exploratory outcome; no funding source or product supplier was disclosed beyond a statement of no conflicts.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**

- **Supplements marketed for brain and body aging keep failing when randomized.** The previous digest had *A cheap antioxidant didn't protect older patients' thinking after day surgery* (PMID 42736670). The new batch adds two more: *Vitamin D and fish oil didn't shift Alzheimer's blood markers over four years* (PMID 42746141), a four-year randomized null on the two most commonly taken brain supplements, and *Lower blood levels of the 'anti-aging' molecule NAD+ tracked with frailty in 529 Japanese seniors — with a catch* (PMID 42744962), where the association largely dissolved after adjusting for blood cell concentration.
- **Behavior and motivation changes, not memory, as the early signal.** Last week's *Eight years of brain scans show how Parkinson's fatigue takes hold — and it isn't depression* (PMID 42726348) separated a non-motor symptom from its usual misattribution. This week does the same for cognition twice over: *Apathy — not depression or anxiety — signals who will progress from mild cognitive impairment to Alzheimer's* (PMID 42749536) and *A blood test for Alzheimer's proteins predicted worsening mood and behavior changes in frail older adults* (PMID 42745934), which ties the same behavioral shifts to underlying Alzheimer's biology.
- **Everyday-task decline outperforms test scores as a warning sign.** *Trouble cooking a hot meal or managing pills predicted dementia eight years later* (PMID 42720743) is now matched by *When everyday tasks start slipping, depression may be part of what shortens life* (PMID 42753032), where an increasing-limitation trajectory in the same kinds of tasks carried more than double the death rate — with depressive symptoms accounting for roughly 16% of that excess.
- **"Less is more" evidence keeps accumulating.** Previously: *After hip fracture surgery, that routine follow-up X-ray may be doing nothing at all* (PMID 42732263), *One hole or two?* (PMID 42734090), and the thin trial base under WHO's ICOPE framework (PMID 42740566). This week: *Big trial finds carotid surgery doesn't rescue thinking skills in people with symptom-free artery narrowing* (PMID 42747834) and *Three weeks of pre-surgery conditioning didn't keep frail older patients independent a year later* (PMID 42743945), both large randomized tests of widely promoted interventions that came back negative.
- **Loneliness and later-life distress as structural, not personal.** *Where you live may keep depression from lifting* (PMID 42107653) and *Social disadvantage doubled dementia risk* (PMID 42730922) are directly extended by *Loneliness in later life tracks with income, housing and internet access — not just personality* (PMID 42742397), which names renting, deprivation, unemployment and rare internet use as independent markers of feeling lonelier than one's network would predict.

**Advances**

- **Digital tools move from "does it help at all" to "which program, for whom."** Last week's *Handing an older adult a tablet — and teaching them to use it — measurably helps their thinking* (PMID 42734971) found a small pooled cognitive benefit from devices and training. This week, *Smartphone brain-training apps showed cognitive gains in nearly every trial reviewed* (PMID 42754558) narrows that to structured app programs in people with subjective decline or mild cognitive impairment, and *Online support for dementia caregivers helped most those juggling a job alongside care* (PMID 42745561) shows the same delivery channel paying off most for the people least able to attend in person.
- **Alzheimer's blood tests broaden their job description.** *One in six memory-clinic patients got a blood test result that didn't match their brain scan* (PMID 42287769) concerned diagnostic accuracy; *A blood test for Alzheimer's proteins predicted worsening mood and behavior changes in frail older adults* (PMID 42745934) tests a plasma marker as a predictor of future behavioral symptoms rather than amyloid status — a different and more fragile use.
- **Retinal imaging goes from a passing idea to two separate papers.** *Eye scans after a mild stroke signaled who would lose cognitive ground over the next year* (PMID 42743445) and *An AI reads aging in your retina — and diabetes and smoking, not blood pressure, showed up* (PMID 42753030) together advance the eye-as-window theme beyond last week's cheap-marker cluster (grip, waist and blood pressure, PMID 42722214) — while both sets of authors caution against individual use.
- **Parkinson's non-motor and diagnostic work gets more actionable.** After *Parkinson's patients can 'collapse' into sudden immobility* (PMID 42250520) and the fatigue study (PMID 42726348), this week supplies an actual treatment — *Botox injections sharply cut the foot pain that torments many people with Parkinson's* (PMID 42753227) — and a diagnostic reality check, *A brain scan changed the diagnosis for one in three patients with unclear parkinsonism* (PMID 42753528).

**Counterpoints**

- **Something finally moved post-surgical confusion.** *A cheap antioxidant didn't protect older patients' thinking after day surgery* (PMID 42736670) was a null; *A common anesthesia add-on may cut post-surgery confusion in older patients by nearly half* (PMID 42750012) points the other way, with 17 trials and a 43% reduction in delirium odds — though graded cautiously.
- **Age and lateness as exclusion criteria take a hit.** Last week's surgical studies leaned toward doing less in frail older patients. *Clot-busting drug helped stroke patients over 70 even when treatment started up to 24 hours later* (PMID 42749350) argues for doing more, in patients who would often be written off on age or timing alone.
- **Why single-target fixes disappoint.** *Autopsies of Alzheimer's trial volunteers find most had multiple brain diseases, not just one* (PMID 42743907) supplies a mechanism for the pattern of modest and null results across both digests — participants averaged 2.54 distinct brain pathologies each.

**New themes**

- **Diet as a dementia and cancer lever:** *Mediterranean-style eating linked to 38-45% lower risk of three more cancers* (PMID 42744960) and *Ketogenic diets modestly improved memory and language in older adults with cognitive impairment — but raised LDL cholesterol* (PMID 42753449). Nothing comparable last week.
- **Oral health as a brain-health question:** *Gum disease keeps turning up in Alzheimer's patients — and treating it moved one brain marker* (PMID 42754555).
- **Vaccination evidence gaps for excluded populations:** *RSV shots look effective for older adults on immune-suppressing drugs — but protection may fade faster* (PMID 42755043) — thematically adjacent to last week's representation findings (PMIDs 42725877, 42287769) but a first appearance for vaccines.
- **Pain as a driver of depression trajectories:** *Where it hurts may predict how depression unfolds in midlife and later life* (PMID 42749164).
- **Working family caregivers as a distinct group:** PMID 42745561.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** This batch reads like a report card on the brain-health and anti-aging marketplace, and the grades are mixed in a very specific way. Vitamin D and fish oil moved no Alzheimer's blood markers in four years (PMID 42746141), NAD+ — the molecule behind a booming supplement category — showed only a fragile link to frailty that faded after adjusting for blood composition (PMID 42744962), a routine liver-enzyme ratio added essentially nothing to dementia prediction (PMID 42741023), and the developers of an AI "retinal age" tool say plainly it is not ready for individual predictions (PMID 42753030). Meanwhile the things that did show measurable benefit were unglamorous and specific: structured, trial-tested smartphone cognitive programs (PMID 42754558), ketogenic eating with a modest memory gain and a cholesterol trade-off (PMID 42753449), and a Mediterranean-style pattern of eating tracking lower cancer risk (PMID 42744960) — with autopsy evidence that most older brains carry several diseases at once, explaining why any single fix underdelivers (PMID 42743907).

**Why pitch this now:** Supplements, brain games and AI "biological age" scores are being marketed hard to older adults and to the wellness budgets of senior living communities, and this is a rare week where the trial evidence on several of them landed at once — including researchers telling buyers their own tool isn't ready.

**Angle:** A buyer's-guide feature that sorts brain-health spending into what has randomized support, what has a trade-off worth discussing with a doctor, and what is still a research tool being sold as a product — written for older adults and adult children, with a sidebar for wellness and activity directors on the questions to ask a vendor.

**Potential headlines:**
- What the Newest Trials Show About Brain Supplements, Memory Apps and Retinal Age Scans
- The Fish Oil Didn't Budge the Brain Markers — the Phone App Did Better
- Before You Spend Another Dollar on Your Memory, Read This

**Potential outlets:**
- **Next Avenue** — its readers are exactly the people buying supplements and app subscriptions for memory, and it runs data-driven consumer pieces without hype.
- **National Council on Aging** — a natural home for a practical "what's proven versus what's sold" guide, tied to the household cost of ongoing supplement spending.
- **Caring.com** — families comparing communities and wellness offerings can use the vendor and program questions this raises during a tour or an admission conversation.
- **LeadingAge** — the provider-side version: how to evaluate "retinal age," NAD+ and brain-training vendors pitching nonprofit communities, and what evidence to demand first.

- **Three weeks of pre-surgery conditioning didn't keep frail older patients independent a year later (PMID 42743945):** Prehabilitation felt good and was safe but did not protect independence at 12 months, with the authors advising against universal programs — for a senior-living and health-system trade brief on where to put rehab dollars instead.
- **Botox injections sharply cut the foot pain that torments many people with Parkinson's (PMID 42753227):** Painful toe curling and foot cramping is often filed as arthritis or "just cramps"; how to document when it happens relative to medication doses so a specialist can recognize a treatable dystonia, for a caregiver-advice column or a Parkinson's community publication.
- **Vitamin D and fish oil didn't shift Alzheimer's blood markers over four years (PMID 42746141):** A supplement-list review piece — what to keep, what to question, and how to raise cost with a doctor, for a consumer health explainer.
- **Online support for dementia caregivers helped most those juggling a job alongside care (PMID 42745561):** Working caregivers got the biggest benefit from flexible online support; how to ask an employer or dementia service whether a program is available, for a caregiver-advice column and a workplace-benefits or aging-services trade piece.
- **Medicare data reveal four very different hospital paths after a heart failure diagnosis (PMID 42750129):** Most readmission risk clusters in the weeks after discharge, but roughly one in ten faces rising risk years later — the discharge questions to ask, for a consumer health explainer plus a discharge-planning trade brief.
- **Clot-busting drug helped stroke patients over 70 even when treatment started up to 24 hours later (PMID 42749350):** Late arrival and advanced age do not automatically close the treatment window if imaging shows salvageable tissue — why recording "last known normal" and choosing a stroke center with perfusion imaging matters, for a local-news health segment and a senior-living clinical brief.
- **Mediterranean-style eating linked to 38-45% lower risk of three more cancers (PMID 42744960):** A practical adaptation piece — how to shift toward this pattern with a limited budget, a small appetite, or chewing and swallowing difficulties, for a consumer nutrition column and a dining-director trade brief.

- **A blood test for Alzheimer's proteins predicted worsening mood and behavior changes in frail older adults (PMID 42745934):** New apathy, irritability or anxiety deserves the same attention as a memory complaint — and why this is a reason to be evaluated, not a test to request, for a caregiver-advice column.
- **A routine liver-enzyme ratio tracked faster cognitive decline — especially in people carrying the APOE ε4 gene (PMID 42741023):** A useful "don't over-read your lab results" piece, with the honest takeaway that metabolic and liver health belong in a brain-health conversation, for a consumer health explainer.
- **Eye scans after a mild stroke signaled who would lose cognitive ground over the next year (PMID 42743445):** The actionable point is that thinking can keep slipping in the year after a mild stroke even when physical recovery looks fine — ask for a repeat cognitive check at 12 months, for a rehabilitation-focused consumer piece or a stroke-recovery column.
- **When everyday tasks start slipping, depression may be part of what shortens life (PMID 42753032):** Pair functional decline assessments with depression screening, since low mood after losing independence is treatable even when the physical limitation isn't, for a caregiver-advice column and a care-planning trade brief.
- **A brain scan changed the diagnosis for one in three patients with unclear parkinsonism (PMID 42753528):** Much parkinsonism in older adults is drug-induced or vascular rather than degenerative; how to find out whether a diagnosis was confirmed by imaging or is still presumptive, for a consumer health explainer and a memory-care trade brief.
- **Smartphone brain-training apps showed cognitive gains in nearly every trial reviewed (PMID 42754558):** How to tell a trial-tested program from an app-store brain game, and what the gains actually were (memory test scores, not dementia prevention), for a consumer technology-and-aging explainer or an activity-director brief.
- **Gum disease keeps turning up in Alzheimer's patients — and treating it moved one brain marker (PMID 42754555):** Daily mouth care and dental access as a tracked quality measure in dementia care — carefully framed, since this does not show that treating gums prevents dementia, for a senior-living operations brief and a caregiver column.
- **An AI reads aging in your retina — and diabetes and smoking, not blood pressure, showed up (PMID 42753030):** Diabetes left the biggest aging imprint on the retina, and the developers say the tool isn't ready for individuals — a template for questioning any "biological age" product, for a consumer health explainer and a technology-purchasing trade brief.

- **RSV shots look effective for older adults on immune-suppressing drugs — but protection may fade faster (PMID 42755043):** What to ask a rheumatologist about vaccine timing around methotrexate or a biologic, and why staff should keep RSV in the picture even for vaccinated residents, for a consumer health explainer ahead of respiratory season and a clinical trade brief.
- **Lower blood levels of the 'anti-aging' molecule NAD+ tracked with frailty in 529 Japanese seniors — with a catch (PMID 42744962):** How to answer a resident or relative asking about NAD+ boosters, and what still has the strongest evidence against frailty — resistance training, protein, medication review, for a consumer health explainer or a wellness-staff brief.
- **Where it hurts may predict how depression unfolds in midlife and later life (PMID 42749164):** Describe exactly which body sites hurt rather than saying "I ache" — hands, feet, head and stomach pain may be the under-treated piece of a depression that isn't lifting, for a consumer mental-health explainer or a caregiver column.
- **A common anesthesia add-on may cut post-surgery confusion in older patients by nearly half (PMID 42750012):** Delirium prevention as a pre-op conversation — what to ask the anesthesia team, plus the non-drug basics (baseline mental status on record, glasses, hearing aids, sleep routines) that help regardless, for a consumer health explainer and a senior-living clinical brief.
- **Ketogenic diets modestly improved memory and language in older adults with cognitive impairment — but raised LDL cholesterol (PMID 42753449):** Real but modest gains, a cholesterol trade-off, and serious feasibility problems in congregate dining — the honest version of this conversation with families, for a consumer nutrition explainer and a dining-services trade brief.
- **Ashwagandha improved sleep and menopausal symptoms in a 12-week randomized trial — but the study was small (PMID 42742071):** One of the few sleep supplements with placebo-controlled data behind it, plus the broader lesson that self-purchased supplements belong on the medication reconciliation list, for a women's health column and a care-staff medication-safety brief.
