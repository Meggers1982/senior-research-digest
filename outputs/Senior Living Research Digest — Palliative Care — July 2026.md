### 1. Most Hospital Patients and Nursing Home Residents Need Palliative Care—Even Without Cancer

**Journal:** *Aging Clinical and Experimental Research* | **Published:** June 28, 2026
**PMID:** 42366281 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40520-026-03445-w

**The study:** Italian researchers assessed over 5,300 adults in hospitals and long-term care facilities using the NECPAL screening tool for palliative care needs. They found that 58% of hospitalized patients and 48% of nursing home residents had unmet palliative care needs. Critically, these needs were not limited to cancer patients—more than half of non-cancer patients also screened positive. Common concerns included functional decline, nutritional problems, swallowing difficulties, delirium, and moderate-to-severe pain.

**Why it matters:** This large study challenges the assumption that palliative care is primarily for cancer patients, revealing that the majority of older adults in hospitals and care facilities could benefit from a comfort-focused approach to care.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or a loved one is hospitalized or living in a care facility, you can ask your care team whether palliative care services might help manage symptoms like pain, difficulty eating, or confusion—regardless of whether cancer is involved.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Facilities should consider routine screening for palliative care needs among all residents, not just those with terminal diagnoses, to ensure symptom management and quality of life are prioritized.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design (snapshot in time); single-country data may not generalize to all healthcare systems.

### 2. Aged Care Staff Want to Talk About End-of-Life Earlier—But Face Barriers

**Journal:** *Australasian Journal on Ageing* | **Published:** June 2026
**PMID:** 42163024 | **DOI:** 10.1111/ajag.70184

**The study:** Researchers interviewed 64 aged care staff across 14 Australian facilities about communicating with families regarding end-of-life planning. Staff reported wanting to begin conversations early but often faced obstacles—families reluctant to discuss death, no clear medical prognosis, and lack of confidence among less experienced staff. Experienced clinical staff tended to be more direct about decline.

**Why it matters:** The findings highlight a gap between staff intentions and family readiness, suggesting that both staff training and family education could improve the quality and timeliness of end-of-life conversations.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Consider starting conversations about your wishes for care early—before a health crisis—so your family and care team are prepared to honor your preferences.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Facilities may benefit from providing structured resources and training to help staff initiate end-of-life discussions earlier and with greater confidence, reducing last-minute decision-making stress for families.

**Caveats:** Qualitative study (explores experiences, not prevalence); findings specific to Australian aged care context.

### 3. People Living Alone or With Dementia Less Likely to Have End-of-Life Needs Met

**Journal:** *Age and Ageing* | **Published:** July 2, 2026
**PMID:** 42407095 | **DOI:** 10.1093/ageing/afag202

**The study:** Analyzing end-of-life interviews from over 500 deceased participants in England (mean age 83), researchers found that while most had their social and psychological needs met in the last three months of life, those living alone or with dementia were significantly less likely to receive adequate support. Family members—not formal services—were the primary source of non-medical support.

**Why it matters:** Heavy reliance on family networks creates gaps for those without close support systems, particularly older adults living alone or those with cognitive impairment.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you live alone or have limited family nearby, consider discussing your social and emotional support needs with your healthcare provider—formal services may be available to fill gaps.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Communities and healthcare systems should develop more robust formal support services for isolated seniors, especially those with dementia, to ensure equitable end-of-life care.

**Caveats:** Observational design (cannot prove causation); proxy interviews may not perfectly capture the deceased person's experience.

### 4. Palliative Care Rounds in Nursing Homes May Reduce Emergency Visits and Hospital Admissions

**Journal:** *Age and Ageing* | **Published:** June 1, 2026
**PMID:** 42315168 | **DOI:** 10.1093/ageing/afag173

**The study:** An Australian health service implemented "palliative care needs rounds"—specialist outreach visits—in 39 of 56 residential aged care homes. Compared to a similar period two years earlier, emergency department visits dropped 20% and hospital admissions fell 64% among residents, with estimated monthly cost savings of nearly AU$500,000.

**Why it matters:** Bringing palliative care expertise directly into nursing homes may reduce disruptive hospital transfers for frail older residents while saving healthcare resources.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Ask your nursing home whether specialist palliative care support is available on-site—these services may help you manage symptoms without needing emergency care.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Aged care facilities and health systems should consider adopting palliative care rounds as a model for improving resident comfort and reducing costly, often distressing hospital transfers.

**Caveats:** Before-and-after design without a control group; findings coincided with the COVID-19 pandemic, which may have influenced hospital utilization patterns; full implementation costs not captured.

### 5. Older Adults With Dementia in Nursing Homes Less Likely to Complete Advance Care Planning Despite Higher Needs

**Journal:** *Journal of Geriatric Oncology* | **Published:** May 2026
**PMID:** 41967253 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jgo.2026.102972

**The study:** In 15 German nursing homes offering advance care planning (ACP), researchers tracked 342 deceased residents, 22% of whom had cancer. Cancer residents survived a median of 227 days versus 661 days for those without cancer, yet both groups received ACP consultations at similar rates (~40%). However, cancer residents less often completed the ACP process or documented care preferences before death.

**Why it matters:** Despite shorter survival and greater end-of-life care needs, nursing home residents with cancer are not receiving timely advance care planning, highlighting a structural gap in care.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or a loved one has cancer and is in a nursing home, consider prioritizing conversations about care preferences early—these discussions may not happen automatically.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Nursing homes should establish protocols to initiate advance care planning promptly for residents with cancer, given their often rapid decline.

**Caveats:** Single-region study in Germany; cross-sectional design; findings may not generalize to other healthcare systems.

### 6. Comorbid Dementia Linked to Poorer Quality of Death Across Major Diseases

**Journal:** *Archives of Gerontology and Geriatrics* | **Published:** June 2026
**PMID:** 41856050 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.archger.2026.106218

**The study:** A nationwide Japanese survey of bereaved families (N=45,509 decedents aged 65+) compared end-of-life outcomes between those with and without comorbid dementia. Across cancer and heart disease, those with dementia were far less likely to be aware of their condition, communicate with loved ones, or express their care preferences. They also had fewer end-of-life discussions.

**Why it matters:** Dementia significantly undermines the ability to participate in end-of-life planning, underscoring the need for earlier conversations and dementia-inclusive care protocols.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or a family member has memory concerns, consider discussing care wishes and completing advance directives now—before cognitive changes make these conversations more difficult.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Healthcare systems should integrate dementia-specific advance care planning early in the disease course and train clinicians to engage families as proxies when communication becomes limited.

**Caveats:** Observational design; bereaved family reports may introduce recall bias; Japanese healthcare context may differ from other countries.

### 7. Training Improves Clinicians' Confidence in Life-Sustaining Treatment Conversations

**Journal:** *Geriatric Nursing* | **Published:** June 2026
**PMID:** 41932056 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.gerinurse.2026.104017

**The study:** Forty-five advanced practice clinicians providing home-based geriatric care received a one-hour online training on discussing Physician Orders for Life-Sustaining Treatment (POLST). Three months later, there were significant improvements in POLST documentation, comfort with end-of-life conflict discussions, and use of structured communication frameworks.

**Why it matters:** Brief, targeted training can meaningfully improve clinicians' ability to have difficult conversations about life-sustaining treatment with older adults and families.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're receiving home-based care, ask your clinician about POLST—these forms document your wishes and can guide medical decisions if you become unable to speak for yourself.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Home care organizations should invest in brief, practical communication training to ensure clinicians are prepared to discuss end-of-life preferences with patients and families.

**Caveats:** Quality improvement initiative (not a controlled trial); single organization; modest sample size.

### 8. Home-Based Dementia Care Models With Nurse Practitioners Show Promising Outcomes

**Journal:** *Research in Gerontological Nursing* | **Published:** June 23, 2026
**PMID:** 42343547 | **DOI:** 10.3928/19404921-20260612-01

**The study:** A scoping review identified 10 models of home-based dementia care using nurse practitioners (NPs) for longitudinal care. Reported outcomes included high-quality care, reduced emergency department use, shorter hospital stays, delayed nursing home placement, and high rates of advance care planning and palliative care referrals.

**Why it matters:** For people with advancing dementia who struggle to access office-based care, NP-led home-based models may support aging in place and improve access to palliative services.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If traveling to medical appointments has become difficult due to dementia, ask your healthcare provider whether home-based care programs with nurse practitioners are available in your area.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Health systems should consider expanding NP-led home dementia care programs to fill gaps in access, particularly for those who cannot attend traditional clinic visits.

**Caveats:** Scoping review (describes existing models; does not compare effectiveness); quality of evidence varied across included studies.

### 9. Palliative Care Intervention May Benefit Nursing Home Residents With Dementia

**Journal:** *Research in Gerontological Nursing* | **Published:** June 23, 2026
**PMID:** 42343545 | **DOI:** 10.3928/19404921-20260615-01

**The study:** In a pilot trial (N=52), skilled nursing facility residents with and without dementia received a primary palliative care intervention delivered by trained nurse practitioners. While no statistically significant effect was found overall, quality-of-life scores for residents with dementia trended toward improvement, whereas those without dementia trended toward worsening.

**Why it matters:** These preliminary findings suggest that palliative care interventions may need to be tailored differently for residents with versus without dementia.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or a loved one with dementia is in a skilled nursing facility, ask about palliative care services—these may help address comfort and quality-of-life concerns.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Skilled nursing facilities should explore adapting palliative care approaches based on residents' cognitive status, recognizing that needs and responses may differ.

**Caveats:** Small pilot study; no statistically significant findings; exploratory analysis only.

### 10. Caregivers Prioritize Pain Relief and Compassionate Communication in Hip Fracture Decisions

**Journal:** *European Geriatric Medicine* | **Published:** June 2026
**PMID:** 41784868 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s41999-026-01432-y

**The study:** Researchers interviewed caregivers of frail older adults who received a SPING block—a non-operative pain management procedure—for hip fractures. Caregivers valued rapid pain relief and the possibility of early discharge but described feeling burdened by complex decisions, insufficient communication, and uncertainty about long-term effects.

**Why it matters:** Informal caregivers bear significant emotional weight when navigating palliative decisions for frail loved ones, highlighting the need for clearer communication and support.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're facing decisions about treatment for a serious injury like a hip fracture, ask your care team to explain the options clearly, including what to expect afterward.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Healthcare teams should prioritize transparent communication with caregivers about treatment goals and long-term expectations, especially when palliative approaches are considered.

**Caveats:** Single-center qualitative study; small sample; caregiver perspectives only.

### 11. Existential Distress Common Among Older Adults With Colorectal Cancer

**Journal:** *Aging & Mental Health* | **Published:** July 2026
**PMID:** 41498225 | **DOI:** 10.1080/13607863.2025.2612171

**The study:** Seventeen older Iranians (65+) with colorectal cancer participated in in-depth interviews about their experiences of existential distress. Three themes emerged: inadequate self-realization (diminished self-worth, guilt about burdening family), confronting uncertainty and death (fear of the unknown, feeling lost), and disruption of meaningful relationships (stigma, social isolation).

**Why it matters:** Beyond physical symptoms, older cancer patients often experience profound psychological and spiritual suffering shaped by personal beliefs and culture—domains that deserve attention in supportive care.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're facing a serious illness and struggling with feelings of guilt, fear, or isolation, consider speaking with your care team about counseling or spiritual support services.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Oncology and palliative care teams should integrate psychosocial and spiritual assessments into routine care for older cancer patients, particularly those from diverse cultural backgrounds.

**Caveats:** Small qualitative study; single cancer center in Iran; findings may not generalize to other cultures.

### 12. Umbrella Review Identifies Effective Advance Care Planning Strategies

**Journal:** *Archives of Gerontology and Geriatrics* | **Published:** September 2026
**PMID:** 42085825 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.archger.2026.106255

**The study:** Researchers synthesized 61 systematic reviews on advance care planning (ACP) interventions published from 2015–2025. For older adults, structured conversations reduced unnecessary aggressive treatments and aligned care with preferences. Effective tools included video decision aids, web-based platforms, conversation guides, and facilitator training. Peer support improved ACP completion among racial minorities.

**Why it matters:** This comprehensive review provides a roadmap for what works in advance care planning, highlighting both patient-facing tools and clinician training as effective strategies.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Video decision aids and online tools may help you think through your care preferences—ask your healthcare provider if such resources are available.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Healthcare organizations should invest in structured ACP training for clinicians and offer culturally tailored peer support programs to improve ACP completion rates in underserved communities.

**Caveats:** Umbrella review (synthesizes reviews, not original studies); heterogeneity in included reviews.

### 13. Italian Nursing Homes for Dementia Patients Show Major Regional Disparities

**Journal:** *Journal of Alzheimer's Disease* | **Published:** June 2026
**PMID:** 42089822 | **DOI:** 10.1177/13872877261442226

**The study:** A national survey of 1,671 Italian nursing homes housing people with dementia found significant regional disparities. Northern facilities had more beds, better staffing, more digital systems, and greater integration with palliative care. Southern facilities had shorter waiting times but less specialized care. Nationwide, distribution did not match where dementia is most prevalent.

**Why it matters:** Geographic inequities in dementia care infrastructure leave some older Italians with far less access to specialized support than others.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're seeking a nursing home for yourself or a loved one with dementia, ask about staff training, palliative care availability, and access to specialized dementia services.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Policymakers should address regional disparities in dementia care infrastructure to ensure equitable access regardless of where older adults live.

**Caveats:** Survey-based (relies on facility self-report); Italian context may not generalize to other countries.

### 14. Infectious Diseases Are the Leading Cause of Death Among Hospitalized Dementia Patients in Brazil

**Journal:** *Journal of Alzheimer's Disease* | **Published:** June 2026
**PMID:** 42081122 | **DOI:** 10.1177/13872877261445578

**The study:** Reviewing records of 122 dementia patients who died in a Brazilian geriatric hospital (mean age 83.6), researchers found that infectious diseases—primarily pneumonia (48%) and sepsis (19%)—were the most common causes of death. Antibiotics were prescribed in 76% of cases, yet palliative care measures were documented in only 41% of cases.

**Why it matters:** End-of-life care for hospitalized dementia patients often emphasizes infection treatment over comfort-focused palliative care, suggesting a need for integrated protocols.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If a loved one with dementia is hospitalized with a serious infection, ask the care team about palliative care options alongside infection treatment to ensure comfort is prioritized.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Hospitals caring for dementia patients should develop integrated end-of-life protocols that balance infection management with comfort-focused palliative care.

**Caveats:** Single-center retrospective study; Brazilian healthcare context; small sample.

### 15. Person-Centered Palliative Care in Nursing Homes Requires Staff Training and Organizational Support

**Journal:** *BMC Geriatrics* | **Published:** July 7, 2026
**PMID:** 42414901 | **DOI:** 10.1186/s12877-026-07795-y

**The study:** An integrative review of nine studies examined how to implement person-centered palliative care for older nursing home residents. Effective implementation required advanced communication skills, ethical reasoning, and collaborative practice—not just technical proficiency. Major barriers included insufficient staff training, resource constraints, and institutional rigidity. Leadership, professional development, and interdisciplinary teamwork were key facilitators.

**Why it matters:** Person-centered palliative care cannot be achieved through training alone—it requires organizational commitment, resources, and a culture of collaboration.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** When evaluating nursing homes, ask about staff training in palliative care and whether facilities support person-centered approaches to end-of-life care.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Nursing home administrators should invest in ongoing palliative care training, foster interdisciplinary collaboration, and create organizational structures that support person-centered care.

**Caveats:** Integrative review (synthesizes diverse study types); limited number of included studies.

### 16. Scoping Review Finds No High-Quality Evidence for Best Palliative Care Model in Aged Care

**Journal:** *BMC Geriatrics* | **Published:** July 7, 2026
**PMID:** 42414895 | **DOI:** 10.1186/s12877-026-07915-8

**The study:** Researchers reviewed 30 studies describing 23 models of palliative care delivery in residential aged care facilities. Models were delivered internally by facilities or externally by community or hospital services, using various approaches from literature reviews to expert working groups. However, none demonstrated high-quality evidence of improved clinical outcomes.

**Why it matters:** Despite many palliative care models being implemented in aged care, there's a lack of rigorous evidence about which approaches work best—more research is needed.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If palliative care is important to you, ask aged care facilities about the specific services they offer and how they evaluate their effectiveness.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Researchers and policymakers should prioritize rigorous studies comparing different palliative care models in aged care to determine what actually improves outcomes.

**Caveats:** Scoping review (maps existing literature; does not assess quality); no model demonstrated strong evidence of effectiveness.

### 17. Nurses Identify Strategies to Improve Pain Management for Dementia Patients

**Journal:** *Geriatric Nursing* | **Published:** July 6, 2026
**PMID:** 42407320 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.gerinurse.2026.104153

**The study:** Using the Nominal Group Technique, 28 nurses from Norwegian nursing homes generated and ranked 112 strategies for improving pain management in dementia patients. The top priorities were systematic pain assessment when residents show clear pain expressions and ensuring appropriate staff competence. Nurses rated the relevance of strategies slightly higher than their feasibility.

**Why it matters:** Frontline nurses have practical insights into improving dementia pain care, but implementation barriers—including training and staffing—remain significant challenges.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If your loved one with dementia is in a nursing home, ask staff how they assess and manage pain, especially since dementia patients may not clearly communicate discomfort.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Nursing homes should prioritize staff training in dementia-specific pain assessment and create systems for routine pain screening.

**Caveats:** Qualitative study with nurses only; Norwegian context; patient outcomes not measured.

### 18. Geriatric Hotline Helps Direct Older Patients to Appropriate Care

**Journal:** *BMC Geriatrics* | **Published:** May 11, 2026
**PMID:** 42115946 | **DOI:** 10.1186/s12877-026-07589-2

**The study:** A French university hospital established a geriatric hotline providing community physicians with rapid access to geriatricians. Over 18 months, the hotline received 3,627 calls; among 393 fully documented cases (mean age 84), common reasons included declining health, falls, and neurodegenerative diseases. Following calls, patients were directed to acute geriatric units (35%), day hospitals (16%), or given advice (27%)—with only 7% referred to emergency departments.

**Why it matters:** A geriatric hotline can help community physicians access specialist advice quickly, potentially avoiding unnecessary emergency visits for frail older adults.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Ask your primary care doctor whether a geriatric specialist consultation line is available in your area—this may help ensure you receive appropriate care without an emergency room visit.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Health systems should consider establishing geriatric hotlines to support community physicians and optimize care pathways for complex older patients.

**Caveats:** Single-center observational study; only fully documented cases analyzed; no control group or patient outcome data.

### 19. Physicians Uncertain About Antibiotics at End of Life—Cultural Differences Noted

**Journal:** *BMC Geriatrics* | **Published:** May 12, 2026
**PMID:** 42121047 | **DOI:** 10.1186/s12877-026-07477-9

**The study:** A survey of 195 Swiss physicians presented clinical vignettes of a frail older patient with aspiration pneumonia. Only 22% would prescribe antibiotics, primarily to relieve breathing difficulty. Physicians were more likely to prescribe for patients with better functional status. Those in French-speaking regions were more likely to prescribe antibiotics (34%) than German- or Italian-speaking regions (14% and 11%). Notably, 78% would change their decision if the family disagreed.

**Why it matters:** End-of-life antibiotic decisions vary significantly by region and are often influenced by family preferences, highlighting the need for clearer guidance and shared decision-making tools.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have strong feelings about antibiotics at the end of life, discuss your preferences with your family and document them in an advance directive.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Clinicians should engage families in transparent discussions about antibiotic use at end of life, acknowledging that decisions are value-laden and often shaped by cultural context.

**Caveats:** Survey-based (hypothetical scenarios); Swiss context; response rate not reported.

### 20. People With Parkinson's Disease Fear Disability, Burden—But Find Coping Through Connection

**Journal:** *Journal of the American Geriatrics Society* | **Published:** June 30, 2026
**PMID:** 42378392 | **DOI:** 10.1111/jgs.70541

**The study:** Thirty people living with Parkinson's disease in Brazil participated in semi-structured interviews about what constitutes a "good death." Two major themes emerged: fears (disability, pain, shame, being a burden, abandonment) and coping (feeling well cared for, finding joy, drawing on spirituality/religion). Feeling valued, receiving honest communication, and being treated with kindness were central to the relational experience of good care.

**Why it matters:** Improving end-of-life care for Parkinson's patients requires addressing specific fears while actively supporting coping strategies—including fostering joy and spiritual support.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have Parkinson's disease and are thinking about the future, consider discussing your fears and hopes with your care team—palliative care can help address emotional and spiritual concerns, not just physical symptoms.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Palliative care programs for Parkinson's patients should incorporate spiritual support and opportunities for meaningful connection alongside symptom management.

**Caveats:** Qualitative study; Brazilian context; findings shaped by participants' cultural and religious backgrounds.

### 21. Polypharmacy Persists in Palliative Care Patients Despite Limited Benefits

**Journal:** *Maturitas* | **Published:** June 2026
**PMID:** 41950773 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.maturitas.2026.108928

**The study:** Among 294 older adults screened for palliative care needs in Italian hospitals and nursing homes (mean age 85), 94% were taking five or more medications, and 68% were receiving at least one potentially inappropriate medication (PIM). Drug burden and PIMs did not decline even in advanced stages of illness, suggesting limited deprescribing.

**Why it matters:** Older adults at the end of life often continue taking medications that offer little benefit and may cause harm, highlighting a need for more systematic medication reviews.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or a loved one is receiving palliative care, ask your doctor whether all current medications are still necessary—reducing unnecessary drugs may improve comfort.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Clinicians caring for palliative patients should prioritize deprescribing—systematically reviewing and reducing medications that no longer align with care goals.

**Caveats:** Point-prevalence study (single time point); regional Italian data; observational design.

### 22. Severe Mental Illness Linked to Late Palliative Care Referrals and Care Fragmentation

**Journal:** *Psychogeriatrics* | **Published:** May 2026
**PMID:** 41944270 | **DOI:** 10.1111/psyg.70164

**The study:** A case series of three older adults with severe persistent mental illness (SPMI) in Australia examined end-of-life care challenges. Palliative care referrals occurred late—often days before death—due to delayed recognition of treatment futility, fragmented service delivery, and ethical-legal complexities. Multimorbidity, frailty, and dementia overlapped with SPMI, complicating clinical trajectories.

**Why it matters:** People with severe mental illness face significant disparities in palliative care access, underscoring the need for integrated care models and clearer referral pathways.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or a loved one has a serious mental illness alongside physical health conditions, ask your care team about palliative care options early—don't wait for a crisis.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Mental health and palliative care services should develop integrated pathways to ensure timely recognition of declining health and appropriate referrals for patients with severe mental illness.

**Caveats:** Case series (three cases only); Australian context; cannot establish generalizable patterns.

### 23. What Constitutes a Good Death for Community-Dwelling Dementia Patients?

**Journal:** *International Journal of Older People Nursing* | **Published:** May 2026
**PMID:** 41968118 | **DOI:** 10.1111/opn.70078

**The study:** An integrative review of 21 studies explored what makes a "good death" for community-dwelling people with dementia and their caregivers. Key elements included dying peacefully, preserving dignity and autonomy, and receiving person-centered care. Structural supports (hospice access, skilled workforce) and process factors (advance care planning, symptom management, caregiver education) were identified as essential.

**Why it matters:** Achieving a good death for people with dementia living at home requires coordinated community services, early planning, and robust caregiver support.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have dementia or are caring for someone who does, consider discussing end-of-life preferences and connecting with community palliative care services early.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Community health services should develop integrated care models that support person-centered end-of-life care and provide structured education and psychological support for family caregivers.

**Caveats:** Review of existing literature; heterogeneous study designs; limited to English-language publications.

### 24. Oral Health Overlooked in National Aging Policies

**Journal:** *Gerodontology* | **Published:** April 27, 2026
**PMID:** 42037516 | **DOI:** 10.1111/ger.70085

**The study:** A scoping review examined how 52 English-speaking countries integrate oral health into national health policies for older adults. Of 28 documents that mentioned oral health, most referenced it only superficially—as context rather than specific objectives or activities. Oral health stakeholder representation was limited across palliative care, mental health, nutrition, and healthy aging policies.

**Why it matters:** Despite the importance of oral health to quality of life, it remains marginalized in national aging and palliative care policies, reflecting a need for deeper integration.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Don't overlook dental care as part of your overall health—ask your healthcare providers about oral health resources, especially if you have chronic conditions.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Policymakers should integrate oral health more meaningfully into aging and palliative care frameworks, ensuring dental professionals are represented in interdisciplinary planning.

**Caveats:** Scoping review; limited to English-language government documents; policy presence does not ensure implementation.

### 25. Older Adults Rarely Included in UK Research—Especially Commercial Studies

**Journal:** *Age and Ageing* | **Published:** May 3, 2026
**PMID:** 42202279 | **DOI:** 10.1093/ageing/afag143

**The study:** Analyzing data from over 455,000 participants in UK clinical research (2022–2024), researchers found that adults over 85 made up only 3.2% of research participants—and this proportion decreased over time. Older adults were particularly underrepresented in commercial studies and certain medical specialties, including palliative care.

**Why it matters:** The exclusion of the oldest adults from research means treatment decisions are based on data from younger populations, potentially limiting the relevance of findings to real-world care.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If given the opportunity to participate in research, consider whether it might benefit others like you—older adult participation helps ensure treatments work for your age group.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Research funders and regulators should require justification for age-based exclusions and incentivize studies that specifically include the oldest adults.

**Caveats:** Observational data; UK-specific; does not assess outcomes of included older participants.
