# Senior Living Research Digest: Sarcopenia

## Selected Studies

### 1. Common Blood Pressure Medication May Help Protect Muscles in Hospitalized Older Adults

**Journal:** *Aging Clinical and Experimental Research* | **Published:** July 8, 2026
**PMID:** 42414787 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40520-026-03452-x

**The study:** Researchers in Turkey examined 206 hospitalized patients aged 65 and older (60% female, median age 78) to understand whether taking multiple medications was linked to sarcopenia (age-related muscle loss). While polypharmacy itself wasn't associated with sarcopenia, patients taking angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) — a common blood pressure medication — were significantly less likely to have sarcopenia (15% vs. 40% in non-users).

**Why it matters:** This suggests certain blood pressure medications may have unexpected muscle-protective benefits, offering hope that some commonly prescribed drugs could serve double duty in older adults.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're already taking an ARB for blood pressure, this research suggests it may also be helping preserve your muscle health — one more reason to stay consistent with your medication routine. Don't change medications based on this study alone; discuss with your doctor.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For clinicians managing hypertension in older patients at risk for sarcopenia, ARBs may warrant consideration when choosing among blood pressure medication classes, though more research is needed to confirm causality.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design (cannot establish causation), single-center study, moderate sample size (N=206), hospitalized population may not generalize to community-dwelling adults.

### 2. Sarcopenia Linked to Higher Risk of Lung Disease, Including COPD and Asthma

**Journal:** *Journal of Nutrition, Health and Aging* | **Published:** July 2, 2026
**PMID:** 42391675 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jnha.2026.100919

**The study:** Using UK Biobank data from over 317,000 adults followed for 14 years, researchers found that even "probable sarcopenia" (low muscle strength alone) was associated with 30% higher risk of developing respiratory diseases. Those with confirmed sarcopenia faced 37% higher COPD risk, 35% higher asthma risk, and 74% higher risk of interstitial lung disease. Inflammatory markers like C-reactive protein partially explained these connections.

**Why it matters:** This large-scale study establishes that muscle health isn't just about mobility — it's intimately connected to lung health, suggesting that maintaining muscle strength could help protect breathing function as we age.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Keeping your muscles strong may help protect your lungs. If you've noticed declining grip strength or find it harder to rise from a chair, talk to your doctor — these could be early warning signs worth addressing with exercise and nutrition.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living communities might consider integrating resistance training programs as a respiratory health strategy, not just for fall prevention. Respiratory screenings may be warranted for residents showing signs of muscle weakness.

**Caveats:** Observational design (cannot establish causation), UK population may not generalize globally, sarcopenia assessed at baseline only.

### 3. Muscle and Bone Loss Share Genetic Roots and Lifestyle Risk Factors

**Journal:** *Aging Cell* | **Published:** July 2026
**PMID:** 42380078 | **DOI:** 10.1111/acel.70617

**The study:** Analyzing UK Biobank data using genomic, protein, and metabolic measures, researchers found that sarcopenia and osteoporosis act as mutual risk factors — each condition increases the likelihood of developing the other. They identified shared genetic variants, overlapping blood biomarkers, and common lifestyle factors (smoking, poor sleep, physical inactivity) contributing to both conditions.

**Why it matters:** This comprehensive analysis confirms that muscle and bone health are deeply interconnected biologically, suggesting that interventions targeting one could benefit both.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Your muscles and bones age together as a team. The same habits that protect one — staying active, getting enough sleep, not smoking — appear to protect both. If you've been diagnosed with osteoporosis, paying attention to muscle strength is equally important.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Fall prevention programs in senior living should address both muscle and bone health simultaneously. Screening for one condition should prompt evaluation of the other, as they share underlying mechanisms.

**Caveats:** Observational design, predominantly European ancestry population, some analyses cross-sectional.

### 4. Long-Term Statin Use Shows No Harmful Effect on Muscle in Older Adults

**Journal:** *GeroScience* | **Published:** June 29, 2026
**PMID:** 42373884 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11357-026-02387-5

**The study:** Following 1,083 community-dwelling older adults in Berlin (52% women, average age 68) over 7.4 years, researchers found that long-term statin use was not associated with any measures of muscle mass, strength, or physical performance. However, blood pressure medications that block the renin-angiotensin system showed a modest association with lower lean mass, particularly in women.

**Why it matters:** This provides reassurance for the millions of older adults taking statins for heart health — the medications don't appear to accelerate muscle loss despite occasional reports of muscle-related side effects.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you've worried that your cholesterol medication might be contributing to muscle weakness, this long-term study offers reassurance. Don't stop taking statins without consulting your doctor — the cardiovascular benefits are well-established.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Healthcare providers can share this evidence when older patients express concerns about statins and muscle health. The modest finding regarding blood pressure medications in women warrants monitoring but shouldn't change prescribing without further research.

**Caveats:** Observational design (cannot establish causation), single cohort in Germany, exploratory analysis.

### 5. Poor Living Conditions — Including Water Quality and Housing — Increase Sarcopenia Risk

**Journal:** *Journal of Nutrition, Health and Aging* | **Published:** June 25, 2026
**PMID:** 42349091 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jnha.2026.100911

**The study:** Researchers analyzed data from nearly 13,000 middle-aged and older Chinese adults and found that poor living environmental quality — including air pollution, cooking with solid fuels, unsafe drinking water, and inadequate housing — was associated with 65% higher odds of having sarcopenia and 29% higher risk of developing it over four years. Water source quality and building type were the most significant factors.

**Why it matters:** This research highlights that muscle health isn't just about diet and exercise — the environment where older adults live plays a substantial role, pointing to potential public health interventions.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Your home environment matters for muscle health. If you're concerned about water quality, consider filtration options. Ensuring adequate heating and ventilation may also support overall health as you age.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living facility managers should prioritize air quality, water safety, and temperature control as health interventions, not just comfort measures. These environmental factors may be as important as exercise programs for preventing muscle loss.

**Caveats:** Chinese population (may not generalize globally), some environmental measures self-reported, observational design.

### 6. Longer Hospital Stays Linked to Poorer Muscle Function — Especially in Those Over 80

**Journal:** *Aging Clinical and Experimental Research* | **Published:** June 25, 2026
**PMID:** 42348081 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40520-026-03442-z

**The study:** Analyzing data from 1,071 older hospitalized patients in Copenhagen (aged 65+), researchers found that longer hospital stays were associated with lower physical function, particularly in patients 80 and older. The 30-second chair stand test and handgrip strength were strongly connected to length of stay, with men under 80 showing three times higher odds of sarcopenia with prolonged hospitalization.

**Why it matters:** This reinforces that hospitalization itself can accelerate muscle decline, especially in the oldest patients, highlighting the importance of early mobilization programs.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're hospitalized, ask about getting up and moving as soon as it's safe — even short walks or sitting up can help preserve your strength. The longer you stay in bed, the harder recovery becomes.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Hospital-based rehabilitation should prioritize the oldest patients (80+) for early mobilization, as they appear most vulnerable to hospitalization-related muscle decline. Chair stand tests can help identify those needing extra support.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design (cannot determine if poor function causes longer stays or vice versa), single center, Danish population.

### 7. Replacing Just 10 Minutes of Sitting with Brisk Activity May Lower Sarcopenia Risk

**Journal:** *Journal of Geriatric Physical Therapy* | **Published:** June 19, 2026
**PMID:** 42314003 | **DOI:** 10.1519/JPT.0000000000000508

**The study:** Using accelerometer data from 527 Japanese adults aged 65-84 living in a rural area, researchers found that replacing just 10 minutes of sedentary time daily with moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (like brisk walking) was significantly associated with better walking speed and lower sarcopenia prevalence (6.3% in this population). Simply replacing sitting with light activity wasn't enough.

**Why it matters:** This provides a specific, achievable target — 10 minutes of more vigorous activity daily — that could make a meaningful difference in preserving muscle function.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Trading just 10 minutes of sitting for brisk walking or other moderate activity may help keep your muscles strong. It doesn't have to be all at once — three brief walks could do it.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Activity programming in senior living should emphasize intensity, not just movement. Encouraging residents to pick up the pace during walks or participate in structured exercise may be more effective than simply reducing sitting time.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design, Japanese rural population may not generalize, relatively low sarcopenia prevalence suggests healthier sample.

### 8. Sarcopenia Doubles Risk of Cognitive Impairment in Latin American Older Adults

**Journal:** *Journal of Nutrition, Health and Aging* | **Published:** May 19, 2026
**PMID:** 42155950 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jnha.2026.100877

**The study:** A meta-analysis of 8 studies including over 13,000 community-dwelling adults from Latin America found that sarcopenia was associated with 2.3 times higher odds of cognitive impairment. When using stricter sarcopenia definitions (EWGSOP2 criteria), the association was nearly threefold.

**Why it matters:** This confirms that muscle and brain health are connected in Latin American populations, where such research has been limited, suggesting that preventing muscle loss may help protect cognitive function.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Keeping your muscles strong may help keep your mind sharp too. The same activities that build muscle — resistance training, adequate protein intake — could have brain benefits.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Cognitive screening in senior living settings should consider muscle health as a risk factor. Residents with sarcopenia may benefit from closer monitoring for cognitive changes and targeted interventions addressing both conditions.

**Caveats:** Primarily cross-sectional studies (cannot establish causation), heterogeneous sarcopenia definitions across studies, limited to Latin American populations.

### 9. Sarcopenia and Obesity Together Create "Perfect Storm" for Dementia Risk

**Journal:** *Journal of Nutrition, Health and Aging* | **Published:** May 19, 2026
**PMID:** 42155951 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jnha.2026.100879

**The study:** Following over 420,000 UK Biobank participants without dementia for nearly 14 years, researchers found that those with both sarcopenia and obesity (sarcopenic obesity) had 69% higher risk of developing dementia compared to those with neither condition — higher than having either condition alone. Even "possible sarcopenia" (low grip strength) increased dementia risk by 44%.

**Why it matters:** This massive study reveals that the combination of muscle loss and excess weight is particularly dangerous for brain health, identifying a high-risk group that may benefit from targeted interventions.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're carrying extra weight and noticing muscle weakness, addressing both issues together may be especially important for protecting your brain. Ask your doctor about combined exercise and nutrition strategies.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living wellness programs should identify residents with both low muscle strength and obesity as priority candidates for intensive intervention. Simple grip strength testing can help flag those at highest dementia risk.

**Caveats:** Observational design (cannot establish causation), predominantly white British population, single baseline assessment.

### 10. Vitamin D Deficiency Worsens Sarcopenia Risk — Especially for Those with Diabetes

**Journal:** *Journal of Nutrition, Health and Aging* | **Published:** July 2026
**PMID:** 42229217 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jnha.2026.100886

**The study:** Analyzing data from over 7,500 older adults in the US and England, researchers found that low vitamin D (≤50 nmol/L) was associated with higher sarcopenia risk, with the strongest effects seen in those with diabetes. In the English cohort, adults with both low vitamin D and diabetes had significantly elevated sarcopenia odds. Animal experiments confirmed that vitamin D supplementation improved muscle strength and reduced fat infiltration in aged diabetic rats.

**Why it matters:** This suggests vitamin D optimization may be particularly important for older adults with diabetes, who face compounded risks for muscle loss.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have diabetes, ask your doctor to check your vitamin D levels. Maintaining adequate vitamin D through supplements or safe sun exposure may help protect your muscles, though the right amount varies by individual.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Diabetes management in senior living should include vitamin D monitoring as part of sarcopenia prevention. Residents with diabetes and low vitamin D represent a particularly vulnerable group warranting nutritional intervention.

**Caveats:** Observational human data (cannot establish causation), different results between US and English cohorts, vitamin D thresholds debated in scientific community.

### 11. Exercise Program Improves Independence and Reduces Inflammation in Older Adults with Sarcopenia

**Journal:** *Rejuvenation Research* | **Published:** May 7, 2026
**PMID:** 42098919 | **DOI:** 10.1177/15491684261449707

**The study:** In a 12-week randomized controlled trial, 94 Chinese adults aged 60-75 with sarcopenia either continued usual care or performed 37-minute exercise sessions (combining resistance and aerobic training) three times weekly after meals. The exercise group showed significantly better daily function scores, muscle mass, grip strength, walking distance, and lower inflammatory markers compared to controls.

**Why it matters:** This rigorous trial provides strong evidence that a structured, feasible exercise program can meaningfully improve multiple aspects of health in older adults already diagnosed with sarcopenia.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you've been told you have sarcopenia, a consistent exercise routine combining strength training and walking can make a real difference — this study showed improvements in just 12 weeks. Ask about programs designed for your current fitness level.
- **About older adults and seniors:** This trial provides evidence that senior living activity programs should include structured, multicomponent exercise (not just walking) for residents with sarcopenia. Three sessions weekly of about 40 minutes produced measurable benefits.

**Caveats:** Single-center trial in China, relatively short duration (12 weeks), participants aged 60-75 may not represent oldest adults.

### 12. Combined Exercise and Protein Supplementation Most Effective for Sarcopenia and Falls

**Journal:** *Journal of Nutrition, Health and Aging* | **Published:** June 2026
**PMID:** 42208412 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jnha.2026.100862

**The study:** This umbrella review synthesized 41 systematic reviews covering approximately 4,475 primary studies on sarcopenia and fall prevention. Key findings: combined exercise with protein supplementation (≥1.2 g/kg body weight daily) produced the best improvements in muscle mass and strength; balance and multicomponent exercise reduced fall rates by 23-34%; and vitamin D supplementation (≥700 IU/day) reduced falls by 13%.

**Why it matters:** This comprehensive evidence synthesis provides clear, actionable guidance on the most effective combination strategies for preventing both sarcopenia and falls.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** The research is clear: exercise plus adequate protein works better than either alone for maintaining muscle. Aim for protein with each meal, and include balance exercises alongside strength training. Ask about vitamin D if you're at fall risk.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living programs should integrate resistance training, balance exercises, protein-rich meals (targeting 1.2+ g/kg/day), and vitamin D assessment for optimal sarcopenia and fall prevention. Single-intervention approaches are less effective than combined strategies.

**Caveats:** Umbrella review depends on quality of underlying studies, limited direct evidence that reversing sarcopenia reduces falls, some included reviews had methodological limitations.

### 13. New Metabolic Biomarker Predicts Sarcopenia Development in Middle-Aged and Older Adults

**Journal:** *Journal of Nutrition, Health and Aging* | **Published:** May 30, 2026
**PMID:** 42217315 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jnha.2026.100889

**The study:** Following 5,580 Chinese adults aged 50+ over four years, researchers found that a novel metabolic index combining cholesterol, HDL, and blood sugar (CHG index) predicted new sarcopenia cases. Those in the highest CHG quartile had 29% higher sarcopenia risk. The index outperformed existing metabolic markers in predicting who would develop muscle loss.

**Why it matters:** This simple blood test combination could help identify middle-aged and older adults at risk for sarcopenia before symptoms appear, enabling earlier intervention.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Your routine blood work may reveal more about your muscle health than you realized. Ask your doctor how your cholesterol and blood sugar levels relate to your overall health, including muscle maintenance.
- **About older adults and seniors:** This research suggests metabolic health screening could help identify residents at sarcopenia risk before physical decline becomes apparent. Elevated cholesterol-glucose markers may warrant proactive exercise and nutrition interventions.

**Caveats:** Chinese population (may not generalize), observational design, new biomarker requires validation in other populations.

### 14. Declining Respiratory and Muscle Function Together Signal Higher Chronic Disease Risk

**Journal:** *Journal of Nutrition, Health and Aging* | **Published:** June 11, 2026
**PMID:** 42275840 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jnha.2026.100903

**The study:** Tracking 4,784 middle-aged and older Chinese adults, researchers identified three trajectory patterns of lung function and muscle mass over time. Those with persistently low values in both measures had dramatically elevated risks: 4.3 times higher for asthma, 2.3 times for chronic lung disease, and nearly double for arthritis and hypertension.

**Why it matters:** This study identifies a high-risk phenotype — combined respiratory and muscle decline — that could help target preventive interventions to those most likely to develop multiple chronic conditions.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you notice both breathlessness and muscle weakness developing together, don't dismiss them as "just getting older." This combination may signal elevated risk for several chronic conditions and is worth discussing with your doctor.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Comprehensive assessments in senior living should track both respiratory function (simple peak flow tests) and muscle strength together. Residents declining in both domains may need intensive, multidisciplinary intervention.

**Caveats:** Observational design (cannot establish causation), Chinese population, "probable respiratory sarcopenia" is a research construct not yet clinically validated.

### 15. Food Insecurity Doubles Risk of Early Death in Older Adults

**Journal:** *Aging Clinical and Experimental Research* | **Published:** May 27, 2026
**PMID:** 42201632 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40520-026-03422-3

**The study:** Following 8,686 English adults aged 50+ from 2004-2005, researchers found that those reporting food insecurity had 41% higher risk of death from any cause and 126% higher risk of dying prematurely (before age 75), even after accounting for other health and socioeconomic factors.

**Why it matters:** This highlights food insecurity as a critical but often overlooked health risk in older adults, with implications for sarcopenia prevention and overall longevity.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're ever worried about having enough food or the right foods, please speak up — to family, your doctor, or community services. Food insecurity isn't just uncomfortable; it's a serious health risk that has solutions.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living and community programs should routinely screen for food insecurity, even among those who appear adequately housed. Addressing food access could be as important as medical interventions for extending healthy life.

**Caveats:** Single-item food insecurity measure, English population, observational design, older baseline data (2004-2005).

### 16. Specific Gut Bacteria Linked to Sarcopenia in Iranian Older Adults

**Journal:** *Aging Clinical and Experimental Research* | **Published:** April 30, 2026
**PMID:** 42060019 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40520-026-03403-6

**The study:** In 293 Iranian adults aged 60+, researchers found that higher levels of Akkermansia and Lactobacillus bacteria were associated with increased sarcopenia risk (7-9% higher odds per unit increase), while Roseburia was protective (11.5% lower odds per unit). Nearly 40% of participants had sarcopenia.

**Why it matters:** This adds to growing evidence that gut health influences muscle health, potentially opening new avenues for sarcopenia prevention through diet or probiotics, though the specific bacteria involved may vary by population.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Your gut health may affect your muscle health. A diet rich in fiber and fermented foods supports beneficial gut bacteria, though we don't yet know which specific probiotics might help prevent muscle loss.
- **About older adults and seniors:** While it's too early to recommend specific probiotics for sarcopenia prevention, this research underscores the importance of gut health in comprehensive senior care. Dietary interventions supporting gut health may have muscle benefits.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design (cannot establish causation), small sample size (N=293), single Iranian population, some bacterial associations lost significance after statistical corrections.

### 17. Thigh Muscle Loss Best Predicts Strength and Mobility Decline Over Five Years

**Journal:** *Aging Clinical and Experimental Research* | **Published:** June 13, 2026
**PMID:** 42287341 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40520-026-03425-0

**The study:** Following 120 older adults with CT scans over five years, researchers found that shrinkage of thigh muscles (anterior compartment) was most strongly associated with declining grip strength and mobility in women. Each square centimeter of thigh muscle loss doubled the odds of strength decline and mobility impairment.

**Why it matters:** This identifies the thigh muscles as a key target for preserving function in aging women, with implications for exercise prescription and monitoring.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** The muscles in your thighs are especially important for maintaining strength and mobility as you age. Exercises like squats, leg presses, or even repeated sit-to-stands can help preserve these critical muscles.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Exercise programming for older women should emphasize quadriceps strengthening. Thigh muscle assessment (even simple thigh circumference measurements) may help identify those at risk for functional decline.

**Caveats:** Small sample size (N=120), Chinese population, observational design, CT-based measurements not widely available clinically.

### 18. Sarcopenia Before Hip Surgery Predicts Slower Recovery in Brazilian Older Adults

**Journal:** *Journal of Geriatric Physical Therapy* | **Published:** May 8, 2026
**PMID:** 42104205 | **DOI:** 10.1519/JPT.0000000000000503

**The study:** Following 71 Brazilian adults (average age 76) who underwent hip replacement for fractures, researchers found that sarcopenia before surgery was associated with delayed walking recovery in the first three days. Age over 80, cognitive impairment, and lower functional capacity also predicted slower recovery, while BMI did not.

**Why it matters:** This identifies which patients may need extra support after hip surgery, enabling targeted rehabilitation resources for those most likely to struggle.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're facing hip surgery, your muscle strength beforehand matters for recovery. Ask about "prehabilitation" — exercises to build strength before surgery that may help you recover faster afterward.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Pre-surgical assessments should include sarcopenia screening to identify patients needing intensive early mobilization. Patients over 80 with cognitive impairment and prior functional limitations require extra rehabilitation support.

**Caveats:** Small sample size (N=71), single Brazilian hospital, short follow-up (3 days post-surgery), observational design.

### 19. Simple Sit-to-Stand Test Predicts Mobility Problems Six Years Later

**Journal:** *JMIR Aging* | **Published:** May 11, 2026
**PMID:** 42114049 | **DOI:** 10.2196/77187

**The study:** Following 1,344 Chinese adults aged 45+ for nearly seven years, researchers developed a prediction model for early mobility limitations using simple measures. The most important predictors were: age, lower adherence to Mediterranean-style diet, lower muscle power from a sit-to-stand test, and higher BMI. About 15% developed mobility limitations during follow-up.

**Why it matters:** This provides a practical, home-friendly way to identify people at risk for mobility decline before it becomes severe, using a simple chair test anyone can do.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Try timing how quickly you can stand up from a chair five times — this simple test can reveal a lot about your future mobility. If it's getting harder, that's a signal to focus on leg strength and diet quality now.
- **About older adults and seniors:** The sit-to-stand test should be a standard assessment in senior living wellness programs. Combined with dietary evaluation, it can identify residents who would benefit from targeted intervention before mobility problems develop.

**Caveats:** Chinese population (may not generalize), machine learning models require validation in other settings, Mediterranean diet score adapted for Chinese diet.

### 20. Brain Aging May Drive Muscle Loss Through Systemic Signals

**Journal:** *GeroScience* | **Published:** May 14, 2026
**PMID:** 42133166 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11357-026-02205-y

**The study:** In a mouse study, researchers induced brain aging through targeted radiation while leaving the rest of the body unaffected. Two months later, leg muscles showed gene expression patterns remarkably similar to naturally aged mice — including reduced mitochondrial function and activated muscle-wasting pathways — despite the muscles never being directly damaged.

**Why it matters:** This suggests that brain health directly influences muscle health through systemic signals, potentially explaining why cognitive and physical decline often occur together and opening new therapeutic targets.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Keeping your brain healthy may help keep your muscles healthy too. Activities that challenge your mind — learning new skills, social engagement, cognitive exercises — might have benefits beyond mental sharpness.
- **About older adults and seniors:** This research suggests that cognitive health programs in senior living may have physical benefits, and vice versa. Integrated approaches addressing both brain and body health may be more effective than targeting each separately.

**Caveats:** Mouse study (findings may not translate to humans), artificial model of brain aging (radiation), preliminary mechanistic research.
