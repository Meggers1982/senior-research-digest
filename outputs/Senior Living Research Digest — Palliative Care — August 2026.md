### 1. More Than Half of Hospitalized Older Adults and Nearly Half of Nursing Home Residents Need Palliative Care — Most Don't Have Cancer

**Journal:** *Aging Clinical and Experimental Research* | **Published:** June 28, 2026
**PMID:** 42366281 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40520-026-03445-w

**The study:** Italian researchers conducted a nationwide point-prevalence study across 235 hospitals and long-term care facilities, screening 5,389 patients and residents (median ages 81 and 86 years, respectively) for palliative care needs using the validated NECPAL 4.0 tool. They found that 58.3% of hospitalized patients and 48.1% of nursing home residents had identifiable palliative care needs. While prevalence was highest among those with cancer (77% in hospitals), more than half of non-cancer patients (50.8%) and nearly half of non-cancer nursing home residents (46.8%) also had palliative care needs. Functional decline, nutritional problems, pain, delirium, and dysphagia were common across both settings.

**Why it matters:** This study challenges the long-standing association of palliative care primarily with cancer and hospice, demonstrating that comfort-focused care is needed by the majority of older adults in hospitals and care facilities — regardless of diagnosis.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or a loved one is hospitalized or living in a care facility, know that palliative care isn't just for cancer or the final days of life. You can ask your care team whether a palliative care consultation might help manage symptoms like pain, fatigue, or difficulty eating — even alongside ongoing medical treatment.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living professionals should consider routine screening for palliative care needs among all residents, not just those with terminal diagnoses. Integrating palliative principles into everyday care could address widespread unmet needs for symptom relief and quality of life.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design (cannot track outcomes over time); findings reflect Italian healthcare context and may not directly generalize to other countries.

### 2. Finding the Right Moment: How Aged Care Staff Navigate End-of-Life Conversations With Families

**Journal:** *Australasian Journal on Ageing* | **Published:** June 2026
**PMID:** 42163024 | **DOI:** 10.1111/ajag.70184

**The study:** Australian researchers interviewed 64 aged care staff — including registered nurses, enrolled nurses, and care managers — across 14 facilities to understand how they communicate with families about end-of-life care planning. Five themes emerged: staff view timing as a staged, adaptive process; building expectations gradually prepares families for decisions; clear communication helps bridge mismatches between family perceptions and clinical reality; family dynamics and cultural context shape conversations; and staff confidence, role clarity, and physician endorsement all influence communication success.

**Why it matters:** When there's no clear prognosis or families are reluctant to discuss dying, staff rely on describing symptoms and functional changes to open dialogue. These findings highlight the need for training and resources to help staff initiate conversations earlier and more confidently.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** End-of-life planning conversations don't have to happen all at once. If you're in a care facility, you can ask staff to keep you and your family updated on any changes in your health — even small ones — so decisions feel less sudden when the time comes.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families, understanding that care staff often use gradual, symptom-focused communication can help you recognize when they may be signaling a shift in your loved one's condition. Asking directly about what changes mean can open the door to deeper planning discussions.

**Caveats:** Qualitative study; findings reflect Australian aged care context; no direct measurement of patient or family outcomes.

### 3. Many Older Adults Receive Elective Hospital Procedures in Their Final Three Months of Life

**Journal:** *European Geriatric Medicine* | **Published:** August 3, 2026
**PMID:** 42545407 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s41999-026-01560-5

**The study:** Dutch researchers analyzed nationwide administrative data for 104,544 adults aged 65 and older who died between April and December 2019. In the final three months of life, 42.4% were admitted to a hospital and 15.9% had elective (planned) admissions. Common elective treatments included chemotherapy, radiotherapy, dialysis, and even cataract surgery. Some procedures — including gastrointestinal surgeries, heart valve operations, and hip surgeries — occurred in the final month of life. A substantial proportion of these elective interventions happened when time to benefit was limited.

**Why it matters:** Elective care is plannable, offering opportunities for patients, families, and clinicians to deliberate on whether interventions align with a person's goals — especially when time to experience meaningful benefit may be short.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're facing a decision about an elective procedure, it's reasonable to ask your doctor: "Given my overall health, how likely am I to benefit from this, and over what time frame?" These conversations can help ensure treatments match your priorities.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and senior care professionals, these findings underscore the importance of proactive conversations about goals of care well before a health crisis. Elective procedures offer a window for reflection that emergency situations do not.

**Caveats:** Observational, retrospective design; cannot determine whether specific procedures were inappropriate or unwanted; data reflect Dutch healthcare system.

### 4. Families, Nurses, and Doctors See Dying Differently: A Study of Quality-of-Dying Assessments in Geriatric Wards

**Journal:** *European Geriatric Medicine* | **Published:** July 30, 2026
**PMID:** 42530751 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s41999-026-01564-1

**The study:** In a prospective Belgian study of 103 patients recognized as approaching death in acute geriatric wards, researchers asked relatives, nurses, and doctors to independently rate comfort and symptoms daily using the EOLD-CAD scale. Doctors rated overall comfort higher than relatives, especially regarding dying symptoms. Relatives' comfort ratings improved as death approached, while nurses' and doctors' scores did not rise as much. Correlations between raters were weak, particularly between healthcare staff and families.

**Why it matters:** These divergent perceptions highlight that families may experience their loved one's dying differently than clinical staff — underscoring the need for active, ongoing communication to understand and address family concerns during the final days.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're supporting a loved one in their final days, your perspective matters. Don't hesitate to share your observations and concerns with the care team — they may see things differently, and your input can help bridge that gap.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For clinicians and senior care staff, these findings reinforce that routine check-ins with families about how they perceive comfort and symptoms can reveal concerns that clinical assessments alone may miss.

**Caveats:** Small sample of relatives (n=42); single-country, two-hospital setting; EOLD-CAD scale was designed for dementia patients but applied more broadly here.

### 5. Palliative Care Needs Rounds in Nursing Homes May Reduce Emergency Visits and Hospitalizations

**Journal:** *Age and Ageing* | **Published:** June 1, 2026
**PMID:** 42315168 | **DOI:** 10.1093/ageing/afag173

**The study:** An Australian health service implemented specialist palliative care "needs rounds" — regular outreach visits — in 39 of 56 residential aged care homes over nine months. Comparing outcomes to the same period two years earlier, emergency department presentations fell by 20% and hospital admissions dropped by 64%. Estimated cost savings were nearly AU$500,000 per month. However, the study period overlapped with COVID-19, making it difficult to attribute changes solely to the intervention.

**Why it matters:** If confirmed by future controlled studies, this approach could offer a scalable way to bring palliative expertise into nursing homes, improving comfort while reducing disruptive hospital transfers.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Ask your care home if they have access to palliative care specialists — either on-site or through outreach visits. Specialist involvement can help manage symptoms and avoid unnecessary trips to the emergency room.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living operators, partnering with local palliative care services for regular "needs rounds" may improve resident comfort and reduce costly hospital transfers, though more research is needed to confirm these benefits.

**Caveats:** Pre-post observational design without a control group; conducted during COVID-19, confounding interpretation; full implementation costs not captured.

### 6. Older Adults With Dementia Experience Poorer Quality of Death and Fewer End-of-Life Discussions

**Journal:** *Archives of Gerontology and Geriatrics* | **Published:** June 2026
**PMID:** 41856050 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.archger.2026.106218

**The study:** A nationwide Japanese survey of bereaved families (N=45,509 decedents aged 65+) compared end-of-life outcomes between older adults with and without dementia across major causes of death. Among those with cancer and dementia, the odds of being aware of their condition, communicating meaningfully with loved ones, and dying in a preferred place were significantly lower. Those with dementia also had fewer documented discussions about care preferences, though they were more likely to be rated as free from physical distress.

**Why it matters:** These findings reveal that dementia complicates end-of-life planning and communication, signaling a need for earlier conversations and dementia-inclusive care approaches.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or someone you love has been diagnosed with dementia, consider having conversations about care preferences early — while communication is still possible. Documenting wishes now can guide decisions later.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Clinicians and care teams should integrate advance care planning into routine dementia care, recognizing that waiting until late stages may mean missing the window for meaningful patient input.

**Caveats:** Retrospective, survey-based design relying on proxy reports; cultural context (Japan) may affect generalizability.

### 7. Caregivers Value Pain Relief but Need More Support When Loved Ones Receive Non-Surgical Hip Fracture Treatment

**Journal:** *European Geriatric Medicine* | **Published:** June 2026
**PMID:** 41784868 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s41999-026-01432-y

**The study:** Dutch researchers interviewed informal caregivers of frail older adults who received a SPING block (a spinal injection using phenol for pain control) as a non-operative approach to hip fracture care. Four themes emerged: caregivers prioritized pain relief above all else; decision-making carried moral weight and emotional burden; witnessing decline was distressing; and caregiving responsibilities extended well beyond hospital discharge. While caregivers valued rapid pain control and earlier discharge, many reported insufficient communication about long-term effects.

**Why it matters:** Non-operative pain management for frail elders can align with patient goals, but caregivers need clear information and ongoing support to navigate decisions and the care that follows.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If surgery isn't recommended for a hip fracture, ask your care team about comfort-focused options and what to expect afterward. Knowing the plan can ease worry for you and your family.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and care professionals, these findings highlight that caregiver support shouldn't end at hospital discharge. Continued communication and resources are essential when patients transition home with ongoing care needs.

**Caveats:** Single-center, qualitative study; small sample; findings specific to one intervention (SPING block).

### 8. Living Alone and Dementia Linked to Unmet Social, Emotional, and Spiritual Needs at End of Life

**Journal:** *Age and Ageing* | **Published:** July 2, 2026
**PMID:** 42407095 | **DOI:** 10.1093/ageing/afag202

**The study:** Using end-of-life proxy interviews from the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (N=507 deceased adults aged 55+), researchers assessed whether social, psychological, and spiritual needs were met in the last three months of life. Most individuals had these needs met (74–82%), with family providing the bulk of support. However, those who lived alone or had dementia were significantly less likely to have their needs addressed. Higher education was linked to better spiritual support.

**Why it matters:** Heavy reliance on family-based support creates gaps for those without robust informal networks, pointing to a need for more formal services to ensure equitable end-of-life care.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you live alone or have limited family nearby, consider connecting with community organizations, faith groups, or social services that can provide companionship and support as your needs change.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For caregivers and professionals, these findings call for proactive outreach to isolated elders and those with dementia, ensuring that social, emotional, and spiritual care isn't left to chance.

**Caveats:** Proxy-reported data (family members' perceptions); English population; cross-sectional design.

### 9. Home-Based Dementia Care Models Using Nurse Practitioners Show Promise for Supporting Aging in Place

**Journal:** *Research in Gerontological Nursing* | **Published:** June 23, 2026
**PMID:** 42343547 | **DOI:** 10.3928/19404921-20260612-01

**The study:** A scoping review examined 17 studies describing 10 models of home-based care for people with advancing dementia that incorporate nurse practitioners (NPs). Outcomes included high-quality care ratings, reduced emergency department use, shorter hospital stays, delayed nursing home placement, and high rates of advance care planning and palliative care transitions.

**Why it matters:** For people with dementia who struggle to access office-based care, NP-led home visits may offer a sustainable way to manage complex needs, honor preferences, and avoid disruptive hospitalizations.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If traveling to the doctor's office has become difficult, ask your healthcare provider or insurer about home-based care programs — some include nurse practitioners who can manage many medical needs in your own home.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and senior living professionals, advocating for or implementing NP-supported home care models could help people with dementia remain at home longer with better symptom management and care coordination.

**Caveats:** Scoping review (summarizes existing evidence, does not assess quality); included studies varied in design and outcomes measured.

### 10. Nurses Identify Key Strategies to Improve Pain Management for People With Dementia in Nursing Homes

**Journal:** *Geriatric Nursing* | **Published:** July 6, 2026
**PMID:** 42407320 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.gerinurse.2026.104153

**The study:** Norwegian researchers used the Nominal Group Technique to interview 28 nurses providing dementia care in nursing homes. The nurses generated 112 ideas for improving pain management, organized into eight themes. The highest-ranked priorities were mapping and assessing pain when residents can clearly express it, and ensuring staff have appropriate competence. Nurses also emphasized relational continuity, professional training, and multidimensional approaches addressing physical, emotional, and social aspects of pain.

**Why it matters:** Pain in dementia is notoriously difficult to assess and treat. These frontline insights offer practical direction for training and care model development.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or a loved one with dementia seems uncomfortable, speak up — staff observations combined with family input can help identify pain that might otherwise go unnoticed.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For nursing home administrators, investing in staff education on dementia-specific pain assessment and fostering continuity of care relationships may significantly improve residents' comfort.

**Caveats:** Single-country (Norway), qualitative design; findings reflect nurses' perspectives, not direct patient outcomes.

### 11. Brief Online Training Improves Clinicians' Confidence and Documentation of Life-Sustaining Treatment Conversations

**Journal:** *Geriatric Nursing* | **Published:** June 2026
**PMID:** 41932056 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.gerinurse.2026.104017

**The study:** Forty-five advanced practice clinicians (APCs) providing home-based geriatric care participated in a one-hour online training on discussing Physician Orders for Life-Sustaining Treatment (POLST) forms. The training included instruction, role-playing, and debriefing. At three months, significant improvements were observed in POLST documentation, discussions, and uploads in electronic records, as well as in clinician comfort addressing end-of-life topics and cultural/religious perspectives.

**Why it matters:** Even brief, targeted education can help clinicians feel more prepared to have difficult conversations and document patient wishes — a critical step toward honoring preferences.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If a clinician raises the POLST form or asks about your wishes for life-sustaining treatment, know that they've likely been trained to guide this conversation respectfully. Your input shapes the care you'll receive.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For healthcare organizations, investing in scalable training like this may improve both clinician confidence and the quality of advance care planning documentation across care settings.

**Caveats:** Single-site quality improvement initiative; modest sample size (N=45); no control group.

### 12. Polypharmacy Persists Even in Late-Stage Palliative Care — Potentially Inappropriate Medications Remain Common

**Journal:** *Maturitas* | **Published:** June 2026
**PMID:** 41950773 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.maturitas.2026.108928

**The study:** In a Ligurian (Italy) point-prevalence study of 294 hospitalized older adults with palliative care needs, 93.9% were taking five or more medications (polypharmacy), with a median of nine drugs. Potentially inappropriate medications (PIMs) — drugs unlikely to benefit and possibly harmful — were identified in 68.2% of patients. Notably, medication burden and PIM prevalence did not decline even in the most advanced stages of illness.

**Why it matters:** These findings suggest that current practice often falls short of aligning medication use with comfort-focused goals, raising concerns about unnecessary side effects and burden in seriously ill patients.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're receiving palliative care or have a serious illness, ask your doctor whether all your medications are still needed. Some may no longer help and could cause unwanted side effects.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For clinicians and pharmacists, these data underscore the importance of regular medication reviews — especially as goals shift toward comfort — to reduce pill burden and potential harms.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional, single-region study; observational (cannot prove causation); PIMs identified using one criteria set (STOPP-Frail).

### 13. Cultural and Regional Factors Influence Whether Physicians Prescribe Antibiotics at End of Life

**Journal:** *BMC Geriatrics* | **Published:** May 12, 2026
**PMID:** 42121047 | **DOI:** 10.1186/s12877-026-07477-9

**The study:** Swiss researchers surveyed 195 physicians across three linguistic regions about whether they would prescribe antibiotics for aspiration pneumonia in a frail older patient receiving palliative care. Only 22.1% said they would prescribe, primarily to relieve dyspnea. Prescribing rates were significantly higher among French-speaking physicians (33.7%) than German- or Italian-speaking colleagues (13.8% and 11.1%). Most physicians believed withholding antibiotics could shorten life but did not think antibiotics improved comfort. Notably, 77.9% said they would reconsider their decision if the family disagreed.

**Why it matters:** Antibiotic prescribing at end of life remains uncertain territory, shaped by cultural norms, family input, and individual physician judgment — highlighting the need for clearer guidance and communication tools.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or a loved one is near the end of life and develops an infection, ask the care team what treatment options exist and how each might affect comfort. There's no single right answer, but your values should guide the decision.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For healthcare teams, these findings reinforce that family conversations and cultural sensitivity are essential when navigating antibiotic decisions in palliative settings.

**Caveats:** Survey-based (self-reported intentions, not actual prescribing); conducted in Switzerland; vignette-based scenario may not capture real-world complexity.

### 14. Virtual Reality Shows Promise as a Safe, Non-Drug Approach for Symptom Relief in Palliative Care

**Journal:** *Psychology and Aging* | **Published:** July 23, 2026
**PMID:** 42490252 | **DOI:** 10.1037/pag0000981

**The study:** A systematic review of 54 studies examined virtual reality (VR) use in palliative care across the lifespan, including older adults. Most studies involved high levels of immersion and found VR to be safe, feasible, and helpful for managing physical and psychological symptoms. Adverse reactions were rare (reported in 9% of studies). VR was also used for caregiver support and clinician education.

**Why it matters:** As a non-pharmacological option, VR could offer meaningful symptom relief — such as reducing anxiety, pain, or distress — without adding to medication burden.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're receiving palliative care and interested in trying something new for relaxation or distraction, ask your care team if virtual reality experiences are available. Early research suggests they're generally safe and may help with symptoms.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living and hospice programs, incorporating VR into palliative offerings may expand the toolkit for symptom management and provide respite for caregivers.

**Caveats:** Systematic review of heterogeneous studies; most included mixed adult populations (not exclusively older adults); long-term effects not well studied.

### 15. Oral Health Matters in Palliative Care — But Dental Services Are Often Missing

**Journal:** *Gerodontology* | **Published:** June 2026
**PMID:** 41404855 | **DOI:** 10.1111/ger.70035

**The study:** Chilean researchers conducted qualitative interviews with seven healthcare professionals, four caregivers, and four patients in a palliative care unit. Participants recognized oral health as important for quality of life but identified significant barriers to dental care, including limited integration of dental professionals into the palliative care team and inadequate oral health training for other staff.

**Why it matters:** Oral discomfort can significantly affect eating, communication, and overall well-being at end of life — yet dental care often remains an afterthought in palliative settings.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're receiving palliative care and experiencing mouth pain, dry mouth, or difficulty eating, mention it to your care team. Oral health is part of overall comfort and deserves attention.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For palliative care programs, these findings argue for greater collaboration with dental professionals and basic oral care training for nursing staff to address a commonly overlooked need.

**Caveats:** Small, qualitative study; single palliative care unit in Chile; findings may not generalize to other settings.

### 16. Infections Are the Leading Cause of Death in Hospitalized Dementia Patients — Palliative Care Documentation Lags

**Journal:** *Journal of Alzheimer's Disease* | **Published:** June 2026
**PMID:** 42081122 | **DOI:** 10.1177/13872877261445578

**The study:** A retrospective review of 122 patients with dementia who died in a Brazilian geriatric hospital (2015–2023) found that infectious diseases — primarily pneumonia (48.3%) and sepsis (18.9%) — accounted for 67.2% of deaths. Antibiotics were prescribed in 76.2% of cases, antipsychotics in 58.1%, but palliative care measures were documented in only 41% of cases.

**Why it matters:** The gap between high antibiotic use and low palliative care documentation suggests opportunities to better integrate comfort-focused approaches into dementia end-of-life care.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're caring for someone with advanced dementia, ask the medical team how they balance infection treatment with comfort care. Understanding the goals can help you advocate for your loved one's quality of life.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For hospitals and care teams, these findings highlight the need for protocols that prompt palliative care consultations and documentation for dementia patients, especially when infections occur.

**Caveats:** Single-center, retrospective study; Brazilian context; palliative care documentation may underrepresent actual practices.

### 17. Expert Panel Releases Practical Guidance for Palliative Care in Rapidly Progressive Prion Diseases

**Journal:** *Age and Ageing* | **Published:** July 2, 2026
**PMID:** 42472622 | **DOI:** 10.1093/ageing/afag206

**The study:** A multidisciplinary UK panel — including palliative care doctors, prion disease specialists, nurses, and a patient advocate — developed consensus-based recommendations for managing Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease and related prion disorders. Topics covered include prognostic uncertainty, complex symptom management (such as myoclonus, cognitive decline, and behavioral changes), caregiver distress, and communication strategies. The guidance is also applicable to other rapidly progressive dementias.

**Why it matters:** Prion diseases are rare but devastating, often progressing from diagnosis to death in months. This guidance fills a critical gap for clinicians unfamiliar with these conditions.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or a loved one is diagnosed with a rapidly progressive dementia, ask for a referral to specialists experienced in these conditions. Palliative care involvement early can help manage symptoms and support decision-making.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For healthcare providers, this consensus document offers a practical resource when caring for patients with unfamiliar, fast-moving neurological diseases — even without prior experience.

**Caveats:** Consensus-based (limited high-quality evidence); UK-focused panel; applicability to other healthcare systems may vary.

### 18. Existential Distress in Older Cancer Patients: A Qualitative Study From Iran

**Journal:** *Aging & Mental Health* | **Published:** July 2026
**PMID:** 41498225 | **DOI:** 10.1080/13607863.2025.2612171

**The study:** Iranian researchers interviewed 17 adults aged 65 and older with colorectal cancer about their experience of existential distress — deep psychological suffering related to meaning, identity, and mortality. Three themes emerged: inadequate self-realization (feeling like a burden, diminished self-worth), confronting uncertainty and death (fear of the unknown, pervasive awareness of dying), and disruption of meaningful relationships (stigma, isolation).

**Why it matters:** Beyond physical symptoms, older cancer patients may carry profound emotional and spiritual suffering shaped by personal beliefs and cultural values — needs that deserve attention alongside medical treatment.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If cancer has left you feeling lost, fearful, or isolated, you're not alone. Talking to a counselor, chaplain, or trusted friend about these feelings can be part of your care.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For oncology and palliative care teams, these findings underscore the importance of culturally sensitive psychosocial and spiritual support, recognizing that existential suffering can be as debilitating as physical symptoms.

**Caveats:** Small, qualitative study; single cancer center in Iran; findings reflect specific cultural context.

### 19. Geriatric Oncology in Latin America: Survey Reveals Major Gaps in Care for Older Adults With Blood Cancers

**Journal:** *Journal of Geriatric Oncology* | **Published:** July 22, 2026
**PMID:** 42485998 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jgo.2026.103069

**The study:** A survey of 73 cancer centers across 16 Latin American countries assessed resources for managing older adults with hematological malignancies (blood cancers). Only 27% had dedicated clinics for older patients; just 8% had geriatric oncology specialists. Formal training programs existed at fewer than 10% of centers, and early palliative care was available in only 23%. Among transplant centers, nearly half maintained strict age limits for eligibility.

**Why it matters:** These gaps leave many older Latin American cancer patients without access to geriatric expertise, increasing the risk of treatment toxicity and undertreatment alike.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're an older adult diagnosed with a blood cancer, ask whether your care team includes or can consult with a geriatrician. Their expertise can help tailor treatment to your overall health and goals.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For policymakers and healthcare leaders, these findings highlight an urgent need to train more geriatric oncology specialists and integrate geriatric principles into cancer care across the region.

**Caveats:** Survey-based (self-reported); center-level data may not capture all patient experiences; Latin American context.

### 20. Economic Evidence on Palliative Care in Nursing Homes Remains Limited and Inconclusive

**Journal:** *Age and Ageing* | **Published:** July 2, 2026
**PMID:** 42497095 | **DOI:** 10.1093/ageing/afag217

**The study:** A scoping review identified 15 studies examining the costs and cost-effectiveness of palliative care in residential aged care homes. Findings were mixed: some studies reported reduced healthcare costs, others found increased costs, and only two full economic evaluations suggested favorable cost-effectiveness — though with uncertain statistical significance. Methodological heterogeneity limited robust conclusions.

**Why it matters:** Without consistent economic evidence, policymakers and administrators face uncertainty about investing in nursing home palliative care programs, despite their potential benefits for residents.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** The value of palliative care in nursing homes goes beyond dollars — it's about comfort, dignity, and alignment with your wishes. Don't let cost concerns alone drive decisions about the care you deserve.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living operators and policymakers, this review underscores the need for rigorous, standardized economic evaluations to guide resource allocation and advocacy for palliative services.

**Caveats:** Scoping review (not a systematic synthesis of effect sizes); included studies varied widely in methods and populations.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**

Several studies appear in both digests, reinforcing consistent messages:

- **Palliative care needs extend far beyond cancer.** The Italian NECPAL prevalence study (PMID 42366281) is featured in both runs, confirming that more than half of hospitalized older adults and nearly half of nursing home residents have palliative care needs — most without cancer diagnoses.
- **Dementia complicates end-of-life planning and quality of death.** The Japanese bereaved-family survey (PMID 41856050) appears in both digests, showing that older adults with dementia are less likely to be aware of their condition, communicate meaningfully, or have documented care preferences.
- **Living alone and dementia create gaps in end-of-life support.** The English Longitudinal Study of Ageing analysis (PMID 42407095) is repeated, underscoring that those living alone or with cognitive impairment are significantly less likely to have social, emotional, and spiritual needs met.
- **Palliative care "needs rounds" in nursing homes may reduce hospitalizations.** The Australian outreach intervention (PMID 42315168) appears in both runs, with consistent findings of reduced emergency visits and admissions — though COVID-era confounding remains a caveat.
- **Polypharmacy persists at end of life.** The Italian point-prevalence study (PMID 41950773) is covered in both digests, confirming that medication burden and potentially inappropriate prescribing do not decline even in advanced illness.
- **Nurse practitioners in home-based dementia care show promise.** The scoping review of NP-led models (PMID 42343547) is featured again, with outcomes including reduced ED use, delayed nursing home placement, and high advance care planning rates.
- **Brief training improves clinician confidence in POLST conversations.** The quality improvement study of advanced practice clinicians (PMID 41932056) reappears, showing that one hour of online training improved documentation and comfort with end-of-life discussions.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Multiple studies in this batch reveal that families, clinicians, and systems fail to align around end-of-life care — not because no one cares, but because they see things differently. The Belgian study (PMID 42530751) shows doctors rate comfort higher than families do. The Australian aged care staff study (PMID 42163024) describes staff wanting to start conversations earlier but facing reluctant families. The Dutch elective-procedures analysis (PMID 42545407) finds that plannable interventions continue even when time to benefit is short — suggesting goals-of-care conversations aren't happening when they should.

**Why pitch this now:** These aren't isolated findings; together they suggest a systemic "perception gap" in end-of-life care. With aging populations and growing demand on palliative services, closing this gap is increasingly urgent — and actionable, given the training interventions (PMID 41932056) and communication strategies (PMID 42163024) already showing promise.

**Angle:** A feature titled "The Perception Gap: Why Families and Clinicians See Dying Differently — and What to Do About It" could target both family caregivers (helping them understand why clinical communication feels misaligned) and senior living professionals (offering practical strategies to bridge the divide).
