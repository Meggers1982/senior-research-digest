# Fact-Check Report: Senior Living Research Digest — New This Week — October 2026

**Primary audience:** older adults and seniors | **Secondary audience:** families, caregivers, and senior living professionals

### Study 1: Tai chi, yoga and dance top the list for Parkinson's symptoms — and more weekly exercise tracks with better movement
**PMID:** 42859236 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Unverifiable superlative** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "this is the largest synthesis yet suggesting that mind-body approaches deserve a place alongside conventional gym-style training"
- **Abstract says:** "We synthesized 173 randomized trials using covariance-aware Bayesian random-effects network meta-analysis... The evidence did not establish a universally best modality, an optimal dose, or an individual causal target."
- **Problem:** The abstract never claims this is the largest synthesis of its kind, and makes a point of saying no best modality was established. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Replace with "this large synthesis of 173 trials" and keep the authors' caveat that no modality was shown to be best."

### Study 2: The MIND diet is linked to lower Parkinson's risk and later onset — but the evidence is thin
**PMID:** 42858790 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "First systematic attempt" not supported** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "this is the first systematic attempt to ask whether it also tracks with Parkinson's"
- **Abstract says:** "The Mediterranean-DASH Intervention for Neurodegenerative Delay (MIND) diet has been associated with cognitive benefits, but its relationship with Parkinson's disease (PD) remains unclear. We systematically reviewed evidence on associations between MIND diet adherence and PD-related outcomes."
- **Problem:** The abstract does not claim priority; it only states the relationship is unclear. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change to "this systematic review pulls together what evidence exists on the MIND diet and Parkinson's.""

**Issue B. "All available research" overstates the search** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Neurologists systematically reviewed all available research on the MIND diet"
- **Abstract says:** "PubMed, Embase, and Google Scholar were searched through June 2026."
- **Problem:** Three databases searched to a cutoff date is not "all available research."
- **Suggested fix:** "Say "searched three major databases through June 2026.""

### Study 3: Blood draws keep coming right up to the end of life in cancer — and almost nobody has shown they help
**PMID:** 42857913 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Registration mislabeled** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "A PRISMA-registered systematic review"
- **Abstract says:** "We conducted a PRISMA 2020 systematic review registered in PROSPERO (CRD420261493528)."
- **Problem:** PRISMA is a reporting guideline, not a registry; the review was registered in PROSPERO.
- **Suggested fix:** "Write "a PROSPERO-registered systematic review following PRISMA 2020.""

**Issue B. Causal verb for an association** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the review found advance care planning, more than the palliative care model itself, predicted lower utilization"
- **Abstract says:** "Associations with palliative care services varied by model and setting, whereas timely advance care planning was more consistently associated with lower utilization."
- **Problem:** "Predicted" implies a forecasting/causal relationship in heterogeneous observational studies that only reported associations.
- **Suggested fix:** "Use "was more consistently associated with lower testing.""

### Study 4: Adding nighttime melatonin to an exercise program sharpened cholesterol gains in postmenopausal women
**PMID:** 42858810 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Comparison group misattributed** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "The group taking melatonin alongside training showed additional improvements the placebo-training group did not — notably lower LDL cholesterol and triglycerides, plus better liver enzymes, glucose and markers of cellular damage."
- **Abstract says:** "Alanine aminotransferase, creatine kinase (CK), alkaline phosphatase, glucose, total cholesterol... decreased... in CTM group compared with C group at T1... Comparatively with CTP, triglycerides (p < 0.05) and LDL-C (p < 0.01) decreased in CTM group at T1. In CTP group, CK (p < 0.05) decreased and phosphorus (p < 0.001) increased over time."
- **Problem:** Only LDL-C and triglycerides were better in the melatonin group than in the placebo-training group. The liver enzyme, glucose and creatine kinase improvements were measured against the no-exercise control, and the placebo-training group also lowered creatine kinase, so "markers of cellular damage" is not an exclusive melatonin advantage.
- **Suggested fix:** "State that melatonin's only advantage over training-plus-placebo was lower LDL cholesterol and triglycerides, and note that liver enzyme and glucose improvements were relative to the untrained control group."

### Study 5: A dementia diagnosis didn't raise the odds of going back to the hospital after rehab

### Study 8: A simple bedside score predicts how long patients live after starting home palliative care
**PMID:** 42857916 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Unreported mortality clustering** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "since a large share of deaths cluster in the first one to two months after referral — a signal that many patients are being referred far too late"
- **Abstract says:** "Patients with PPS ≤30 had markedly reduced short-term survival, particularly within the first 30-60 days."
- **Problem:** The abstract describes short survival in the low-performance-status subgroup, not the proportion of all deaths occurring in the first 30-60 days. The "referred far too late" conclusion is the digest's own inference.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say "patients admitted with a performance score of 30% or less had markedly reduced survival in the first 30-60 days," and present the late-referral point as context rather than a study finding."

### Study 9: A hospital system found a way to let general neurologists prescribe new Alzheimer's drugs safely
**PMID:** 42857600 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. ARIA rates converted imprecisely** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "ask directly about the roughly one-in-five to one-in-four chance of brain swelling or microbleeds on follow-up scans"
- **Abstract says:** "Total amyloid-related imaging abnormality rate was 17.4% for lecanemab and 28.8% for donanemab."
- **Problem:** 17.4% is closer to one in six and 28.8% is closer to three in ten, so the stated range understates the upper end and is not drug-specific.
- **Suggested fix:** "Write "about one in six on lecanemab and close to three in ten on donanemab.""

### Study 10: Knowing someone with dementia changes attitudes more than any health campaign
**PMID:** 42857477 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

**Issue A. Causal headline contradicts the digest's own caveat** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Knowing someone with dementia changes attitudes more than any health campaign"
- **Abstract says:** "A cross-sectional survey was conducted among the general public in Makueni County, Kenya... Dementia experiences were associated with better dementia knowledge (ΔR2 = 0.10, p < 0.001) and attitudes (ΔR2 = 0.06, p < 0.001)"
- **Problem:** The study is cross-sectional and reports associations only; the headline says experience "changes" attitudes, directly contradicting the digest's own caveat that causation cannot be shown. No health campaign was studied or compared.
- **Suggested fix:** "Rephrase as "People who have known someone with dementia hold more positive attitudes — and knowledge alone may not be enough.""

**Issue B. Unsupported comparison with education campaigns** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "this suggests that contact and lived experience — not leaflets and lectures — is what actually shifts how communities treat people with dementia"
- **Abstract says:** "Dementia experiences were associated with better dementia knowledge... and attitudes... although sociodemographics accounted for the greatest variance... Regression models revealed that a target knowledge score of 49.5/50 on the Dementia Knowledge Assessment Scale (DKAS) would be required to statistically achieve the aspirational threshold of 102 on the Dementia Attitude Scale (DAS) in this sample."
- **Problem:** The survey compared variance explained by different factors within one sample; it did not evaluate any educational intervention, and the knowledge-target result is a statistical extrapolation, not evidence that contact outperforms campaigns.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say the modelling implies knowledge alone is unlikely to reach the attitude threshold, and that personal experience was associated with more positive attitudes — without claiming superiority over awareness campaigns."

**Issue C. Omitted dominant predictor** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Having personal experience with dementia was linked to both better knowledge and more positive attitudes"
- **Abstract says:** "Dementia experiences were associated with better dementia knowledge (ΔR2 = 0.10, p < 0.001) and attitudes (ΔR2 = 0.06, p < 0.001), although sociodemographics accounted for the greatest variance."
- **Problem:** The digest leaves out that sociodemographic factors, not experience, explained the greatest variance in both models — which undercuts the framing built around experience.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add that sociodemographic characteristics accounted for the largest share of variance in knowledge and attitudes."

### Study 11: The same Alzheimer's damage in the brain hurts thinking more in people who had harder lives
**PMID:** 42856984 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Claim about pathology formation not tested** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "It suggests that social and educational advantage doesn't stop Alzheimer's plaques and tangles from forming — it buffers how much damage they do to everyday thinking"
- **Abstract says:** "SDoH did not modify the APOE-ε4-pathology association (p = 0.806)"
- **Problem:** The abstract reports only that SDoH did not modify the gene–pathology relationship; it does not report testing whether social or educational advantage itself is associated with the presence of AD pathology. The digest converts a null interaction into a null main effect.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to: 'Social circumstances did not alter the link between APOE-ε4 and Alzheimer's pathology, but they did alter how strongly that pathology tracked with poorer thinking.'"

**Issue B. Composition of the SDoH measure unverifiable** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "that link was substantially stronger in people with unfavorable social circumstances (education, income, occupation)"
- **Abstract says:** "SDoH modified this pathology-cognition association (p = 0·002), which was stronger in the unfavorable ... versus the favorable SDoH profile"
- **Problem:** The abstract does not specify which variables made up the favorable/unfavorable SDoH profiles. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Either drop the parenthetical list or attribute it ('the authors' social determinants profile, which the paper defines in full')."

**Issue C. Enrichment framing beyond measured variables** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "this adds to evidence that cognitive and social enrichment may help your brain tolerate disease — keeping up education, complex hobbies, work and social engagement across later life is worth prioritizing"
- **Abstract says:** "AD pathology was more strongly associated with poorer cognition in unfavorable SDoH profiles, highlighting SDoH as targets for AD prevention"
- **Problem:** Hobbies and social engagement were not among the reported study variables; the study measured a social determinants profile in an autopsy cohort, not enrichment activities.
- **Suggested fix:** "Limit the advice to the constructs studied (education, income, occupation/social circumstance) and note that activity-based enrichment was not measured here."

### Study 12: Losing the pleasure in things may be the earliest warning sign of frontotemporal dementia
**PMID:** 42856930 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. 'Earliest' overstates the staging result** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Losing the pleasure in things may be the earliest warning sign of frontotemporal dementia"
- **Abstract says:** "Event-based modeling placed consummatory and anticipatory anhedonia dimensions as early features of bvFTD occurring prior to overt cognitive changes"
- **Problem:** The model staged anhedonia relative to apathy and cognitive symptoms and found it early, not necessarily first; the abstract does not claim it is the earliest sign.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change to 'may be an early warning sign of frontotemporal dementia.'"

**Issue B. 'Far earlier than cognitive tests' is an extrapolation** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "may help separate the two far earlier than cognitive tests do"
- **Abstract says:** "support the value of multidimensional hedonic assessment for improving dementia differentiation"
- **Problem:** The abstract reports probabilistic ordering within a model, not a head-to-head comparison of lead time against cognitive testing.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say the modeling placed pleasure loss before overt cognitive change in bvFTD, so it may add diagnostic information that cognitive tests alone miss."

### Study 13: The first broken bone is the warning: geriatricians argue fracture clinics should be screening for frailty, not just bone density
**PMID:** 42843462 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Timing of CGA shifted from admission to post-discharge weeks** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the review frames the weeks after a fragility fracture as the moment when comprehensive geriatric assessment most changes the trajectory"
- **Abstract says:** "Upon hospital admission, the comprehensive geriatric assessment (CGA) is pivotal to detect and manage frailty and consequences"
- **Problem:** The abstract locates CGA at hospital admission, not in the weeks after discharge; the body text of the digest itself says 'on hospital admission.'
- **Suggested fix:** "Align the story angle with the body: the review positions CGA at admission, with FLS bridging care from acute setting to home."

**Issue B. Specific assessment checklist not in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "ask specifically for a bone density scan, a medication review, a falls risk assessment, and a check of vitamin D and muscle strength — before you leave"
- **Abstract says:** "Timely interventions removing modifiable risk factors are recommended to counteract the frailty vicious cycle, including falls and fragility fractures"
- **Problem:** The abstract endorses CGA and FLS generically; the itemized list (DXA, vitamin D, medication review) is not verifiable from the abstract.
- **Suggested fix:** "Frame as 'ask whether a comprehensive geriatric assessment and Fracture Liaison Service review are part of your care,' and mark the specific tests as standard practice rather than this review's findings."

**Issue C. Author specialties unverifiable** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "A group of Italian geriatricians and orthopedic specialists"
- **Abstract says:** "Affiliations list departments of Medicine and Surgery, General Medicine, Rehabilitation, Internal Medicine and Geriatrics units in Italy, on behalf of FFN Italy"
- **Problem:** Orthopedic specialty of the authors cannot be confirmed from the abstract. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say 'Italian geriatricians and orthogeriatric clinicians writing on behalf of FFN Italy.'"

**Issue D. Unsourced claim about current practice** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Most people who break a bone from a minor fall are treated for the fracture and sent home without anyone assessing why they fell or testing their bones"
- **Abstract says:** "The Fracture Liaison Service (FLS) is a coordinated care model designed to bridge the care gap of people facing fragility fractures in a time-dependent manner"
- **Problem:** The abstract refers to a 'care gap' but gives no prevalence figure supporting 'most people.'
- **Suggested fix:** "Soften to 'many people ... which is the care gap the authors say Fracture Liaison Services are designed to close.'"

### Study 14: Steroids in advanced cancer: two-thirds of patients had a side effect, and most showed up within 10 days
**PMID:** 42843431 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline overstates early-onset proportion** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "two-thirds of patients had a side effect, and most showed up within 10 days"
- **Abstract says:** "Approximately half of all adverse events occurred within the first 10 days of treatment."
- **Problem:** 'Most' implies a clear majority; the abstract reports about half. The digest body states it correctly, so the headline contradicts the body.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change headline to 'and about half showed up within 10 days.'"

**Issue B. Comparison to what patients are told is unsupported** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "this suggests their short-term burden may be substantially higher and faster-arriving than many patients are told"
- **Abstract says:** "adverse events potentially associated with short-term systemic corticosteroid exposure were frequently recorded, often early after treatment initiation ... These exploratory findings support the relevance of existing recommandations for careful monitoring"
- **Problem:** The study made no comparison with patient counselling or prior expectations; the authors frame the findings as consistent with existing monitoring recommendations.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to 'suggests short-term side effects are common and often appear early, supporting existing advice to monitor closely and reassess the dose.'"

### Study 15: A short, intensive home visit program after hospital discharge cut 90-day readmissions by a third
**PMID:** 42859151 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Causal verb in headline for observational data** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "A short, intensive home visit program after hospital discharge cut 90-day readmissions by a third"
- **Abstract says:** "The Safe Homecoming intervention was associated with a lower risk of first hospital readmission at 90 days ... Given the observational design, small intervention sample, potential residual confounding ... these findings should be considered exploratory"
- **Problem:** 'Cut' asserts causation from a retrospective propensity-matched cohort the authors themselves call exploratory.
- **Suggested fix:** "'...was linked to a sharply lower 90-day readmission rate' or 'tracked with ~40% fewer 90-day readmissions.'"

**Issue B. Headline figure inconsistent with body** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "cut 90-day readmissions by a third"
- **Abstract says:** "cumulative incidence of first hospital readmission was 20.3% in the intervention group and 33.6% among controls ... Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard ratio was 0.57 (95% CI 0.37-0.86)"
- **Problem:** The relative reduction is about 40% (SHR 0.57, i.e., 43% lower), which the body states; 'a third' understates and conflicts with the body text.
- **Suggested fix:** "Use 'by about 40%' in the headline to match the body."

**Issue C. Selection mechanism unverifiable** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "residual confounding is likely since clinicians chose who got the program"
- **Abstract says:** "We included 128 patients who received the interprofessional Safe homecoming intervention at a single primary health care centre ... and 512 propensity score-matched controls"
- **Problem:** The abstract notes potential residual confounding but does not state that clinicians selected participants. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "'Residual confounding is likely because participants were not randomized to the program.'"

### Study 16: One set of strength exercises worked as well as three for older adults with cognitive impairment — and neither improved memory
**PMID:** 42678372 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Primary outcomes described as secondary** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "cognition and BDNF were secondary-signal outcomes that an 8-week trial of this size was unlikely to detect even if real"
- **Abstract says:** "Primary outcomes assessed at baseline and post-intervention included the Dementia Rating Scale-2 (DRS-2), serum BDNF, and the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB). Secondary outcomes were creatine kinase (CK) and composite strength"
- **Problem:** DRS-2 and BDNF were pre-specified primary outcomes; calling them secondary signals softens a null result on the trial's main questions.
- **Suggested fix:** "State that cognition and BDNF were primary outcomes and both were null, while noting the small sample and 8-week duration limit power."

**Issue B. 'Older adults' not established by abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "One set of strength exercises worked as well as three for older adults with cognitive impairment"
- **Abstract says:** "individuals with cognitive impairment (CI)"
- **Problem:** The abstract gives no ages or age range for participants, so describing them as older adults is unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say 'people with cognitive impairment' and note that participant ages are not reported in the abstract."

**Issue C. Between-group superiority implied** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the single-set group gained more on the Short Physical Performance Battery (+2.7 points versus +1.4)"
- **Abstract says:** "Physical performance improved in both RT groups compared with CON, with SPPB increasing by 2.7 points in SET1 and 1.4 points in SET3"
- **Problem:** The abstract reports both within-group gains but no statistical test showing SET1 outperformed SET3; 'gained more' implies a demonstrated difference.
- **Suggested fix:** "'The single-set group's SPPB gain was numerically larger (+2.7 vs +1.4 points), though the study did not show one dose beat the other.'"

### Study 17: What predicts whether you feel old? Memory decline, new illness — and loneliness
**PMID:** 42625387 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Causal-sounding framing of associations** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "this preregistered study points to loneliness and memory, rather than sensory decline, as the levers most tied to a loss-focused outlook"
- **Abstract says:** "higher baseline loneliness perceived more losses and fewer gains ... Steeper declines in recall and stronger increases in morbidity were associated with heightened awareness of losses"
- **Problem:** 'Levers' implies these are modifiable causes of a loss-focused outlook; the study reports longitudinal associations only, as the digest's own Caveats acknowledge.
- **Suggested fix:** "'...points to loneliness and memory, rather than sensory decline, as the factors most consistently associated with a loss-focused outlook.'"

### Study 18: Painful hand arthritis diagnoses jumped 65% in a decade, Dutch records show
**PMID:** 42839719 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Positive predictive value described as accuracy** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "using a validated algorithm that reads doctors' free-text notes as well as diagnostic codes (90% accuracy on manual review)"
- **Abstract says:** "Manual review of 100 algorithm-identified records yielded a positive predictive value of 90%."
- **Problem:** A 90% PPV on 100 flagged records is not the same as overall accuracy; sensitivity was not reported, so cases could still be missed.
- **Suggested fix:** "'90% of flagged records were confirmed as true cases on manual review (positive predictive value).'"

### Study 19: Older adults say they're more tuned in to their bodies — but one lab test suggests they're less accurate
**PMID:** 42625395 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Clinical extrapolation beyond the study** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "it may help explain why some older adults miss or misread cardiac warning signs — a known contributor to delayed care for heart events"
- **Abstract says:** "older age was associated with reduced cardiac interoceptive sensitivity during breath-holding ... this study opens new avenues for characterizing age-related changes in interoceptive awareness and bodily regulation"
- **Problem:** The study tested healthy volunteers in a lab task and measured no clinical outcomes or symptom recognition; the link to delayed care for heart events is the digest's inference.
- **Suggested fix:** "Flag it explicitly as speculation: 'Whether this translates into missing real cardiac symptoms was not tested here.'"
