### 1. How you fill out a questionnaire may hint at dementia years before diagnosis

**Journal:** *J Gerontol B Psychol Sci Soc Sci* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 18
**PMID:** 42760255 | **DOI:** 10.1093/geronb/gbag197

**The study:** Researchers pooled eight long-running studies of aging (total N = 76,350) and looked not at what people answered on routine surveys, but at how they answered — skipped items, apparently random answers, statistically odd or internally contradictory response patterns. After adjusting for demographics and accounting for death as a competing risk, several of these 'atypical response' markers predicted later dementia diagnoses, with risk rising roughly 22% to 31% per standard deviation for multivariate outliers and incompatible answer patterns. Simply giving extreme answers (always picking the end points of a scale) was not linked to dementia risk, and the signals were strongest in people under 75 and those with more education.

**Why it matters:** It suggests that ordinary paperwork older adults already complete — health surveys, intake forms, research questionnaires — carries early behavioral signals of cognitive change that cost nothing extra to collect.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you notice questionnaires and forms are getting harder to keep straight — losing your place, giving answers that contradict each other, skipping items you would once have finished — that is worth mentioning at your next check-up as one more piece of information, not as a diagnosis. This research describes patterns across tens of thousands of people, not a test you can apply to yourself.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For researchers and large health systems, this points toward a low-burden, no-extra-cost screening signal buried in data already being collected; for senior living staff, it is a reminder that difficulty completing routine paperwork can be an observation worth documenting rather than quietly correcting.

**Caveats:** Observational design across existing cohorts — cannot establish causation and cannot be used diagnostically for an individual; effect sizes per person are modest; associations varied by cohort, age, and education, so generalizability to any single population is uncertain.

### 2. For patients in their 70s, a lower-quality donor kidney may offer no survival edge over staying on dialysis

**Journal:** *BMJ* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 18
**PMID:** 42759976 | **DOI:** 10.1136/bmj-2026-100624

**The study:** Using registry data from five European countries, researchers emulated a randomized trial among 64,036 waitlisted dialysis patients aged 20 and older, 39,244 of whom received a deceased donor kidney between 2000 and 2019. Standard-criteria donor kidneys improved five-year survival compared with staying on dialysis at every recipient age. But the benefit of expanded-criteria donor kidneys shrank with age: among 75-year-olds receiving an expanded-criteria kidney donated after circulatory death, five-year survival was 59% versus 55% on continued dialysis — statistically indistinguishable — and that group had three times the early post-transplant death rate.

**Why it matters:** Older kidney patients are routinely offered marginal-quality organs on the assumption that any transplant beats dialysis; this large analysis suggests that assumption may not hold for the oldest recipients, making the offer a genuine decision rather than an obvious yes.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you are older and on the transplant waiting list, you can ask your nephrologist exactly what kind of kidney you are being offered — standard or expanded criteria, donated after brain death or after circulatory death — and what the projected survival difference is for someone your age compared with staying on dialysis, including the risk in the first months after surgery.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and care teams supporting an older dialysis patient, this reframes transplant counseling as a quality-of-life and risk-tolerance conversation, since the surgery's early mortality is front-loaded while any benefit accrues over years.

**Caveats:** Observational registry data with statistical emulation of a trial rather than actual randomization; estimates for the oldest recipients carry wide confidence intervals; European registries may not generalize to US allocation practices; data span 2000–2019, before some recent advances in organ preservation.

### 3. Methylphenidate eased depression in palliative care patients — but the evidence is thin

**Journal:** *J Pain Symptom Manage* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 19
**PMID:** 42762836 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jpainsymman.2026.09.015

**The study:** Researchers systematically reviewed randomized trials of methylphenidate — a fast-acting stimulant — for depressive symptoms in adults with advanced, life-limiting illness receiving palliative care. Five trials with a combined 342 participants met criteria; pooled results showed a moderate improvement in depressive symptoms (SMD -0.47) and a higher rate of treatment response (RR 1.78). The authors rated overall certainty as very low to low, citing small samples, inconsistency between trials, and the fact that two of the five trials were actually studying fatigue.

**Why it matters:** Standard antidepressants take weeks to work, which can exceed a hospice patient's remaining time; a faster option matters enormously, but this analysis says the supporting evidence is preliminary rather than settled.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or someone close to you is in hospice or palliative care and struggling with low mood, it is reasonable to ask the care team whether a faster-acting option like methylphenidate has been considered — while understanding the research behind it is still limited and the decision should be individualized.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For hospice clinicians and senior living staff, the practical takeaway is permission for cautious, case-by-case trials rather than a new standard of care — and a reminder that depression in advanced illness is frequently undertreated because conventional drugs are too slow.

**Caveats:** Small N overall (342 across five trials); substantial heterogeneity (I² = 58.9%); risk of bias was high in one trial and of some concern in three; two trials measured depression only as a secondary outcome of fatigue studies; authors themselves rate certainty as very low to low.

### 4. Your own sleep, not your spouse's, tracks with your memory — a large couples study finds

**Journal:** *J Gerontol A Biol Sci Med Sci* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 17
**PMID:** 42755049 | **DOI:** 10.1093/gerona/glag235

**The study:** Drawing on four waves of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (2011–2018), researchers analyzed 4,260 married couples (8,520 people) aged 45 and older to see whether one spouse's sleep duration predicted the other's cognitive scores. Both husbands and wives showed a robust inverted U-shaped relationship between their own nighttime sleep and their own cognition — too little and too much both tracked with worse scores. A hint that husbands' sleep related to wives' cognition did not survive correction for multiple comparisons, and daytime napping showed little relationship either way.

**Why it matters:** Couples share beds, schedules, and sleep disruptions, and it has been assumed one partner's poor sleep drags down the other's brain health; this well-powered analysis found no reliable evidence for that spillover.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Focus on your own nighttime sleep — both short and unusually long durations were linked to lower cognitive scores in this study — rather than assuming your partner's restlessness is damaging your memory. If your sleep has drifted well outside your usual pattern, that is a reasonable thing to raise with your doctor.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For caregivers and senior living staff, this argues for treating each resident's or partner's sleep individually rather than assuming that fixing one spouse's sleep problem will benefit the other's cognition — though shared-bedroom disruptions remain worth addressing for comfort and safety reasons.

**Caveats:** Observational cohort — cannot establish causation; sleep duration and napping were self-reported; Chinese, married, mixed-sex, two-person households only, so findings may not generalize to other populations or living arrangements; cognition measured by brief screening batteries, not clinical diagnosis.

### 5. Steroid-sparing drugs for giant cell arteritis and polymyalgia rheumatica don't automatically mean fewer infections

**Journal:** *Joint Bone Spine* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 19
**PMID:** 42762860 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jbspin.2026.106142

**The study:** Researchers at Boston University pooled 10 randomized controlled trials (1,487 patients, average age 50 and above) that tested newer biologic and small-molecule drugs — tocilizumab, sarilumab, upadacitinib and avacopan — added to steroids in giant cell arteritis, polymyalgia rheumatica and ANCA-associated vasculitis. The drugs are marketed largely on their ability to cut steroid exposure, but infection risk only fell when the steroid dose in the treatment group was substantially lower than in the comparison group. When steroid doses stayed similar, adding the newer drug raised the relative risk of infection above 1.

**Why it matters:** These inflammatory conditions overwhelmingly affect people over 50, and infection is one of the most feared complications of treatment; the analysis suggests the benefit hinges on actually tapering the steroid, not simply adding a second drug.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you've been started on a newer drug like tocilizumab or avacopan for giant cell arteritis, polymyalgia rheumatica or vasculitis, ask your rheumatologist what the plan and timeline are for lowering your prednisone dose — this review suggests the infection-risk advantage largely depends on that taper actually happening. Also ask what infection warning signs should prompt a call.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For family members and care staff supporting someone on combination immunosuppression, the practical takeaway is vigilance: track current steroid dose alongside the newer agent, and treat fever, cough or new confusion as urgent rather than routine, since older patients on both drug classes may be at higher infection risk than the marketing suggests.

**Caveats:** Systematic review of trial data rather than a new trial; heterogeneous steroid-tapering protocols across studies limit direct comparison; infection definitions varied; trial populations averaged 50+ but results may not reflect the frailest patients seen in practice.

### 6. Prism glasses plus tablet games beat pencil-and-paper cognitive rehab for stroke survivors — and the gains lasted six months

**Journal:** *Ann Phys Rehabil Med* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 18
**PMID:** 42759357 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.rehab.2026.102168

**The study:** Italian researchers randomized 60 stroke survivors to 10 sessions over two weeks of either standard paper-and-pencil cognitive rehabilitation or Mindlenses Professional, a device pairing wearable prismatic glasses with tablet-based "serious game" training. Over six months of follow-up, the device group showed greater and sustained improvement in hemineglect and language, memory gains that emerged at three months and grew by six months, and stable visual perception — while the standard-care group's visuoperceptual scores declined over time.

**Why it matters:** Cognitive problems affect more than half of stroke survivors and long-term rehabilitation options are thin; a two-week protocol producing benefits still visible half a year later would be a meaningful addition to post-stroke care.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or your spouse is recovering from a stroke with lingering attention, language or memory problems, it's worth asking your rehabilitation team whether combined prism-adaptation and digital cognitive training is available in your area — and noting that in this trial the benefits kept growing for months after the two-week course ended.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Rehab directors and senior living activity staff should note the delayed-benefit pattern: measuring outcomes only at discharge would have missed the memory gains entirely, which argues for re-assessing cognition at three and six months rather than closing the file when sessions end.

**Caveats:** Small trial (N=60), single research center, trial registered retrospectively, and a senior author is a shareholder in the company producing the device — a significant conflict of interest. Participant age range was not reported in the abstract.

### 7. 596 stroke rehab trials analyzed: sample sizes are growing, but study quality is flat and women are being enrolled less often

**Journal:** *Ann Phys Rehabil Med* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 18
**PMID:** 42759356 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.rehab.2026.102166

**The study:** Canadian researchers pooled three systematic reviews to analyze 596 randomized controlled trials of post-stroke cognitive rehabilitation — covering neglect, aphasia and general cognitive impairment — published through December 2024. The median trial enrolled just 36 people; only 42% of participants were female, a proportion that has declined over time; and despite steady growth in the number of trials, methodological quality scores have not improved. Outcome measures varied so widely across studies that results are difficult to compare or apply to guidelines.

**Why it matters:** Stroke disproportionately affects older adults and women live longer with its consequences, so the evidence base guiding what therapy a survivor is offered is thinner, more male-skewed and less consistent than the volume of publications implies.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** When a therapist recommends a specific cognitive rehabilitation approach after your stroke, it is fair to ask how strong the evidence is and how progress will be measured — this analysis found most supporting trials are very small and use inconsistent yardsticks, so tracking your own function over time matters.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For clinicians and program directors, the finding that female enrollment in stroke cognitive rehab trials is falling — in a condition where women carry a heavy share of long-term disability — is a concrete equity problem worth raising with research partners and funders.

**Caveats:** This is a research-landscape analysis, not a study of patients, so it says nothing directly about whether any particular therapy works. Restricted to English-language trials; pooled from three reviews with differing inclusion criteria.

### 8. Three months of online group exercise in midlife lifted mood and changed blood biology — but didn't sharpen memory

**Journal:** *NPJ Aging* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 17
**PMID:** 42754613 | **DOI:** 10.1038/s41514-026-00439-w

**The study:** The NeuroFit randomized controlled trial assigned 52 healthy middle-aged UK adults to a three-month instructor-led live online exercise program or control. The primary outcome — pattern separation, a memory task tied to the hippocampus — showed no significant benefit. However, depressive symptoms on the PHQ-9 improved significantly, and participants' blood, applied to human hippocampal progenitor cells in a laboratory assay, altered cell proliferation, suggesting exercise changes circulating factors that reach brain stem cells.

**Why it matters:** Midlife is increasingly seen as the window where later-life brain health is decided, and this trial suggests mood benefits from exercise arrive early while any cognitive payoff, if it exists, likely needs far longer than three months.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you started exercising to protect your memory and feel discouraged that nothing has changed in a few months, this trial suggests you're on a normal timeline — the mood lift comes first, and the researchers think cognitive benefits need longer exposure. A live, instructor-led online class was enough to produce measurable effects without leaving home.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For activity coordinators and family members, the standout practical detail is format: a structured, instructor-led, live online program proved feasible and produced real mood improvement, a useful model for residents or relatives who can't easily get to an in-person class.

**Caveats:** Small trial (N=52), participants were healthy middle-aged adults rather than seniors, so findings may not transfer to people over 65; short three-month follow-up; primary outcome was null; depressive symptoms were self-reported; several co-authors report food- and fitness-industry honoraria or research funding (Nestle, Yakult, Nutricia, DuPont IFF, ASICS, FitXR, Marigot).

### 9. Older walkers keep their eyes glued to the ground — and that may be part of the fall problem

**Journal:** *Gait Posture* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 16
**PMID:** 42753670 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.gaitpost.2026.110641

**The study:** Researchers systematically reviewed 53 studies that used wearable eye-trackers to record where older adults look while walking — during simple walking, walking while talking or doing a second task, stepping over obstacles, precision stepping, turning, stair climbing and navigating. Across studies, older adults made more and longer fixations on whatever was immediately in front of them and spent less time scanning the wider environment than younger adults. Differences in the timing of eye movements were mixed overall, but were most pronounced in older adults at high risk of falling and in certain patient groups.

**Why it matters:** How and where a person looks while walking is emerging as a measurable window into fall risk, and it suggests that heavily focused, ground-fixed gaze may come at the cost of spotting hazards further ahead.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you notice you have to stare at your feet or the floor just ahead to feel steady, that is a common age-related pattern — and a reason to mention your walking confidence at your next visit. Good lighting, clear uncluttered pathways, single-vision distance glasses for walking rather than multifocals on stairs, and an up-to-date eye exam all reduce how much visual work walking demands of you.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living operators and rehab staff: this review maps out which eye-tracking measures are usable in real-world walking assessments, and hints at a future where gaze behavior supplements gait speed in fall-risk screening. In the meantime, the practical takeaway is environmental — hazards placed outside a resident's narrowed visual scan (low obstacles, trailing cords, thresholds, cart wheels) are the ones most likely to be missed.

**Caveats:** Systematic review of heterogeneous studies using widely varying eye-tracking metrics and walking tasks; observational designs that cannot establish that gaze changes cause falls; laboratory settings may not generalize to everyday environments; no pooled quantitative analysis.

### 10. Why deep brain stimulation transforms some Parkinson's patients and disappoints others — the answer may be in the brain scan taken before surgery

**Journal:** *Neurosci Biobehav Rev* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 17
**PMID:** 42753347 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.neubiorev.2026.106977

**The study:** Investigators systematically reviewed 53 studies covering 1,758 patients that linked brain imaging taken before deep brain stimulation (DBS) surgery to how well the surgery worked. Most patients had movement disorders — primarily Parkinson's disease — while about one in five had psychiatric conditions, mainly major depression. Outcomes tracked with the pre-existing wiring of disease-specific circuits (the primary motor cortex, thalamus, brainstem and subthalamic nucleus in Parkinson's) plus shared higher-order networks including the salience, cerebellar and default mode networks, suggesting that individual brain organization before surgery helps determine who benefits.

**Why it matters:** DBS is a major, costly operation with famously variable results, and this work points toward a future in which imaging helps identify in advance who is most likely to gain — sparing others an invasive procedure with limited payoff.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or your neurologist are weighing DBS for Parkinson's tremor or fluctuations, it is reasonable to ask what imaging is being done beforehand and what the team knows about your likely response — while understanding that imaging-based prediction is still a research tool, not a test you can currently have done to decide.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families supporting someone through DBS evaluation: this review helps explain why two people with seemingly similar Parkinson's can have very different results, which can ease the disappointment and self-blame that follow a modest response. It also flags that patient-selection criteria may shift meaningfully over the next several years.

**Caveats:** Systematic review of observational imaging studies; the network analyses rely on normative connectivity data rather than individual patients' outcomes; findings are not yet validated for clinical patient selection; several authors report consulting and honoraria from DBS device manufacturers including Medtronic, Boston Scientific and Abbott.

### 11. Genetic testing explained only 7% of Parkinson's cases in a large clinical cohort — but the gene it kept finding matters

**Journal:** *Neurogenetics* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 19
**PMID:** 42762359 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s10048-026-00943-x

**The study:** Researchers retrospectively applied clinical exome sequencing to 137 patients diagnosed with Parkinson's disease under Movement Disorder Society criteria at a Turkish center. Using strict international classification standards, a definitive disease-causing variant was found in just 10 patients (7.3%) — nine of them in GBA1 and one a deletion in PRKN. Additional variants in genes such as LRRK2, FBXO7, PARK7 and EIF4G1 were classified as uncertain, and a previously undescribed LRRK2 duplication was detected only because the analysis looked for copy number changes that routine testing misses.

**Why it matters:** It sets realistic expectations: for most people with Parkinson's, genetic testing will not find a clear cause, but GBA1 findings are increasingly relevant to prognosis, family counseling and eligibility for gene-targeted clinical trials.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have Parkinson's and are curious about genetic testing, ask your neurologist what a result would actually change for you — most tests come back without a definitive answer, and many findings are labeled 'uncertain,' which can create anxiety without guiding treatment. Testing is most worth discussing if you have early onset, a strong family history, or interest in enrolling in a trial.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families: a GBA1 finding has implications beyond the person diagnosed, including for adult children, so genetic counseling — not just the lab report — should be part of any testing decision. Clinicians should also note the study's argument that standard testing pipelines can miss structural duplications and deletions entirely.

**Caveats:** Retrospective, single-center cohort of 137 patients with no control group; Turkish population and referral patterns may not generalize; diagnostic yield depends heavily on which genes were sequenced and how strictly variants were classified; many findings were variants of uncertain significance with no established clinical meaning.

### 12. Could the amino acids in your protein affect your mood? An early review finds a signal in older adults — and a lot of uncertainty

**Journal:** *Nutr Neurosci* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 15
**PMID:** 42740686 | **DOI:** 10.1080/1028415X.2026.2729717

**The study:** Researchers systematically reviewed 11 studies involving 3,902 participants that examined branched-chain amino acids — leucine, isoleucine and valine, found in meat, dairy, eggs and legumes — in relation to depression. Seven studies reported fewer depressive symptoms with higher dietary intake or supplementation, with improvement noted particularly in older populations and people with metabolic or chronic conditions, and studies combining exercise with supplementation suggested additional benefit. However, several studies found nothing, and the review was marked by wide variation in design, dose and how depression was measured; the authors call the evidence inconclusive.

**Why it matters:** Protein intake is already a priority for older adults because of muscle loss, and this adds a possible — though unproven — mood dimension to a conversation many seniors are already having with their clinicians and dietitians.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** This is not a reason to buy amino acid supplements, but it is a reasonable prompt to ask whether you are getting enough protein overall, especially if you eat lightly, live alone, or have lost weight — and the strongest signal in this review came when supplementation was paired with exercise, not used instead of it.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For dining directors and caregivers: the evidence is too preliminary to change menus or recommend supplements, but it strengthens the case for tracking protein adequacy in residents who are both low-appetite and low-mood, two problems that often travel together.

**Caveats:** Only 11 heterogeneous studies with inconsistent results; mix of observational and interventional designs, so causation cannot be established; wide variation in dosage, populations and depression assessment methods, many self-reported; the elderly benefit was a subgroup observation, not a pre-specified test; no safety analysis of supplementation in older adults with kidney or liver disease.

### 13. Heat waves, cold snaps and smoky air send nursing home residents to the ER — even with indoor temperature rules on the books

**Journal:** *J Am Geriatr Soc* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 18
**PMID:** 42760629 | **DOI:** 10.1111/jgs.70720

**The study:** Researchers linked 2017-2020 Medicare and Medicaid claims to daily weather and air quality data covering nearly 24.8 million nursing facility-days at 24,760 facilities in 47 states and Washington, DC, all for dual-eligible residents aged 65 and older. On days of extreme heat, emergency department visits and hospitalizations both rose; on days when fine particulate pollution (PM2.5) exceeded 9 µg/m³, ED visits and hospitalizations also rose; extreme cold days were linked to more hospitalizations. The increases occurred despite federal regulations that govern indoor temperature in nursing facilities — and there are no comparable federal standards at all for indoor air quality.

**Why it matters:** It suggests the buildings older adults live in are not fully buffering them from outdoor weather and wildfire or traffic smoke, and that air filtration is a regulatory blind spot in long-term care.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or your spouse live in a nursing facility or assisted living, you can ask directly what the building does on extreme heat days and smoky-air days: Is there central air conditioning in resident rooms as well as common areas? Are windows kept closed and filters upgraded when air quality alerts are issued? Is there a written heat and air-quality emergency plan, and who checks on residents most at risk?
- **About older adults and seniors:** For operators and directors of nursing: this is national-scale evidence that environmental days drive measurable acute care utilization, which argues for tracking local heat and PM2.5 alerts as an operational trigger — pre-positioning hydration rounds, checking HVAC and filtration capacity before summer, and documenting indoor air quality even though no federal standard requires it.

**Caveats:** Observational analysis of administrative claims linked to outdoor (not indoor) exposure data, so it cannot prove causation and cannot confirm what residents actually breathed indoors; absolute increases per 1000 resident-days are small individually, though they scale across millions of resident-days; limited to dual-eligible residents, who may differ from the broader nursing home population.

### 14. Group physical therapy on a hospital geriatrics ward: safe, popular, and it let therapists see 21% more patients

**Journal:** *J Am Geriatr Soc* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 18
**PMID:** 42760622 | **DOI:** 10.1111/jgs.70718

**The study:** At a 39-bed medical-surgical geriatrics unit in an academic medical center, therapists ran weekly group physical therapy sessions during ordinary clinical operations with no extra staffing, for medically stable patients aged 60 and older who could transfer to a chair with help. Over roughly 11 months, 37 sessions were delivered with an average of 4.2 participants each and 156 total patient participations; 69% of patients approached agreed to join, average age was 77.9 years, and no safety events occurred. Therapist productivity was about 21% higher on group days (1.59 versus 1.31 patients per hour).

**Why it matters:** Hospitalized older adults lose strength and independence fast when they stay in bed, and one-to-one therapy often can't reach everyone who needs it — a group model may extend mobility care without new hires.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're hospitalized, movement is medicine: ask your nurse or therapist how often you'll be getting out of bed, whether there's a group mobility or walking session on the unit, and whether you can join it. Being told you're 'on the PT list' is not the same as actually being walked.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For hospital rehab leaders and senior living rehab teams: this is early feasibility evidence that a once-weekly group format can be layered onto existing staffing, but the authors are explicit that patient-centered outcomes, true staff time costs and acceptability haven't been measured yet — treat it as a pilot worth replicating, not a proven intervention.

**Caveats:** Single-center quality improvement project with no control group and no patient outcome data (only feasibility, reach and productivity); small number of sessions; only relatively stable patients who could transfer to a chair were eligible, so results may not generalize to sicker or more impaired patients.

### 15. What makes 'age-friendly' hospital care actually stick — and where it keeps breaking down

**Journal:** *J Am Geriatr Soc* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 18
**PMID:** 42760752 | **DOI:** 10.1111/jgs.70723

**The study:** Researchers identified three Veterans Affairs medical centers that were unusually good at completing the Age-Friendly Health Systems '4Ms' assessment — What Matters, Medications, Mentation, and Mobility — on inpatient units, then interviewed 11 leaders and frontline staff about why it worked. Success rested on freedom to adapt the model locally, interdisciplinary teams with clear roles and genuine enthusiasm for geriatrics, leadership backing with real resources, and visible champions. The persistent stumbling block was 'What Matters': staff found it genuinely hard to elicit older patients' priorities and harder still to fold those priorities into inpatient care, alongside staffing gaps and no inpatient teams to consult for advice.

**Why it matters:** Age-friendly care frameworks are spreading through American hospitals, and this shows the element most directly about the patient's own voice is the one most likely to be skipped.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're admitted to the hospital, don't wait to be asked what matters most to you — say it out loud and ask that it go in the chart. Being able to get to the bathroom alone, get back to your own home, stay alert enough to talk with family: naming a specific goal gives the team something concrete to plan around, and this study shows they often struggle to draw it out.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For family caregivers and senior living staff coordinating a hospital stay: you can supply the 'What Matters' information the hospital team has trouble capturing — a short written note on the resident's baseline mobility, usual mental status, medications, and stated priorities, handed over at admission, addresses three of the four Ms directly.

**Caveats:** Qualitative study with only 11 interviews at three deliberately chosen high-performing VA sites; VA is an integrated system with an unusual patient population (largely male veterans), so findings may not transfer to community hospitals; no patient outcomes measured.

### 16. AI reading doctors' notes flagged mobility problems in 4 of 5 older adults — and those patients landed in the ER twice as often

**Journal:** *J Am Geriatr Soc* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 18
**PMID:** 42760637 | **DOI:** 10.1111/jgs.70732

**The study:** Mayo Clinic researchers used a large language model to pull mobility information out of free-text clinical notes from 2022 for 2,994 people aged 60 and older in the Mayo Clinic Study of Aging, then tracked what happened to them in 2023. Nearly 80% had at least one documented mobility impairment. After adjusting for age, sex, comorbidity burden and education, those with three or more impairments had more than double the odds of an emergency department visit (aOR 2.18) and hospitalization (aOR 2.35) compared with unimpaired peers; all-cause mortality was not significantly associated.

**Why it matters:** Mobility is rarely coded in medical records the way diagnoses are, but it is buried in the narrative notes — and it predicts who will end up in the hospital, which means health systems may soon be able to spot at-risk patients automatically.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** How well you walk, climb stairs, get out of a chair and move around your home is clinical information, not small talk. Mention specific changes at your next visit — 'I've started holding the wall in the hallway,' 'I can't manage the stairs without stopping' — because it now appears that what's written in your notes about mobility tracks with your risk of a hospital trip, and it opens the door to physical therapy or a home safety review.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living and home care staff: documented mobility decline in three or more domains marks a meaningfully higher-risk resident. Even without AI tools, a structured note on transfers, gait, stair use and walking distance gives clinicians the signal this model was detecting.

**Caveats:** Observational study — mobility impairment may be a marker of underlying illness rather than a cause of hospital use; single health system with a largely white Midwestern study population, limiting generalizability; the AI extraction depends on how clinicians happened to write their notes; only one year of follow-up; authors state prospective validation is needed before any clinical use.

### 17. A group exercise and coaching program cut opioid-type painkiller use — and that's why falls went down

**Journal:** *J Am Geriatr Soc* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 17
**PMID:** 42755166 | **DOI:** 10.1111/jgs.70696

**The study:** Researchers re-analyzed the BOOST randomized trial, in which 435 older adults with neurogenic claudication (leg pain and walking limits from spinal narrowing) were assigned either to best-practice advice or to a 12-week physical and psychological group program. At the start, 34% were taking at least one controlled pain medication such as an opioid. By 6 months, participants in the group program were significantly less likely to be taking controlled pain drugs (OR 0.62), and mediation analysis showed this medication reduction was a key reason the program reduced falls by 12 months.

**Why it matters:** It connects two problems older adults often face separately — back-and-leg pain treated with strong painkillers, and falls — showing that a non-drug program can reduce both, with the drug reduction doing much of the work.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If spinal stenosis pain has you leaning on opioids or other controlled painkillers, ask your clinician whether a structured physical and psychological group program is available in your area — this trial suggests it may help you cut back on those drugs, and cutting back may be what protects you from falls.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living and rehab staff: medication review and falls prevention are often run as separate initiatives. This analysis suggests group exercise programs may be a lever on both at once — and that the benefit faded between 6 and 12 months, arguing for ongoing rather than one-off programming.

**Caveats:** Secondary and mediation analysis of a trial not designed to test medication reduction; the difference in controlled medication use narrowed by 12 months; pain benefit was modest; findings apply specifically to older adults with neurogenic claudication.

### 18. 1 in 10 older ED patients return four or more times a year — and what upsets them most isn't the wait

**Journal:** *Arch Gerontol Geriatr* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 17
**PMID:** 42762629 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.archger.2026.106414

**The study:** Australian researchers linked hospital and mortality records for 336,283 emergency department presentations by adults aged 65 and older in New South Wales, following them for five years, and paired this with 47,461 patient experience surveys. About 10.6% were frequent presenters (four or more visits), most commonly people aged 75–84, with multimorbidity, rural residence, and living in aged care raising the odds. Negative care experiences were linked to waits of 60 minutes or more (OR 1.70) and complications (OR 1.63), but by far the strongest driver was not getting enough information about treatment (OR 5.17).

**Why it matters:** Repeat emergency visits among older adults track with complex, unmet health and social needs rather than trivial complaints — and the single biggest determinant of whether the visit felt bad was communication, something within any department's control.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or a relative keeps ending up in the emergency department, treat it as a signal that ongoing care needs a rethink — and before you leave, ask explicitly for an explanation of your treatment, what to watch for, and who to call, since patients who didn't get that information were five times more likely to rate the experience negatively.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For aged care operators: residence in aged care was itself associated with frequent ED use, which makes in-facility escalation pathways, after-hours clinical support, and advance care planning concrete tools for reducing transfers — and for families, being present to hear discharge instructions addresses the strongest predictor of a bad experience.

**Caveats:** Observational cohort using administrative data, so it cannot establish causation; patient experience data are self-reported and cover a subset of presentations; single Australian state, so health system context may not generalize.

### 19. Returning drained abdominal fluid doesn't extend life in advanced cancer — but it nearly doubles the time between drainage procedures

**Journal:** *J Pain Symptom Manage* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 18
**PMID:** 42759903 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jpainsymman.2026.09.017

**The study:** Using Japan's national health insurance claims database, researchers identified 52,625 people with cancer who had abdominal fluid (malignant ascites) drained in their final year of life, comparing 10,654 who received cell-free and concentrated ascites reinfusion therapy (CART, which filters and returns the patient's own proteins) with 37,272 who had standard drainage alone. Survival was essentially identical (median 31 vs. 28 days). But CART was associated with fewer repeat drainage procedures and a longer interval between them — a median of 15 days versus 8.3 — with the biggest benefit in people with liver cancer or liver metastases.

**Why it matters:** For people near the end of life with a swollen, painful abdomen, halving the frequency of an uncomfortable procedure is a meaningful quality-of-life gain even when it doesn't add days — and this reframes what to expect from the treatment.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If fluid buildup in your abdomen is being drained repeatedly, it's reasonable to ask your palliative care or oncology team whether ascites reinfusion is available and appropriate — but ask about comfort and how often you'd need the procedure, not about living longer, because this large study found no meaningful survival benefit.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For hospice and palliative teams: this supports framing CART to patients and families honestly as a burden-reduction intervention rather than a life-prolonging one, and suggests targeting it toward patients with liver-dominant disease, where the reduction in repeat drainage was largest.

**Caveats:** Retrospective claims-based analysis, so it cannot establish causation and unmeasured differences between groups (including why clinicians chose one approach) may explain results; symptom relief and quality of life were not directly measured; Japanese health system, where CART is reimbursed, may not generalize; data from 2013–2016.

### 20. A 100-day survival score for older cancer patients holds up — but the researchers warn against using it to decide on treatment

**Journal:** *J Geriatr Oncol* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 18
**PMID:** 42759253 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jgo.2026.104382

**The study:** French researchers tested the Nice Cancer Ageing Survival (NCAS) score, a tool meant to predict short-term death risk, in 543 patients aged 70 and older who underwent a geriatric assessment before cancer treatment at Gustave Roussy between 2022 and 2025. Overall, 28% died within 100 days. Survival fell steadily across score bands — from 95.5% in the lowest-risk group to 58.3% in the highest — but the score's ability to discriminate between individuals was only modest (C-index 0.67), leading the authors to conclude it should not be used alone to guide treatment decisions.

**Why it matters:** Geriatric assessment before cancer treatment is increasingly standard, and this shows that even validated scoring tools give useful group-level information but cannot reliably predict what will happen to any one person.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're over 70 and facing cancer treatment, a geriatric assessment is worth asking for — it captures function, nutrition and other factors that shape how you'll tolerate therapy — but if a numerical risk score comes up in the conversation, ask how much weight it should carry, because even its developers' validators say it isn't accurate enough to drive a treatment decision on its own.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and clinicians: the 28% 100-day mortality rate in this referred population is a reminder that pre-treatment geriatric assessment is a natural moment to open advance care planning conversations, regardless of what any risk score says.

**Caveats:** Retrospective, single-center study at a specialized French cancer center; patients referred for geriatric assessment are likely sicker than the general older cancer population, limiting generalizability; modest discriminative accuracy; one author reports fees from Ipsen, Johnson & Johnson, MSD and AstraZeneca outside this work.

### 21. A tablet-based cognitive test could rule out two-thirds of people before they ever need an Alzheimer's blood test

**Journal:** *Alzheimers Dement (Amst)* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 17
**PMID:** 42757183 | **DOI:** 10.1002/dad2.70486

**The study:** Researchers trained machine-learning models on digital cognitive assessments (the Altoida NeuroMarker) from 688 people across Australia, Spain and the United States, testing whether the digital test could work as a first-stage screen before blood testing for p-tau217, a marker of Alzheimer's pathology. The digital tool identified mild cognitive impairment with an AUC of 0.89 and elevated p-tau217 with an AUC of 0.77. In a modeled two-step workflow, it ruled out 64.4% of people with a 90.2% negative predictive value, raising the modeled accuracy of a positive blood test from 81.7% to 95.3%.

**Why it matters:** Blood tests for Alzheimer's are spreading fast into primary care, where false positives are a real risk in people with mild symptoms; a cheap digital screen first could mean fewer people get an alarming and possibly wrong result.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're worried about memory changes and hear about the new Alzheimer's blood tests, know that a positive result in someone with low pre-test likelihood is often wrong — ask your doctor what other assessment will be done before and alongside any blood test to interpret the result properly.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For memory clinics and primary care practices: the practical proposal here is triage — a short digital assessment before ordering blood biomarkers — though the second stage of the workflow was modeled rather than prospectively tested in a real clinic population.

**Caveats:** The two-step workflow was modeled under an assumed 30% prevalence of Alzheimer's pathology, not prospectively validated; heavy industry involvement — several authors are employees of Altoida Inc. (the test's maker), Johnson & Johnson and Quanterix, and may hold equity or stock options; research cohort participants may not reflect primary care populations.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**

- **Large analyses keep puncturing "of course it helps" assumptions about treating older patients.** The previous digest had *Big trial finds carotid surgery doesn't rescue thinking skills in people with symptom-free artery narrowing* (PMID 42747834) and *Three weeks of pre-surgery conditioning didn't keep frail older patients independent a year later* (PMID 42743945). The new batch adds the sharpest version yet: *For patients in their 70s, a lower-quality donor kidney may offer no survival edge over staying on dialysis* (PMID 42759976), where 75-year-olds given an expanded-criteria kidney had 59% five-year survival versus 55% on dialysis — and triple the early death rate. *Returning drained abdominal fluid doesn't extend life in advanced cancer* (PMID 42759903) lands in the same place from the palliative side.
- **Everyday function predicts hard outcomes better than test scores.** Last week's *When everyday tasks start slipping, depression may be part of what shortens life* (PMID 42753032) is now matched by *AI reading doctors' notes flagged mobility problems in 4 of 5 older adults — and those patients landed in the ER twice as often* (PMID 42760637), where three or more documented mobility impairments more than doubled the odds of an emergency visit and a hospitalization.
- **Digital delivery keeps working — modestly, specifically, and with industry fingerprints.** *Smartphone brain-training apps showed cognitive gains in nearly every trial reviewed* (PMID 42754558) and *Online support for dementia caregivers helped most those juggling a job alongside care* (PMID 42745561) are joined by *Prism glasses plus tablet games beat pencil-and-paper cognitive rehab for stroke survivors* (PMID 42759357) and *Three months of online group exercise in midlife lifted mood and changed blood biology — but didn't sharpen memory* (PMID 42754613). As with last week's retinal-age tool (PMID 42753030), the conflict-of-interest disclosures are part of the story in both new trials.
- **Mood and motivation in later life keep turning out to be physical as much as psychiatric.** Previously: *Where it hurts may predict how depression unfolds in midlife and later life* (PMID 42749164) and PMID 42753032. Now: *Methylphenidate eased depression in palliative care patients* (PMID 42762836), *Could the amino acids in your protein affect your mood?* (PMID 42740686) and *A group exercise and coaching program cut opioid-type painkiller use — and that's why falls went down* (PMID 42755166), where a non-drug program for spinal stenosis pain reduced controlled medication use, which in turn reduced falls.
- **Under-representation in the evidence base recurs.** *RSV shots look effective for older adults on immune-suppressing drugs* (PMID 42755043) flagged a population excluded from pivotal trials; *596 stroke rehab trials analyzed* (PMID 42759356) finds female enrollment at 42% and falling in a condition where women carry a heavy share of long-term disability.

**Advances**

- **Alzheimer's blood testing moves from accuracy problem to triage solution.** Last week's *A blood test for Alzheimer's proteins predicted worsening mood and behavior changes in frail older adults* (PMID 42745934) stretched plasma markers into new uses; *A tablet-based cognitive test could rule out two-thirds of people before they ever need an Alzheimer's blood test* (PMID 42757183) proposes a workflow that would head off the false positives that concerned earlier work — though its second stage was modeled, not tested in a clinic.
- **Parkinson's care gets more personalized on both ends.** After *A brain scan changed the diagnosis for one in three patients with unclear parkinsonism* (PMID 42753528) and *Botox injections sharply cut the foot pain that torments many people with Parkinson's* (PMID 42753227), the new digest asks who benefits from which intervention: *Why deep brain stimulation transforms some Parkinson's patients and disappoints others* (PMID 42753347) and *Genetic testing explained only 7% of Parkinson's cases in a large clinical cohort* (PMID 42762359).
- **Prediction tools get graded rather than celebrated.** Last week's honest null on the AST/ALT ratio (PMID 42741023) has a companion in *A 100-day survival score for older cancer patients holds up — but the researchers warn against using it to decide on treatment* (PMID 42759253), a C-index of 0.67 with the authors themselves ruling it out as a standalone decision tool.

**Counterpoints**

- **Age is not the variable — the specific offer is.** *Clot-busting drug helped stroke patients over 70 even when treatment started up to 24 hours later* (PMID 42749350) argued for doing more in older patients; PMID 42759976 argues for doing less in an equally old group. Together they push against age-based rules in either direction.
- **One partner's sleep, revisited.** Last week's pain-and-depression trajectory work (PMID 42749164) found self-reported sleep explained little; *Your own sleep, not your spouse's, tracks with your memory* (PMID 42755049) similarly deflates an assumed sleep pathway — this time the idea that a restless partner drags down your cognition.

**New themes**

- **The building and the weather as clinical variables:** *Heat waves, cold snaps and smoky air send nursing home residents to the ER — even with indoor temperature rules on the books* (PMID 42760629). Nothing comparable in the previous digest.
- **How hospital care for older adults is actually delivered:** group physical therapy on a geriatrics ward (PMID 42760622), Age-Friendly '4Ms' implementation (PMID 42760752), and frequent emergency presenters (PMID 42762629).
- **Signals hidden in routine paperwork:** *How you fill out a questionnaire may hint at dementia years before diagnosis* (PMID 42760255).
- **Infection risk in older rheumatology patients:** *Steroid-sparing drugs for giant cell arteritis and polymyalgia rheumatica don't automatically mean fewer infections* (PMID 42762860).

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Several studies this week point at the same quiet failure inside hospital care for older adults — the information that most predicts how a stay goes is information the system either never asks for or never writes down. *What makes 'age-friendly' hospital care actually stick* (PMID 42760752) found that "What Matters" — the patient's own stated priorities — was the single element staff most often skipped, while *1 in 10 older ED patients return four or more times a year* (PMID 42762629) found that not getting enough information about treatment raised the odds of a negative experience more than fivefold, far outweighing long waits. Meanwhile *AI reading doctors' notes flagged mobility problems in 4 of 5 older adults* (PMID 42760637) showed that mobility — rarely coded, usually buried in narrative notes — predicted who would be back in the emergency room, and *Group physical therapy on a hospital geriatrics ward* (PMID 42760622) showed that when someone does act on that information, existing staff can reach 21% more patients without new hires.

**Why pitch this now:** Age-friendly care frameworks are spreading through American hospitals and health systems are starting to point artificial intelligence at their own clinical notes — which makes this the moment to say plainly what the data show: the missing ingredient is often a conversation, not a technology.

**Angle:** A practical piece on how older patients and their families can put the right information into the record themselves — a short written handover at admission covering baseline mobility, usual mental status, medications and stated goals — paired with a section for hospital and senior living professionals on why the patient's-voice piece of the 4Ms keeps getting dropped.

**Potential headlines:**
- Four Things Hospitals Struggle to Learn About Older Patients — and How Families Can Supply Them
- It Wasn't the Long Wait That Made the Emergency Visit Go Badly
- Your Goals Belong in the Chart, Not Just in Your Head

**Potential outlets:**
- **Next Avenue** — its readers are the adult children doing hospital handovers, and this is exactly the kind of evidence-backed, do-this-on-Tuesday guidance it runs well.
- **Caring.com** — families moving a parent between hospital, rehab and a community need the admission-handover checklist this pitch is built around.
- **LeadingAge** — the provider side: why "What Matters" stalls on inpatient units, and what facility staff can send with a resident to close the gap.
- **Generations Journal** — a natural home for an adapted, practice-focused treatment of Age-Friendly Health Systems implementation and where it breaks down.

## Story Ideas by Study

- **How you fill out a questionnaire may hint at dementia years before diagnosis (PMID 42760255):** The forms older adults already fill out may carry an early behavioral signal — framed carefully as a research finding, not a self-test — for a consumer health explainer on early cognitive change.
- **For patients in their 70s, a lower-quality donor kidney may offer no survival edge over staying on dialysis (PMID 42759976):** The exact questions to ask when a kidney is offered — standard or expanded criteria, and what the survival difference looks like at your age — for a consumer health explainer or a kidney-disease patient publication.
- **Methylphenidate eased depression in palliative care patients — but the evidence is thin (PMID 42762836):** Depression in hospice is undertreated partly because standard antidepressants take longer than a patient has; a faster option exists but the evidence is preliminary — for a hospice and palliative-care trade brief or a caregiver-advice column.
- **Your own sleep, not your spouse's, tracks with your memory (PMID 42755049):** Reassurance for the person blaming a restless partner for their memory, plus the finding that both short and unusually long sleep tracked with lower scores — for a consumer health explainer or a couples-and-aging column.
- **Steroid-sparing drugs for giant cell arteritis and polymyalgia rheumatica don't automatically mean fewer infections (PMID 42762860):** Ask for the prednisone taper plan and timeline, because the infection advantage appears to depend on the taper actually happening — for a consumer health explainer aimed at rheumatology patients and a care-staff clinical brief.
- **Prism glasses plus tablet games beat pencil-and-paper cognitive rehab for stroke survivors (PMID 42759357):** The delayed-benefit finding is the story — memory gains appeared at three months and grew by six, so closing the file at discharge would have missed them entirely — for a stroke-recovery consumer piece and a rehab-director trade brief.
- **596 stroke rehab trials analyzed: sample sizes are growing, but study quality is flat and women are being enrolled less often (PMID 42759356):** Falling female enrollment in a condition where women live longest with the disability, and what that means for what therapy a survivor is offered — for a women's health column or a research-policy trade piece.
- **Three months of online group exercise in midlife lifted mood and changed blood biology — but didn't sharpen memory (PMID 42754613):** If you started exercising for your memory and feel discouraged, the mood lift arrives first — plus the format finding that a live, instructor-led online class produced real effects at home — for a consumer fitness-and-aging explainer or an activity-director brief.
- **Older walkers keep their eyes glued to the ground — and that may be part of the fall problem (PMID 42753670):** Hazards outside a narrowed visual scan — low obstacles, trailing cords, thresholds, cart wheels — are the ones most likely to be missed, with practical fixes on lighting, clutter and glasses for stairs — for a falls-prevention consumer piece and a senior-living operations brief.
- **Why deep brain stimulation transforms some Parkinson's patients and disappoints others (PMID 42753347):** Why two people with similar Parkinson's can have very different results — useful for easing the self-blame that follows a modest response — for a caregiver-advice column or a Parkinson's community publication.
- **Genetic testing explained only 7% of Parkinson's cases in a large clinical cohort (PMID 42762359):** Setting expectations before testing — most results are inconclusive, "uncertain" findings can cause anxiety without guiding care, and a GBA1 result has family implications that call for counseling — for a consumer health explainer.
- **Could the amino acids in your protein affect your mood? (PMID 42740686):** Not a reason to buy supplements, but a reason to check whether protein intake is adequate in someone eating lightly, living alone or losing weight — for a consumer nutrition column and a dining-services brief.
- **Heat waves, cold snaps and smoky air send nursing home residents to the ER (PMID 42760629):** The questions families can ask on a tour — air conditioning in resident rooms, filter upgrades on smoky days, a written heat and air-quality plan — plus the fact that no federal indoor air standard exists — for a consumer senior-housing piece and a provider operations/policy brief.
- **Group physical therapy on a hospital geriatrics ward: safe, popular, and it let therapists see 21% more patients (PMID 42760622):** "On the PT list" is not the same as being walked — what to ask during a hospital stay — plus an early feasibility model for rehab leaders short on staff, for a consumer hospital-stay explainer and a rehab trade brief.
- **What makes 'age-friendly' hospital care actually stick — and where it keeps breaking down (PMID 42760752):** Don't wait to be asked what matters to you; name a concrete goal and ask that it go in the chart — for a caregiver-advice column and a hospital-quality trade piece.
- **AI reading doctors' notes flagged mobility problems in 4 of 5 older adults (PMID 42760637):** How you walk, climb stairs and get out of a chair is clinical information, not small talk — say it in specifics at your next visit — for a consumer health explainer and a documentation-focused care-staff brief.
- **A group exercise and coaching program cut opioid-type painkiller use — and that's why falls went down (PMID 42755166):** Medication review and falls prevention are usually separate programs; this suggests one group program can move both, though the benefit faded between 6 and 12 months — for a consumer health explainer and a senior-living wellness trade brief.
- **1 in 10 older ED patients return four or more times a year — and what upsets them most isn't the wait (PMID 42762629):** Before you leave the emergency department, ask for the explanation, the warning signs and who to call — patients who didn't get that were five times more likely to rate the visit badly — for a consumer health explainer and an aged-care operations brief on escalation pathways.
- **Returning drained abdominal fluid doesn't extend life in advanced cancer — but it nearly doubles the time between drainage procedures (PMID 42759903):** Ask about comfort and procedure frequency, not survival — an honest reframe of a burden-reducing treatment — for a palliative-care trade brief or a caregiver column.
- **A 100-day survival score for older cancer patients holds up — but the researchers warn against using it to decide on treatment (PMID 42759253):** Geriatric assessment before cancer treatment is worth asking for; a risk number attached to it is not a decision — and this is a natural moment for advance care planning — for a consumer cancer-care explainer.
- **A tablet-based cognitive test could rule out two-thirds of people before they ever need an Alzheimer's blood test (PMID 42757183):** As Alzheimer's blood tests spread into primary care, a positive result in someone with mild symptoms is often wrong — what to ask before testing, and who built the screening tool — for a consumer health explainer and a memory-clinic trade brief.
