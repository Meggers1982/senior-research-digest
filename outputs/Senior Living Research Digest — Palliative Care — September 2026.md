### 1. More Than Half of Hospitalized Older Adults Have Unmet Palliative Care Needs—and It's Not Just Cancer Patients

**Journal:** *Aging Clinical and Experimental Research* | **Published:** June 28, 2026
**PMID:** 42366281 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40520-026-03445-w

**The study:** Italian researchers conducted a one-day snapshot across 235 hospitals and long-term care facilities, screening 5,389 patients and residents (median age 81–86 years) for palliative care needs using a validated tool. They found 58% of hospitalized patients and 48% of nursing home residents had significant palliative care needs—including high rates of pain (20–26%), delirium (19–36%), and swallowing difficulties. Crucially, nearly half of those *without* cancer still met criteria for palliative support.

**Why it matters:** This large study challenges the assumption that palliative care is primarily for cancer patients, demonstrating that older adults with heart failure, dementia, and other chronic conditions often have equally pressing comfort needs.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or a loved one is hospitalized or in a nursing home with serious chronic illness—even without cancer—you have every right to ask about palliative care services for symptom relief and support.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Facilities should implement routine screening for palliative care needs across all diagnoses, not just oncology cases, to ensure comfort-focused care reaches everyone who could benefit.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design (single time point); Italian healthcare context may not generalize to all countries.

### 2. Elective Surgeries and Treatments Common in Final Three Months of Life Among Older Adults

**Journal:** *European Geriatric Medicine* | **Published:** August 3, 2026
**PMID:** 42545407 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s41999-026-01560-5

**The study:** Dutch researchers analyzed linked health records for 104,544 adults aged 65+ who died in 2019. They found that 16% underwent elective hospital admissions in their final three months of life, with procedures including dialysis (30,087 sessions), chemotherapy/immunotherapy (12,102 treatments), radiotherapy (13,924 treatments), and even cataract surgeries. A substantial portion occurred in the final month before death.

**Why it matters:** These findings raise important questions about whether planned treatments in the final months align with patients' goals, particularly when time to experience benefit is limited.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** When facing a serious diagnosis, consider asking your doctor: "Given my overall health and prognosis, how likely is this treatment to improve my quality of life or the time I have left?"
- **About older adults and seniors:** Clinicians and families should engage in earlier conversations about goals of care to ensure elective procedures reflect what matters most to the patient, not just medical possibility.

**Caveats:** Observational design; Dutch healthcare system may differ from others; data cannot determine whether treatments aligned with patient preferences.

### 3. Family Members, Nurses, and Doctors Perceive End-of-Life Comfort Differently

**Journal:** *European Geriatric Medicine* | **Published:** July 30, 2026
**PMID:** 42530751 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s41999-026-01564-1

**The study:** Belgian researchers followed 103 dying patients on acute geriatric wards, with family members, nurses, and doctors each rating comfort daily using a validated scale. Doctors consistently rated patients as more comfortable than family members did—particularly regarding symptoms like breathing difficulties and restlessness. Family members' perceptions of comfort improved as death approached, while healthcare providers' scores remained relatively stable.

**Why it matters:** These perception gaps highlight why families may feel distressed even when medical staff believe symptoms are well-controlled, underscoring the need for ongoing communication during the dying process.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're caring for a dying loved one and feel their comfort isn't being adequately addressed, speak up—your observations are valuable and may differ meaningfully from what staff perceive.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Healthcare teams should proactively ask families about their perceptions of the patient's comfort, recognizing that clinical assessments alone may miss family distress.

**Caveats:** Two-center study in Belgium; relatively small sample of family respondents (n=42 with complete data).

### 4. Palliative Care Referrals for Adults 90+ Often Come Too Late

**Journal:** *Geriatric Nursing* | **Published:** August 11, 2026
**PMID:** 42580255 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.gerinurse.2026.104223

**The study:** A Canadian hospital reviewed 735 palliative care consultations for patients aged 90 and older over five years. Only 7% had any prior palliative care involvement before hospitalization. Most (74%) had severe functional impairment at the time of consultation, and the median time from consultation to death or discharge was just four days. Nearly two-thirds died in the acute care hospital or were transferred to a palliative unit for end-of-life care.

**Why it matters:** These findings suggest that the oldest patients are missing opportunities for earlier palliative support that could have improved comfort and helped clarify goals before a crisis.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're in your late 80s or older with serious health conditions, consider asking your doctor about palliative care now—it's not just for the final days and can help with symptoms and planning at any stage.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Hospitals and primary care providers should develop systematic approaches to identify very old adults who could benefit from earlier palliative care integration, rather than waiting for hospitalization.

**Caveats:** Single-center retrospective study; Canadian healthcare context; no control group.

### 5. Living Alone Linked to Unmet End-of-Life Support Needs

**Journal:** *Age and Ageing* | **Published:** July 2, 2026
**PMID:** 42407095 | **DOI:** 10.1093/ageing/afag202

**The study:** Researchers analyzed end-of-life interviews with proxies of 507 deceased participants (average age 83) from the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing. While most had social (82%) and psychological (78%) needs met in their final three months, those who lived alone were significantly less likely to have these needs addressed. People with dementia also faced gaps in social and psychological support.

**Why it matters:** Because non-medical end-of-life support relies heavily on family networks, older adults without nearby relatives are at risk of dying without adequate emotional or spiritual care.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you live alone, consider identifying friends, neighbors, or community resources who could provide companionship and emotional support if your health declines—don't wait until a crisis.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Healthcare systems and communities should develop formal support services for isolated older adults approaching end of life, rather than assuming families will fill this role.

**Caveats:** Proxy-reported data; English population; observational design cannot establish causation.

### 6. Virtual Reality Shows Promise for Symptom Relief in Palliative Care

**Journal:** *Psychology and Aging* | **Published:** July 23, 2026
**PMID:** 42490252 | **DOI:** 10.1037/pag0000981

**The study:** A systematic review of 54 studies examined virtual reality (VR) use in palliative care across all ages. Most studies involved adults and older adults with cancer or other life-limiting conditions. The review found VR was generally safe (adverse reactions reported in only 9% of studies) and appeared effective for managing both physical symptoms like pain and psychological distress. VR was also used successfully for caregiver support and end-of-life decision-making education.

**Why it matters:** As a drug-free option, VR could offer comfort and distraction for seriously ill patients who cannot tolerate additional medications or want alternatives to pharmacological approaches.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're dealing with pain, anxiety, or distress from serious illness, ask your care team whether virtual reality programs are available—they may offer relief without added medications.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Palliative care programs and senior living facilities should explore VR technology as a complementary tool for symptom management and emotional support.

**Caveats:** Review includes studies of varying quality; most studies were small; older adult-specific evidence is limited within the broader review.

### 7. Advance Care Planning Works—But Needs Tailoring for Diverse Communities

**Journal:** *Archives of Gerontology and Geriatrics* | **Published:** April 16, 2026
**PMID:** 42085825 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.archger.2026.106255

**The study:** Researchers synthesized 61 systematic reviews on advance care planning (ACP) interventions published over the past decade. They found that structured conversations reduce unwanted aggressive treatments and help align care with patient wishes. Video decision aids and web-based tools showed promise for patients, while conversation guides and reminder systems helped providers. However, the review noted that minority communities and those with stigmatized illnesses face additional barriers to ACP completion.

**Why it matters:** This comprehensive analysis confirms ACP's benefits while highlighting that one-size-fits-all approaches may leave some communities behind.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Advance care planning conversations can help ensure your wishes are honored—consider using video tools or asking your doctor about conversation guides to make these discussions easier.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Healthcare organizations should adapt ACP programs for cultural sensitivity and invest in community health workers or peer support to reach underserved populations.

**Caveats:** Umbrella review of existing reviews (not primary studies); heterogeneous interventions and populations across included reviews.

### 8. Nurse Practitioners in Home-Based Dementia Care Reduce ER Visits and Support Aging in Place

**Journal:** *Research in Gerontological Nursing* | **Published:** June 23, 2026
**PMID:** 42343547 | **DOI:** 10.3928/19404921-20260612-01

**The study:** A scoping review identified 10 home-based dementia care models that use nurse practitioners (NPs) for ongoing care. Across 17 included articles, these models were associated with decreased emergency department use, shorter hospital stays, delayed nursing home placement, and high rates of advance care planning completion and palliative care transitions.

**Why it matters:** For people with advancing dementia who struggle to access office-based care, NP-led home visits may help maintain quality of life while avoiding unnecessary hospitalizations.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or a family member has dementia and finds it difficult to get to doctor's appointments, ask whether home-based care programs with nurse practitioners are available in your area.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Health systems should expand NP-led home visit programs for dementia patients as a strategy to support aging in place and reduce costly, disruptive emergency care.

**Caveats:** Scoping review design; included studies varied in quality and setting; most evidence is observational.

### 9. Family Caregivers Value Palliative Care but Want Earlier, Better-Coordinated Support for Hospitalized Dementia Patients

**Journal:** *Journal of Applied Gerontology* | **Published:** November 17, 2025
**PMID:** 41247831 | **DOI:** 10.1177/07334648251389300

**The study:** Researchers interviewed 32 racially and ethnically diverse family caregivers of hospitalized dementia patients who received specialist palliative care consultations. While caregivers valued the emotional support and help with difficult decisions, they reported that care during hospitalization often felt "uncoordinated and reactive." Many expressed frustration about lack of guidance for what comes after hospital discharge.

**Why it matters:** Even when palliative care is consulted, families navigating dementia hospitalizations may feel adrift without clear transition planning and proactive communication.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If your loved one with dementia is hospitalized, don't hesitate to ask the palliative care team specifically about what happens next—what to expect at discharge and who will coordinate ongoing care.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Hospitals should integrate palliative care earlier in dementia hospitalizations and ensure discharge planning is explicit, with designated care coordinators for families.

**Caveats:** Small sample (18 completed interviews); two-hospital study; qualitative design.

### 10. Pain, Anxiety, and Worried Families: Common Palliative Needs in Skilled Nursing Facilities

**Journal:** *Journal of Applied Gerontology* | **Published:** August 17, 2025
**PMID:** 40820287 | **DOI:** 10.1177/07334648251363646

**The study:** Researchers examined baseline data from 52 older adults admitted to skilled nursing facilities (SNFs) after hospitalization, comparing those with and without dementia. The most common palliative care needs across both groups were pain (64%), anxiety (60%), family worry (58%), and unmet information needs (46%). Functional decline was the top indicator that palliative care could help.

**Why it matters:** Older adults receiving post-acute SNF care—whether or not they have dementia—frequently have significant comfort and support needs that may go unaddressed without systematic screening.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or a loved one is recovering in a skilled nursing facility, ask staff about pain management, anxiety support, and whether palliative care services are available to address comfort concerns.
- **About older adults and seniors:** SNFs should implement universal screening for palliative care needs at admission, recognizing that rehabilitation goals and comfort care are not mutually exclusive.

**Caveats:** Small sample size (N=52); single pilot study; cross-sectional design.

### 11. Poor Sleep Hygiene Independently Linked to Worse Insomnia in Older Palliative Care Patients

**Journal:** *SAGE Open Aging* | **Published:** August 28, 2026
**PMID:** 42668859 | **DOI:** 10.1177/30495334261485624

**The study:** Researchers surveyed 76 older veterans (mean age 75) receiving outpatient palliative care about sleep habits, insomnia severity, depression, and nighttime pain. After adjusting for other factors, poorer sleep hygiene was independently associated with worse insomnia—as were higher depression scores and nighttime pain.

**Why it matters:** While pain and depression often dominate palliative care discussions, this study suggests that practical sleep habits may also be a modifiable target for improving rest.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're struggling with sleep, consider whether habits like irregular bedtimes, screen use, or caffeine might be contributing—addressing these alongside pain and mood may help.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Palliative care teams should routinely assess and counsel patients on sleep hygiene as part of comprehensive symptom management, not just medication adjustment.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design (cannot prove causation); veteran population (predominantly male); relatively small sample.

### 12. Telehealth Palliative Care Rated Comparable to In-Person Visits by Many Patients and Families

**Journal:** *Journal of Palliative Medicine* | **Published:** August 27, 2026
**PMID:** 42661449 | **DOI:** 10.1177/10966218261484819

**The study:** Researchers interviewed 26 patients and family caregivers receiving outpatient palliative care at an academic medical center about their experiences with telehealth versus in-person visits. While some felt in-person visits allowed more authentic connection, many found telehealth communication comparable—and the convenience often outweighed perceived differences. Some patients noted they would not have accessed palliative care at all without the telehealth option.

**Why it matters:** For patients with serious illness, the burden of travel can be substantial; this study suggests telehealth can maintain meaningful care relationships while improving access.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If getting to palliative care appointments is difficult due to symptoms or distance, ask about video visits—many patients find them just as helpful for conversations about goals and symptoms.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Palliative care programs should maintain hybrid in-person and telehealth options, allowing patients to choose based on their circumstances and preferences.

**Caveats:** Single-center study; qualitative design; may not reflect all patient populations or technology comfort levels.

### 13. Functional Ability at Admission Predicts Survival and Discharge Home from Palliative Care Units

**Journal:** *BMJ Supportive & Palliative Care* | **Published:** August 27, 2026
**PMID:** 42660629 | **DOI:** 10.1136/spcare-2026-006303

**The study:** Japanese researchers studied 224 terminal cancer patients admitted to a palliative care unit, measuring their functional independence at admission. Patients with higher functional scores lived significantly longer (median 40 vs. 15 days) and were more likely to be discharged home (62% vs. 28%). A total functional score of 87 or above (out of 126) best predicted the possibility of going home.

**Why it matters:** Objective measurement of daily functioning can help palliative care teams and families set realistic goals and have informed conversations about what's achievable.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Understanding your current functional abilities can help your care team set appropriate goals—ask how assessments of daily activities factor into your care plan and discharge planning.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Palliative care units should routinely use standardized functional assessments at admission to guide rehabilitation goals and family discussions about prognosis.

**Caveats:** Single-center study in Japan; cancer patients only; retrospective design.

### 14. Cancer Cachexia Present in Over 90% of Patients with Advanced Disease, Contributing to Most Deaths

**Journal:** *BMJ Supportive & Palliative Care* | **Published:** August 31, 2026
**PMID:** 42674857 | **DOI:** 10.1136/spcare-2026-006305

**The study:** British researchers reviewed 513 cancer-related deaths over three years at a specialist palliative care unit, applying four different definitions of cachexia (severe muscle and weight loss). Depending on the definition used, 68–92% of patients met cachexia criteria. The researchers estimated cachexia contributed to 70% of deaths, often through infection, blood clots, or heart failure.

**Why it matters:** Cachexia is often overlooked as a distinct condition requiring its own management approach; this study highlights how prevalent and consequential it is in advanced cancer.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're living with advanced cancer and experiencing significant weight or muscle loss, ask your team about cachexia—it's a recognized condition with specific management strategies beyond standard cancer treatment.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Oncology and palliative care teams should proactively screen for and address cachexia, recognizing it as a major factor in how patients feel and how long they live.

**Caveats:** Single palliative care center; retrospective design; UK population.

### 15. Community Health Worker-Led Intervention Helps Pancreatic Cancer Patients Complete Advance Care Planning

**Journal:** *Journal of Palliative Medicine* | **Published:** September 1, 2026
**PMID:** 42681826 | **DOI:** 10.1177/10966218261485724

**The study:** Researchers piloted a community health worker (CHW)-led intervention for 48 newly diagnosed pancreatic cancer patients, using digital tools and stepped palliative care support. The intervention was well-accepted (94% strongly agreed it was acceptable), with patients particularly valuing CHW support as symptoms worsened. Physical quality of life and symptom burden improved from baseline, and 83% completed advance care planning.

**Why it matters:** With palliative care specialist shortages, CHW-led models may help bridge gaps and ensure more patients with aggressive cancers receive timely support.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're facing a serious cancer diagnosis, ask whether your care team includes community health workers or patient navigators who can help coordinate support and planning.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Cancer centers should explore task-shifting models that train community health workers to extend palliative care reach, particularly for patients with rapidly progressing diseases.

**Caveats:** Single-arm pilot study (no control group); small sample; single institution.

### 16. Multiple Long-Term Conditions at End of Life: Professionals Call for Clearer Coordination and Earlier Planning

**Journal:** *Palliative Medicine* | **Published:** August 28, 2026
**PMID:** 42663268 | **DOI:** 10.1177/02692163261468154

**The study:** Researchers interviewed 27 healthcare professionals and decision-makers across England about palliative care for people dying with multiple chronic conditions. Three themes emerged: cumulative complexity that single-disease services don't address; crisis-driven, reactive care with little anticipatory planning; and unclear responsibility for coordination across service boundaries.

**Why it matters:** Most people die with multiple conditions, yet services remain organized around single diseases, creating gaps that leave patients and families scrambling during emergencies.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have several chronic conditions, consider asking your primary care provider who is responsible for coordinating your overall care—especially if your health is declining.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Health systems need designated coordination roles and proactive review processes for patients with multiple conditions, rather than waiting for crises to trigger palliative involvement.

**Caveats:** Qualitative design; English healthcare context; professional perspectives only (not patient/family views).

### 17. Nurses Identify Key Strategies for Better Pain Management in Dementia Care

**Journal:** *Geriatric Nursing* | **Published:** July 6, 2026
**PMID:** 42407320 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.gerinurse.2026.104153

**The study:** Researchers conducted eight group interviews with 28 nurses caring for people with dementia in Norwegian nursing homes, using a structured technique to generate and rank ideas for improving pain management. The top priorities were systematic pain assessment when pain is clearly expressed and ensuring staff have appropriate competencies. Nurses emphasized that comprehensive, person-centered approaches addressing multiple dimensions simultaneously are needed.

**Why it matters:** Pain in dementia is often undertreated because it can be hard to recognize; this study offers practical strategies from frontline caregivers.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If your loved one with dementia seems uncomfortable or agitated, advocate for systematic pain assessment—changes in behavior may signal pain that's not being expressed in words.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Nursing homes should invest in staff training for dementia pain assessment and implement structured approaches that recognize pain expression may look different in this population.

**Caveats:** Norwegian healthcare context; qualitative design; nurse perspectives only.

### 18. Hearing Loss Often Overlooked in Healthcare—Patients Forced to Constantly Self-Advocate

**Journal:** *Geriatric Nursing* | **Published:** May 22, 2026
**PMID:** 42167081 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.gerinurse.2026.104083

**The study:** Researchers interviewed 15 older adults with hearing loss and chronic illness, along with their partners, using a grounded theory approach. A central finding was the exhausting need for constant self-advocacy—patients repeatedly had to inform healthcare providers about their hearing loss because they were never asked. Participants described fear of missing critical health information and frustration with lack of accommodations.

**Why it matters:** As both hearing loss and chronic illness increase with age, routine conversations about care preferences may be compromised if hearing accommodations aren't standard practice.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Don't hesitate to remind your healthcare providers about your hearing needs at every visit—and advocate for systemic changes so you shouldn't have to.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Healthcare systems should implement automatic prompts for hearing accommodations in electronic records and train staff to proactively address communication needs.

**Caveats:** Small pilot study (N=15); qualitative design; findings may not generalize to all settings.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**

Multiple studies appear in both digests, reinforcing core messages:

- *Palliative care needs extend beyond cancer* — The Italian NECPAL prevalence study (PMID 42366281) appears again, confirming that most hospitalized older adults and nursing home residents with palliative needs do not have cancer.
- *Elective treatments continue near death* — The Dutch end-of-life procedure analysis (PMID 42545407) is repeated, showing 16% of older adults had elective hospital admissions in their final three months.
- *Families and clinicians perceive dying differently* — The Belgian comfort-rating study (PMID 42530751) reappears, with doctors consistently rating patients as more comfortable than family members did.
- *Palliative referrals for the oldest-old come late* — The Canadian 90+ referral study (PMID 42580255) returns, showing median time from consultation to death was just four days.
- *Living alone predicts unmet end-of-life support* — The English Longitudinal Study of Ageing analysis (PMID 42407095) appears in both digests with consistent findings.
- *Virtual reality shows promise in palliative settings* — The systematic review (PMID 42490252) is covered again, finding VR safe and potentially effective for symptom relief.
- *Advance care planning works but needs adaptation* — The umbrella review (PMID 42085825) from the NEW digest echoes the PREVIOUS digest's emphasis on ACP benefits while noting barriers for minority communities.
- *NP-led home dementia care reduces acute utilization* — The scoping review (PMID 42343547) reappears with consistent findings on emergency visit reductions and advance care planning completion.
- *Pain, anxiety, and family worry are common SNF palliative needs* — The baseline pilot data (PMID 40820287) appears in both runs, confirming high needs regardless of dementia status.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Several studies in this batch point to a coordination crisis in palliative care — not just late referrals, but fragmented handoffs and unclear responsibility once palliative care is involved. The family caregiver interviews on dementia hospitalizations (PMID 41247831) describe care as "uncoordinated and reactive" with poor discharge guidance. The professional interviews across England (PMID 42663268) identify "cumulative complexity" from multiple chronic conditions that single-disease services cannot address, with no one owning coordination. The SNF baseline data (PMID 40820287) show high unmet needs including family worry (58%) and information gaps (46%). And the Belgian comfort-rating study (PMID 42530751) demonstrates families and clinicians perceive dying differently — a gap that structured communication could close but often doesn't.

**Why pitch this now:** Most coverage of palliative care focuses on access and timing — getting people referred earlier. But this batch reveals that referral alone doesn't solve the problem when coordination breaks down afterward. Families report feeling adrift, professionals describe unclear responsibility, and perception gaps persist even in specialized settings. A feature exploring what happens *after* the palliative care consult could fill a gap in public understanding.

**Angle:** A feature examining the "coordination gap" in palliative care — why families feel lost even after specialists are involved, and what integrated models (designated coordinators, structured family check-ins, explicit discharge planning) could look like. Framed for family caregivers navigating serious illness, with practical questions to ask.

**Potential headlines:**
- The Palliative Care Consult Came — So Why Does My Family Still Feel Lost?
- After the Referral: The Hidden Coordination Crisis in End-of-Life Care
- Who's in Charge? When Palliative Care Gets Consulted but No One Owns the Plan

- **Being Patient** — Covers dementia caregiving with a practical, solution-oriented tone; the coordination angle applies directly to families managing hospitalizations and transitions.
