### 1. A Computer Model Can Flag Which New Nursing Home Residents Are Most at Risk of Dying Within Six Months

**Journal:** *JMIR Aging* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 16
**PMID:** 42748398 | **DOI:** 10.2196/94567

**The study:** Researchers used routine intake assessment records from 636 long-term care facilities in Taiwan to build a machine learning model predicting death within 180 days of admission, training it on 23,901 first-time residents admitted from 2020 to 2023 and then testing it on a separate group of 6,216 residents admitted in 2024. Roughly 22% of the development group and 29% of the validation group died within six months. The best model distinguished those who died from those who survived with high accuracy (area under the curve 0.90), and the most influential warning signs were frequent hospitalizations in the prior six months, difficulty with activities of daily living, and weight loss.

**Why it matters:** Early death after moving into long-term care is common but often unanticipated, and a tool that uses information already collected at admission could prompt earlier conversations about goals of care, advance directives, and palliative care referral instead of leaving families blindsided.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or your spouse is moving into a long-term care facility, this is a reasonable moment to put your wishes in writing. Ask the facility how they identify residents who would benefit from advance care planning or a palliative care assessment, and mention specifically if you have had repeat hospitalizations, unintended weight loss, or new difficulty with dressing, bathing, or walking — those were the strongest signals in this study.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For facility leaders and clinical directors, this shows risk stratification is achievable with intake data you already gather, no hospital records linkage required — but the authors themselves note no one has yet tested whether flagging residents actually changes care or family experience. The practical question for administrators is what workflow follows a high-risk flag: who has the conversation, when, and with what training.

**Caveats:** Retrospective and observational — the model predicts, it does not explain or establish cause. Conducted entirely in Taiwanese facilities using a private administrative registry, so performance in US or European settings is unproven. Notable industry entanglement: one author is a paid consultant for Jubo Health Technologies and another is a full-time Jubo employee; Jubo was contracted with grant funds for technical implementation and data extraction, though the authors state the company had no role in design, analysis, or the decision to publish. Mild miscalibration at the highest predicted risk levels, and no prospective implementation data.

### 2. What Actually Counts as Success When Palliative Care Comes Into a Nursing Home? Staff Say the Usual Metrics Miss Most of It

**Journal:** *Gerontologist* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 12
**PMID:** 42742117 | **DOI:** 10.1093/geront/gnag220

**The study:** Researchers interviewed 33 key participants — nursing home staff, palliative care consultants, nursing home directors, and palliative care agency directors — across 14 nursing homes in the eastern United States about what makes a palliative care consultation worthwhile. Rather than the hospitalization and utilization numbers health systems typically track, participants described meaningful results in four interlocking domains: residents (care matching their stated goals, symptom relief, understanding their choices), families (shared decision-making, understanding the disease trajectory, feeling supported), staff (greater capacity to deliver quality care, plus emotional support), and the facility itself (filling care gaps, reputation, and staff morale).

**Why it matters:** How palliative care gets measured determines whether it gets funded and expanded, and this work suggests current evaluation frameworks capture only a fraction of its value — particularly the family support and staff-sustaining effects that people closest to the work consider central.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you live in a nursing home or are considering one, you can ask directly whether the facility has a palliative care consultant and what that consultation involves. Based on what the professionals in this study described, a good consultation should leave you clearer about your options, better on symptoms like pain and breathlessness, and confident that the care plan reflects what you actually want — not just fewer trips to the hospital.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families, this is a reminder that palliative care consultation is partly for you: participants named shared decision-making, understanding what to expect as the illness progresses, and simply feeling supported as core outcomes. For nursing home administrators, the findings make an unusual business case — staff morale and facility reputation were described as real returns on palliative care partnerships, alongside the clinical benefits.

**Caveats:** Qualitative study with a small sample (N=33) drawn from 14 facilities in one US region, so views may not generalize nationally. Reflects the perspectives of staff, consultants, and directors — residents and family members were not interviewed directly, a limitation the authors flag themselves. No measurement of whether these outcomes actually improve after consultation.

### 3. Cancer Patients Preferred AI-Written Words of Comfort Over Those From Palliative Care Doctors Nearly Three Times Out of Four

**Journal:** *J Pain Symptom Manage* | **Published:** 2026 Oct
**PMID:** 42599269 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jpainsymman.2026.05.011

**The study:** In a secondary analysis of the randomized PALL-AI trial, 105 cancer patients read blinded pairs of empathic statements responding to fear and anxiety about cancer treatment — some written by palliative care physicians, some generated by AI, all working from the same prompt and length limit. Patients chose the AI-generated statement 72.4% of the time. Language analysis found all three AI statements registered positive sentiment while all three physician statements registered negative sentiment, with AI using roughly twice as many positive words; the emotional tones most strongly linked to patient preference were "trust" and "joy."

**Why it matters:** As health systems begin drafting patient messages with AI, this suggests tone may matter more than patients or clinicians assume — while also raising the uncomfortable question of whether a more upbeat message is actually a more honest or useful one in serious illness.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you are living with cancer or another serious illness, it is worth knowing that some messages from your care team may be AI-drafted, and you can ask whether that is the case. This study also hints at something you can use: if a clinician's words land as cold or discouraging, say so — patients in this study responded strongly to language that conveyed warmth and trust, and you are entitled to ask for a conversation, not just a summary.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For clinicians and care staff, the finding is less about AI's superiority than about what patients respond to: more positive-valence language, without necessarily removing the hard news. Worth discussing in team training — and worth noting that preference for a written statement is not the same as benefit from a real conversation.

**Caveats:** Small sample (N=105), single health system (Mayo Clinic referrals), and only three statement pairs tested. Patients rated short written statements in a survey, not real clinical encounters, and preference was the outcome — not comprehension, trust over time, or any health result. Secondary analysis of an existing trial. No external funding reported.

### 4. A Tape Measure Around the Calf, Combined With Two Simple Lab Values, Helps Predict Survival in Advanced Cancer

**Journal:** *Clin Nutr ESPEN* | **Published:** 2026 Oct
**PMID:** 42674364 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.clnesp.2026.105053

**The study:** Brazilian researchers followed 370 adult outpatients with incurable cancer at a specialized palliative care unit between June 2021 and February 2025, measuring calf circumference (a low-cost proxy for muscle mass) alongside nutritional and inflammatory markers. Low calf circumference alone was independently linked to a 58% higher risk of dying within 90 days, but its predictive power was weak on its own. Combining it with recent weight loss, low albumin, or elevated C-reactive protein roughly tripled the associated mortality risk and meaningfully improved prediction accuracy.

**Why it matters:** Prognosis in advanced cancer drives decisions about treatment intensity, hospice timing, and nutrition support, and this points to a prediction strategy that needs only a tape measure and standard blood tests — usable in clinics without advanced body composition equipment.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you are receiving palliative care for advanced cancer, muscle loss and unintended weight loss are not just cosmetic — they carry real prognostic weight. It is reasonable to ask your team whether they are tracking your muscle mass, weight trend, and inflammatory markers, and what those numbers mean for decisions you may want to make about treatment and planning. Do not read a single measurement as a verdict; these tools describe groups, not individuals.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For clinicians and care staff in resource-limited settings, this supports building calf circumference into routine assessment rather than treating it as a standalone screen — the signal comes from combining it with weight loss history and inflammation. For families, understand these are statistical tools that inform planning conversations, not predictions about a specific person's timeline.

**Caveats:** Single-center, observational cohort at one Brazilian cancer institute — cannot establish causation, and the population (palliative care outpatients with incurable cancer) may not generalize to other groups. Predictive accuracy remained modest even in the best combinations (area under the curve around 0.63). Short outcome window of 90 days.

### 5. Pharmacy Technicians Visiting Housebound Seniors at Home Found Dozens of Missed Checkups and Cut Emergency Contacts by More Than Half

**Journal:** *Int J Clin Pharm* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 12
**PMID:** 42730948 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11096-026-02211-2

**The study:** Two general practices in a deprived area of Glasgow tested home visits by pharmacy technicians, supervised by a clinical pharmacist, for 25 housebound adults aged 60 to 89 (median age 81) who had at least two long-term conditions and five or more regular medications. Participants were taking a median of 14 regular medications, and 23 of them had collectively missed 91 long-term condition reviews since becoming housebound. Across 83 visits, 21 medications were stopped, 19 started and 15 adjusted; over three months, self-reported falls fell from 8 to zero, unscheduled care contacts dropped from 56 to 21, and quality-of-life scores rose modestly.

**Why it matters:** Older adults who cannot get to the clinic quietly fall out of routine medication and condition reviews, and this suggests a relatively low-cost workforce — pharmacy technicians going into the home — can surface a large backlog of unmet need.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you are largely housebound and taking multiple medications, you may be overdue for reviews you do not know you have missed. It is worth asking your practice whether a pharmacist or pharmacy technician can come to your home to go through your medicines, and whether any of them can safely be stopped — the people in this study were on a median of 14 drugs, and some were discontinued.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For family caregivers, keep a running list of which annual condition reviews have actually happened, because housebound status made these slip for nearly everyone in the study. For commissioners and primary care leaders, this is a feasibility signal for deploying technician-led domiciliary review in deprived areas — but note that people receiving palliative care were explicitly excluded, so the model has not been tested in that group.

**Caveats:** Very small (N=25), two practices only, pre-post design with no control group, and just three months of follow-up — improvements could reflect regression to the mean or other changes. Falls were self-reported. Conducted as a service evaluation within NHS Greater Glasgow and Clyde; findings may not generalize outside the UK primary care context. Cannot establish causation.

### 6. Written Wishes Do Cut Hospital Trips at the End of Life — But Only If Staff Follow Them

**Journal:** *J Hosp Palliat Nurs* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 1;28(5):E217-E224
**PMID:** 42714187 | **DOI:** 10.1097/NJH.0000000000001217

**The study:** Australian researchers conducted a scoping review of 23 studies published between 2004 and 2024 across nine countries, looking at why residents of aged care facilities get sent to hospital in their final months and what role advance care planning plays. Roughly 70% of aged care residents are transferred to a hospital during the last few months of life. Advance care plans and directives that contained specific instructions — such as do-not-resuscitate or do-not-hospitalize orders — were associated with fewer transfers than plans without them. The reviewers also found troubling reports of residents being sent to hospital against the wishes documented in their own plans.

**Why it matters:** A hospital transfer in the last weeks of life is disruptive, often distressing, and frequently unwanted — and this review suggests that the specificity of what you write down, and whether staff actually act on it, determines whether your wishes hold.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have an advance care plan, consider whether it says what you actually want in concrete terms. Vague statements about "comfort" leave room for interpretation; explicit instructions about resuscitation and hospitalization were the ones linked to fewer transfers. Ask your facility where your document is stored, who can find it at 2 a.m., and whether the after-hours or locum doctor will see it.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and aged care staff: the review found transfers happening against documented wishes, often driven by acuity, perceived vulnerability, or family pressure in a crisis. Facilities can audit how often a resident's plan is actually retrieved and consulted before a 000/911 call, and families can decide in advance who will speak for the resident so a panicked late-night decision doesn't override years of planning.

**Caveats:** Scoping review of heterogeneous studies, mostly observational, so associations between documented instructions and fewer transfers cannot establish causation; studies span 20 years and nine countries with differing aged care and emergency systems.

### 7. Palliative Care Doctors Are Warming to AI Prognosis Tools — While Admitting They Barely Understand Them

**Journal:** *J Pain Symptom Manage* | **Published:** 2026 Oct;72(4):e340-e347
**PMID:** 42463061 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jpainsymman.2026.07.005

**The study:** Researchers at the University of Colorado surveyed 2,500 US hospice and palliative medicine physicians between January 2024 and March 2025, receiving 537 completed responses. Nearly all respondents reported low knowledge of AI-based prognostication (91.8%), and only 24% had ever used an AI-generated prognosis, yet attitudes were broadly positive: 70.9% believed AI could bring patients to palliative care earlier, 78% thought it could help patients plan for the end of life, and 62.8% supported a role in hospice eligibility decisions. Meanwhile, 36.3% worried about legal liability and 37.5% feared such tools could damage patients' sense of hope.

**Why it matters:** Machine learning models that estimate how long someone has to live are already being built into electronic health records, and they may soon shape when you're offered a palliative care referral or judged eligible for hospice.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If a clinician gives you a prognosis, it's fair to ask how it was arrived at — clinical judgment, a statistical model, or a tool built into the medical record. You can also say how much detail you want. More than a third of these doctors worried these tools could undercut patients' hope, so telling your team how you prefer to receive difficult numbers is a reasonable request.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and senior living professionals: expect prognostic algorithms to increasingly influence referral timing and hospice eligibility conversations. Worth asking hospital and hospice partners whether they use such tools, who reviews the output before it reaches a family, and how disagreements between the model and the clinical team are resolved.

**Caveats:** Survey with a roughly 21% response rate, raising the possibility that respondents were more technology-interested than the field overall; respondents were predominantly White (73.2%); self-reported attitudes, not actual behavior; US-only, so findings may not generalize.

### 8. An IV Drug Hoped to Relieve Cancer Pain That Opioids Couldn't Touch Failed to Beat Placebo

**Journal:** *J Palliat Care* | **Published:** 2026 Oct;41(4):424-431
**PMID:** 42536632 | **DOI:** 10.1177/08258597261461797

**The study:** French investigators ran a multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in palliative care units testing 48 hours of continuous low-dose intravenous lidocaine against placebo in patients whose cancer pain was not responding to opioids. Thirty-five patients were analyzed (18 lidocaine, 17 placebo). There was no significant difference between groups on the primary pain endpoint or on secondary pain outcomes — pain fell in both arms — although lidocaine was well tolerated with mostly mild side effects. The trial did not reach its planned enrollment and was therefore underpowered.

**Why it matters:** Intravenous lidocaine is sometimes offered when opioids fail to control cancer pain; this trial provides no evidence it helps more than placebo, and it highlights how thin the rigorous evidence base is for many palliative pain treatments.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If your cancer pain isn't controlled by opioids and an intravenous lidocaine infusion is proposed, it's reasonable to ask what the evidence is, what the goal is, and how long the team will try it before reassessing. The reassuring news is that it appeared safe; the sobering news is that in this trial it worked no better than a placebo drip.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and clinicians: the meaningful pain reduction seen in the placebo group is a reminder of how much attentive monitoring, being in a palliative care unit, and expectation can contribute to symptom relief — and of how hard it is to recruit enough seriously ill patients to answer these questions definitively.

**Caveats:** Small sample (N=35), underpowered because planned enrollment was not reached, so a real benefit cannot be firmly ruled out; very short 48-hour intervention and follow-up; conducted in specialist French palliative care units, which may not generalize to other settings.

### 9. Grief After a Child's Cancer Death Can Last Decades — And Poverty Makes It Worse

**Journal:** *Palliat Med* | **Published:** 2026 Oct;40(9):1408-1419
**PMID:** 42603866 | **DOI:** 10.1177/02692163261465772

**The study:** Swiss researchers surveyed 103 parents of 81 children who had died of cancer at least a year earlier, recruited through the Swiss Childhood Cancer Registry and three pediatric oncology centers. Using the PG-13 grief measure, they found average grief intensity of 23.3 out of 55, with yearning the most prominent symptom. Latent profile analysis identified three groups: low grief (56%), moderate (32%), and high grief (12%). High poverty risk and the child having died in a healthcare facility rather than at home were both linked to more intense grief, while more time since the death was linked to less.

**Why it matters:** Bereavement support typically fades within months, yet a substantial minority of parents carry severe grief symptoms for many years — a reality that matters for older adults who lost a child decades ago and for the professionals who serve them.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you are living with grief from a child's death years or even decades ago, this research confirms you are not unusual and not failing to "move on" — about one in eight bereaved parents in this study still had high-intensity grief. Persistent yearning that interferes with daily life is treatable, and it's worth raising with your doctor rather than assuming too much time has passed.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For clinicians and senior living staff: financial hardship was the strongest predictor of severe long-term grief, suggesting bereavement risk screening should include economic circumstances. Where a death occurred also mattered, which strengthens the case for anticipatory guidance and home-death support during palliative care rather than only after the loss.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design cannot establish causation or track individuals over time; only 103 of 388 eligible parents participated, raising selection bias concerns; single-country Swiss sample; self-reported grief measures; the apparent decline in grief with time may reflect differences between cohorts rather than true recovery.

### 10. Adding a Palliative Care Nurse Practitioner to an MS Clinic Uncovered Symptoms Nobody Was Asking About

**Journal:** *J Hosp Palliat Nurs* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 1;28(5):230-236
**PMID:** 42714185 | **DOI:** 10.1097/NJH.0000000000001230

**The study:** A New York multiple sclerosis center embedded a nurse practitioner-led palliative care clinic within its existing neurology practice and evaluated the first three months. Twenty-six patients were referred; the average number of symptoms identified per patient jumped from 1.8 before the intervention to 6.7 afterward. Seventeen patients (85% of those assessed) had a documented serious illness discussion, compared with none beforehand, and nurse practitioner productivity rose 67%.

**Why it matters:** Palliative care is still widely assumed to be for cancer or the final weeks of life, but people with progressive neurological disease carry heavy symptom burdens for years — and this project suggests many of those symptoms simply go unrecorded unless someone is assigned to ask.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you live with multiple sclerosis, Parkinson's, or another progressive neurological condition, you can ask your neurology team whether palliative or supportive care is available alongside your regular treatment. It is not the same as hospice, and the point is symptom relief and planning conversations — patients in this project had nearly four times as many symptoms identified once someone specifically looked.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and care organizations: this is a model worth raising with specialty clinics — embedding a palliative-trained nurse practitioner within an existing center increased uptake without requiring patients to accept an outside "palliative care" referral, which many resist. The productivity gain also suggests the model can be financially viable.

**Caveats:** Very small feasibility project (N=26 referred), single center, no control group, only three months of follow-up, and the sharp rise in documented symptoms partly reflects more thorough documentation rather than proven improvement in patients' well-being.

### 11. Propofol for Sedation at the End of Life: Promising in Hard Cases, but the Evidence Is Thin

**Journal:** *Palliat Med* | **Published:** 2026 Oct
**PMID:** 42533745 | **DOI:** 10.1177/02692163261465774

**The study:** Researchers at the University of Lisbon systematically reviewed 10 non-randomized studies published between 2005 and 2024 covering 4,072 adult patients with terminal illness — mostly cancer — of whom about 28% received palliative sedation. Propofol, a fast-acting intravenous anesthetic, controlled refractory symptoms in 73%–100% of cases and reached the intended depth of sedation in 52%–100%, used either alone or alongside other sedatives. Respiratory depression was the main safety concern, and the reviewers rated the overall certainty of evidence as low to very low. They also flagged ethical gaps, including instances where explicit consent was not documented.

**Why it matters:** Palliative sedation is reserved for suffering that cannot be relieved any other way at the very end of life, and families often face this decision with little information; this review shows propofol can work but should be handled by experienced teams under clear institutional protocols.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you are thinking ahead about how you want severe, unrelievable symptoms handled at the end of life, palliative sedation is a topic worth raising now with your doctor and naming in your advance directive — you can ask which medications your hospital or hospice uses, who monitors you, and how your consent will be documented.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For hospice and senior living clinicians, the review's most actionable finding is not the drug itself but the documentation gap: consent for palliative sedation was often absent or implicit, which argues for written institutional protocols and explicit family conversations before sedation begins.

**Caveats:** Systematic review of only 10 non-randomized studies (observational, cohort, case series, before-and-after designs) of mostly moderate quality; certainty of evidence rated low to very low; outcome definitions varied widely across studies; most studies conducted in Europe and in cancer patients, which may limit generalizability.

### 12. Where People Die Is Poorly Recorded Worldwide — And That Distorts Planning for Hospice and Nursing Home Care

**Journal:** *Palliat Med* | **Published:** 2026 Oct
**PMID:** 42544535 | **DOI:** 10.1177/02692163261468152

**The study:** Researchers surveyed 16 international experts who had collectively analyzed death certificate data from 67 countries, most for more than a decade, to assess how well national systems record where people die. While most classification systems were stable and included a "home" category, researchers consistently reported missing or ambiguous categories for settings that matter most to older adults — hospices and nursing homes — along with inconsistent terminology across countries. The authors call for a standardized international classification aligned with UN and WHO recommendations and better training for clinicians completing death certificates.

**Why it matters:** Governments and health systems use place-of-death data to decide where to fund hospice beds, home care teams, and nursing home palliative services — if deaths in care homes and hospices are invisible in the data, those services are under-counted and under-resourced.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Most people say they want to die at home or somewhere familiar rather than in a hospital — it is worth stating your preference in writing now, because national statistics that guide service funding may not be capturing the settings where older adults actually die.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living and nursing home administrators have a direct stake here: if your residents' deaths are being coded simply as "other" or lumped in with hospital deaths, the case for funding on-site palliative capacity gets harder to make. Accurate death certificate completion is a low-cost advocacy tool.

**Caveats:** Small sample (16 researchers surveyed), cross-sectional survey of expert opinion rather than an audit of the data systems themselves, self-reported perspectives, and data coverage ended in 2022; one author declares an advisory role with the La Caixa Foundation Programme of Comprehensive Care for People with Advanced Diseases.

### 13. China Built a Palliative Care Policy Framework in 35 Years — Now Comes the Harder Part

**Journal:** *Palliat Med* | **Published:** 2026 Oct
**PMID:** 42592757 | **DOI:** 10.1177/02692163261464373

**The study:** Researchers analyzed the full body of palliative care policy documents issued in mainland China from 1991 to 2023, retrieved from legal databases and government websites, using network analysis and text mining to map how policy priorities and the agencies behind them evolved. They identified five distinct periods, with development accelerating sharply after 2016, and a shift from single-department regulation to multi-sector governance led by the National Health Commission. Policy language increasingly emphasized an institution-community-home continuum of care, but persistent gaps remain in dedicated legislation, sustainable funding, clinician training, and digital infrastructure.

**Why it matters:** China is home to one of the world's largest and fastest-growing populations of older adults, and how it funds and organizes end-of-life care will shape the experience of hundreds of millions of families — and offers a case study for other rapidly aging countries.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or your family have roots in China or are planning care across borders, this analysis shows palliative services there are expanding fast on paper but access remains uneven — worth asking specifically what hospice and home-based support exists in a given city or province rather than assuming national policy has reached it.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior care professionals and policymakers, the takeaway is the sequence: China built the framework first and is still working on funding, workforce education, and equitable access. Framework-building is the easy part, and that lesson travels.

**Caveats:** Policy document analysis rather than a study of patient outcomes — it tells you what governments wrote, not what care people actually received; no measurement of access, quality, or patient experience; findings specific to mainland China.

### 14. When Fewer Pills Is Better Care: Rethinking Medications at the End of Life

**Journal:** *J Hosp Palliat Nurs* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 1;28(5):E210-E216
**PMID:** 42519959 | **DOI:** 10.1097/NJH.0000000000001254

**The study:** Researchers conducted a rapid review of the evidence on deprescribing — the deliberate, supervised stopping of medications — for adults with serious illness and limited life expectancy. Searching PubMed, CINAHL, and Scopus for studies published from January 2020 through December 2025, they screened 494 records and included 37 studies, spanning qualitative and observational research, pharmacist-led interventions, systematic reviews, and evaluations of tools such as STOPPFrail and OncPal. The evidence indicates that long-term preventive drugs near the end of life — statins, blood pressure medicines, diabetes drugs, blood thinners, and vitamins — often offer little short-term benefit while adding side effects and treatment burden, yet deprescribing remains inconsistent and usually reactive rather than planned.

**Why it matters:** People in hospice and palliative care frequently keep taking pills that can no longer help them in the time they have, and each unnecessary drug adds cost, side effects, swallowing difficulty, and daily work for family caregivers. The review positions nurses as the professionals best placed to review medication lists, spot inappropriate drugs, and guide conversations about stopping them.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or someone you love is on hospice or palliative care, it is reasonable to ask the care team a simple question about every medication on the list: what is this doing for me now, and what would happen if I stopped it? Preventive medicines aimed at problems years away — cholesterol drugs, some blood pressure and diabetes pills, vitamins — may be the first candidates for a planned, monitored taper rather than an abrupt stop.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For hospice nurses, care home staff, and family caregivers managing pill boxes, this review argues for building a proactive medication review into the care plan instead of waiting for a swallowing problem or a fall to force the issue — and for monitoring the patient closely after any medication is withdrawn so changes can be reversed if symptoms worsen.

**Caveats:** A rapid review rather than a full systematic review or meta-analysis; the included evidence is largely qualitative and observational, so it cannot prove that deprescribing itself improves comfort or quality of life. No new patient data were collected.

### 15. What Actually Stops Doctors and Patients From Cutting Back Unnecessary Medicines at Home

**Journal:** *Palliat Med* | **Published:** 2026 Oct;40(9):1270-1288
**PMID:** 42533730 | **DOI:** 10.1177/02692163261465769

**The study:** A Dutch team ran a qualitative systematic review, searching MEDLINE, Embase, CINAHL, and PsycINFO from 2000 to January 2025, to understand what helps and hinders deprescribing for people living at home with palliative care needs. Twenty-three studies were synthesized, covering the views of patients, their caregivers, and primary care clinicians. Four themes emerged: each person's starting attitude toward stopping medicines (shaped by personal values and clinician confidence), the quality of the deprescribing conversation itself (individualized deliberation and shared decision-making), organizational factors such as teamwork and systematic work processes, and broader system-level conditions.

**Why it matters:** Most people with advanced illness live at home and get their prescriptions from a family doctor, so whether unhelpful medications ever get reviewed usually comes down to a single conversation that may never happen. The review shows the decisive ingredient is a tailored, two-way discussion rather than a rule or checklist.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** You do not have to wait for your family doctor to raise the subject — you can bring your full medication list, including over-the-counter pills and supplements, to an appointment and ask which ones still serve your goals. Frame the conversation around what matters to you now, whether that is fewer side effects, easier days, or simply fewer tablets to swallow.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For caregivers and primary care teams, the practical lesson is structural: deprescribing happens when someone owns the medication review, when pharmacists, nurses, and GPs communicate, and when the conversation is scheduled rather than squeezed in. Care home and home care staff can flag medication burden early and prompt a formal review.

**Caveats:** Qualitative synthesis of 23 studies, so it describes perceptions and barriers rather than measuring outcomes; several review authors had co-authored included papers; findings come largely from Western primary care systems and may not generalize elsewhere.

### 16. Predicting How Long Someone With Advanced Dementia Has Left Remains Guesswork, Review Finds

**Journal:** *Palliat Med* | **Published:** 2026 Oct;40(9):1289-1301
**PMID:** 42533729 | **DOI:** 10.1177/02692163261468148

**The study:** Researchers at University College London systematically reviewed prognostic models built to estimate survival in advanced dementia, searching four databases from inception to April 2025 plus grey literature. They found six tools across seven studies covering 225,553 people with advanced dementia in community, hospital, and nursing home settings. Accuracy was modest and inconsistent — discrimination scores (AUC) ranged from 0.55 to 0.76, with the PRO-MADE model performing best but losing accuracy when tested in new populations, and five of the studies carried a high risk of bias.

**Why it matters:** Dementia is often not recognized as a terminal illness, which delays hospice referral and comfort-focused care, but the review makes clear that no current tool can reliably tell families how much time is left. Key predictors that did matter were comorbidities and how dependent the person had become in daily function.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you are planning ahead for dementia, do not wait for a prediction of months remaining to set out your wishes — write down your preferences about hospitalization, feeding, antibiotics, and comfort care while you can still express them, because the science cannot pinpoint timing.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and care home staff pressing clinicians for a number, this review is permission to shift the question: instead of "how long?", ask what changes in eating, mobility, infections, and function would signal it is time to bring in palliative or hospice support. Treat any prognostic score as one input among many, never as a standalone decision-maker.

**Caveats:** Systematic review of existing models, not new patient research; five of seven studies were rated high risk of bias and most tools lacked external validation, so the pooled picture may overstate or understate real-world accuracy.

### 17. In Rural Hospitals, One Nurse Practitioner May Be Handling Every End-of-Life Conversation

**Journal:** *J Hosp Palliat Nurs* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 1;28(5):208-213
**PMID:** 42406410 | **DOI:** 10.1097/NJH.0000000000001241

**The study:** This practice-focused article describes the reality for advanced practice registered nurses working as solo hospitalists in critical access hospitals — small rural facilities where specialty palliative care consultations, ethics committees, and structured advance care planning processes are frequently unavailable. Using a case example from a single shift, the author shows how one clinician must simultaneously handle admissions, discharges, deteriorating patients, and family distress while also leading goals-of-care, health care agent, and end-of-life planning discussions. Proposed remedies include tele-palliative care, remote ethics consultation, and APRN-focused palliative education.

**Why it matters:** Millions of older Americans live in areas served only by critical access hospitals, where the depth of end-of-life support depends heavily on one overstretched clinician rather than a specialist team. Knowing that gap exists changes how rural families should prepare.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If your local hospital is a small rural or critical access facility, do your advance care planning before a crisis: name a health care agent, put your wishes in writing, and ask your primary care clinician whether tele-palliative care is available in your area, rather than relying on a specialist team being on hand when you are admitted.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For rural senior living operators and health systems, the article is a call to invest in tele-palliative and remote ethics support and to recognize the moral and emotional load carried by solo APRN hospitalists — burnout in that single clinician directly degrades the quality of end-of-life conversations for an entire community.

**Caveats:** This is a descriptive clinical practice article with a single illustrative case, not original research — no data, no control group, and no measurement of outcomes; the perspective reflects one clinician's US rural setting and may not generalize.

### 18. Why people choose assisted suicide: it's rarely about pain alone

**Journal:** *BMJ* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 15
**PMID:** 42744336 | **DOI:** 10.1136/bmj-2026-100610

**The study:** Researchers in Switzerland interviewed 30 adults with decision-making capacity who had already contacted a right-to-die association and were actively pursuing an assisted suicide request; 14 died by assisted suicide within weeks of their interview. Interviews were conducted at home or in hospital in French, German, or Italian and analyzed thematically. Six interwoven motivations emerged, including imagining and shaping one's own death, reclaiming control over the medicalization of dying, seeking validation from others, crossing a personal threshold of unbearability, and acting before a narrowing window of physical or mental capacity closed. The authors concluded that the decision was a forward-looking, meaning-making process built on personal values and relationships, not simply a reaction to physical suffering.

**Why it matters:** It reframes a conversation that families and clinicians often reduce to pain control, showing that fear of losing control, of prolonged medicalized dying, and of missing the moment to act can matter as much as symptoms.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If thoughts about how and when you want to die are on your mind, this research suggests they are often about control, timing, and values rather than pain alone — which means it's worth naming those specific concerns out loud to your doctor or palliative care team, and asking what options exist to address each one, including advance directives and symptom planning.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and care staff: when an older person raises assisted dying, treating it purely as a symptom-management problem may miss the point. The findings suggest listening for the underlying themes — loss of authority over one's own care, fear of a drawn-out institutional death, wanting to be understood — and opening dialogue rather than closing it.

**Caveats:** Qualitative study with 30 participants recruited largely through right-to-die associations, so views likely skew toward people already committed to that path; Swiss legal context differs substantially from most countries; findings describe experiences, not causes or outcomes. Funded by the Swiss National Science Foundation and the Pallium Foundation, which had no role in the work.

### 19. Videos and booklets about end-of-life choices boost understanding — but don't ease the hardest part of deciding

**Journal:** *Palliat Med* | **Published:** 2026 Oct
**PMID:** 42517466 | **DOI:** 10.1177/02692163261464375

**The study:** A systematic review and meta-analysis pooled 16 randomized controlled trials of decision aids — mostly videos or printed materials covering goals of care, prognosis, and treatment options — for adults with palliative care needs and their caregivers, with participant ages spanning 44 to 85. The aids significantly improved decision-related knowledge and sharply reduced the likelihood that patients wanted CPR (odds ratio 0.28). However, they made no measurable difference to decisional conflict, satisfaction with the decision, or later regret, and the authors rated overall evidence quality as low.

**Why it matters:** Decision aids are increasingly handed out in hospitals and nursing homes; this shows they genuinely inform people about what resuscitation and aggressive treatment involve, but they are not a substitute for an unhurried conversation with a clinician who knows you.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If a clinician offers you a video or booklet about CPR and goals of care, it's worth watching or reading — the evidence says people come away understanding their options better. But don't expect it to settle how you feel; ask for a follow-up conversation to work through the doubts the material raises.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living and hospice teams: a decision aid can standardize information delivery, but this analysis shows it won't reduce family conflict or second-guessing on its own. Budget staff time for the discussion that follows, and be aware that the drop in CPR preference reflects better-informed choices rather than proof of better-quality decisions.

**Caveats:** Only 16 trials, heterogeneous in design and setting; overall evidence graded low quality by GRADE; no evidence that decisions better matched patients' values; most aids were tested in specific clinical populations that may not generalize to all older adults.

### 20. What actually gets palliative care started earlier: lessons from a four-hospital Australian trial

**Journal:** *Palliat Med* | **Published:** 2026 Oct
**PMID:** 42504041 | **DOI:** 10.1177/02692163261465765

**The study:** As part of a stepped-wedge implementation trial of an early palliative care model called 'Care Plus' across four Australian hospitals, researchers conducted 51 interviews with patients (n=11), caregivers (n=2), and healthcare providers (n=38); 232 patients in total received the intervention, which offered at least three outpatient palliative care consultations alongside usual cancer treatment plus primary care case conferences. The strategies participants judged most effective were defining clear illness 'transition points' that trigger a referral, systematically identifying eligible patients, building referral into routine workflows, branding the service as 'Care Plus' with agreed phrasing for clinicians, giving clinicians regular feedback, and physically co-locating palliative and cancer clinics.

**Why it matters:** Early palliative care improves quality of life for people with serious illness, yet referrals are inconsistent; this identifies concrete, copyable fixes — including simply calling it something other than 'palliative care' and putting the clinic next door to oncology.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** You don't have to wait for your oncologist to raise it. If you have advanced cancer, you can ask directly whether a palliative or supportive care consultation can run alongside your treatment — this research found that framing and wording are often what stand between patients and a referral, not medical need.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For clinicians and senior care administrators: the effective ingredients here are systems-level, not personality-driven — embedded referral triggers, workflow integration, co-location, and mentored scripts for clinicians who find the conversation hard. These are replicable without new clinical evidence.

**Caveats:** Qualitative component only, with very few caregivers (n=2) and modest patient representation; four Australian hospitals and cancer patients specifically, so may not generalize to other health systems or non-cancer illness; perceptions of effectiveness are not the same as measured outcomes.

### 21. Caregiver burnout, not the patient's illness, shapes whether families feel a death was a good one

**Journal:** *J Palliat Care* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 28
**PMID:** 42804262 | **DOI:** 10.1177/08258597261487886

**The study:** Researchers surveyed 256 primary caregivers of terminally ill patients at a medical center in southern Taiwan between December 2022 and April 2023, measuring filial piety values, caregiving burden, and perceptions of a good death. Higher caregiving burden was the strongest predictor of a poorer perception of the patient's death, and being employed also predicted lower scores. Stronger filial piety values, holding religious beliefs, and being older were each associated with more positive perceptions of a dignified death; the model explained about 28% of the variation.

**Why it matters:** How a family member remembers a death shapes their grief for years, and this suggests that supporting the exhausted caregiver may be as important to that memory as anything done for the patient.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you are caring for a dying spouse, sibling, or parent, accepting respite, hired help, or hospice support isn't a failure of devotion — burden was the single biggest factor linked to caregivers feeling the death went badly. Ask the care team specifically what respite and practical support is available to you, not just to the patient.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For hospice and senior living teams: screening caregivers for burden, and paying particular attention to those still working full-time and to younger caregivers, may be a practical lever for improving family-reported quality of dying. In East Asian and other family-centered cultures, the findings suggest honoring rather than sidelining traditional duty-of-care values within the care plan.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional and observational, so causation cannot be established — burdened caregivers may also be caring for patients with harder deaths; purposive sampling at a single medical center in Taiwan, so results may not generalize to other cultures; all outcomes self-reported.

### 22. Adults with intellectual disabilities and advanced lung cancer get far less cancer treatment — though palliative care access held up

**Journal:** *Palliat Med* | **Published:** 2026 Oct
**PMID:** 42490651 | **DOI:** 10.1177/02692163261464345

**The study:** Using population health data from Ontario, Canada, researchers followed 33,184 adults diagnosed with stage IV non-small-cell lung cancer between 2010 and 2022, including 106 with intellectual or developmental disabilities. Those with disabilities were substantially less likely to see any cancer specialist (hazard ratio 0.62), to receive any cancer-directed treatment (HR 0.52), or to receive radiation or systemic therapy (HR 0.49). Receipt of palliative care in the year after diagnosis, by contrast, did not differ between the two groups.

**Why it matters:** It documents a real gap in specialist consultations and treatment for a group that already faces communication and access barriers, while showing that palliative care is one part of the system reaching them equally.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have a disability and a serious cancer diagnosis, you are entitled to the same specialist consultations as anyone else. It's reasonable to ask explicitly whether you have been referred to a medical or radiation oncologist and, if not, why — and to bring someone with you who can help you ask.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families, group home staff, and guardians supporting adults with intellectual or developmental disabilities: this is a prompt to track whether referrals are actually being made and to ensure that decisions to forgo treatment are genuinely the person's informed choice rather than an assumption made on their behalf. Communication supports and extra appointment time can be requested.

**Caveats:** Observational administrative-data study; cannot establish why the gaps occurred and cannot distinguish clinically appropriate decisions or patient preference from bias. Only 106 patients with intellectual or developmental disabilities, giving imprecise estimates; single Canadian province with universal health coverage, so findings may not generalize to other systems; administrative codes may under-identify disability.

## Research Trends & Continuity

Nine studies appear in both digests with identical PMIDs — the nursing home outcomes interviews (PMID 42742117), the PALL-AI empathy analysis (PMID 42599269), the calf circumference cohort (PMID 42674364), the Glasgow pharmacy technician pilot (PMID 42730948), the hospital transfer scoping review (PMID 42714187), the AI prognosis survey (PMID 42463061), the lidocaine trial (PMID 42536632), the deprescribing rapid review (PMID 42519959) and the Swiss assisted dying interviews (PMID 42744336). The comparison below focuses on what is genuinely new against them.

**Convergent findings**

- *Caregiver strain is a measurable clinical variable, not background noise.* "Caregiver burnout, not the patient's illness, shapes whether families feel a death was a good one" (PMID 42804262) lands on the same ground as the previous digest's Gaza caregiver survey (PMID 42728769) and the Türkiye spiritual well-being study (PMID 42697036): burden and unmet need, not disease severity, drive how families experience a death. The new grief study, "Grief After a Child's Cancer Death Can Last Decades — And Poverty Makes It Worse" (PMID 42603866), extends the same logic years past the death itself.
- *Getting palliative care in earlier is a systems problem with systems fixes.* "What actually gets palliative care started earlier: lessons from a four-hospital Australian trial" (PMID 42504041) and "Adding a Palliative Care Nurse Practitioner to an MS Clinic Uncovered Symptoms Nobody Was Asking About" (PMID 42714185) both land on co-location and embedded referral — precisely the mechanism the previous digest's JAMA Internal Medicine program comparison identified as separating high from low hospice use (PMID 42734951), and the answer to the 23-day referral lag documented there (PMID 42713708).
- *Cheap, routine data predicts death reasonably well.* "A Computer Model Can Flag Which New Nursing Home Residents Are Most at Risk of Dying Within Six Months" (PMID 42748398) uses intake assessments already collected, echoing the tape-measure-and-blood-test approach in PMID 42674364 carried over from the previous digest.

**Advances**

- Deprescribing moves from "what to stop" to "why it doesn't happen." The previous digest's rapid review (PMID 42519959) established that preventive drugs linger; the new Dutch qualitative synthesis, "What actually stops doctors and patients from cutting back unnecessary medicines at home" (PMID 42533730), names the decisive ingredient as an individualized, scheduled conversation with a clear owner — and the Glasgow technician pilot (PMID 42730948) shows one workforce that can own it.
- Advance care planning gains a harder edge. The previous digest's scoping review showed specific written instructions track with fewer transfers (PMID 42714187); the new meta-analysis "Videos and booklets about end-of-life choices boost understanding — but don't ease the hardest part of deciding" (PMID 42517466) shows the tools we hand people to reach those decisions inform them without resolving their doubt.
- Physician attitudes toward AI prognosis (PMID 42463061) now have performance data beside them: PMID 42748398 reports an AUC of 0.90 in Taiwanese long-term care, while "Predicting How Long Someone With Advanced Dementia Has Left Remains Guesswork, Review Finds" (PMID 42533729) reports 0.55–0.76 with most models unvalidated elsewhere.

**Counterpoints**

- Accuracy depends entirely on the population. The nursing home model's 0.90 (PMID 42748398) and the dementia review's 0.55–0.76 (PMID 42533729) sit in the same digest, cautioning against the broadly favorable physician attitudes captured in PMID 42463061.
- The thin-evidence pattern deepens. Alongside the null lidocaine trial (PMID 42536632) and the previous digest's urine drug testing review (PMID 42362165), the new systematic review "Propofol for Sedation at the End of Life: Promising in Hard Cases, but the Evidence Is Thin" (PMID 42533745) rates certainty as low to very low for a practice used at the most consequential moment of care.

**New themes**

- *Who gets left out, and who is not even counted.* "Adults with intellectual disabilities and advanced lung cancer get far less cancer treatment" (PMID 42490651), "In Rural Hospitals, One Nurse Practitioner May Be Handling Every End-of-Life Conversation" (PMID 42406410), "Where People Die Is Poorly Recorded Worldwide" (PMID 42544535) and "China Built a Palliative Care Policy Framework in 35 Years" (PMID 42592757) together open an access-and-infrastructure thread absent from the previous run.
- *Palliative care outside oncology.* PMID 42714185 pushes the specialty into progressive neurological disease, where symptom burden runs for years.
- *Consent and documentation at the sedation decision.* PMID 42533745 flags missing or implicit consent as the actionable gap, a governance question no prior study in these digests raised.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Several studies in this batch, read together, say the same uncomfortable thing: the paperwork of end-of-life planning is easier to complete than it is to make stick. Written wishes reduce hospital transfers only when they contain explicit instructions, and residents are still sent out against plans in their own files (PMID 42714187); videos and booklets improve what people know without reducing their doubt, dissatisfaction or later regret (PMID 42517466); no tool can tell a family how much time is left in advanced dementia (PMID 42533729); consent for palliative sedation is often undocumented (PMID 42533745); and in rural critical access hospitals, one solo nurse practitioner may be handling every goals-of-care conversation on the floor (PMID 42406410).

**Why pitch this now:** Advance directive campaigns have succeeded at getting documents signed, and this batch is the first to show clearly that signing is where the system stops — the gap is now in retrieval, specificity, and who is standing there at 2 a.m.

**Angle:** A practical feature for older adults and adult children that walks through the difference between a plan that exists and a plan that works — what to write in concrete terms, where it must be stored, who can find it after hours, and who will speak if the moment comes — with the honest note that no prognosis tool will tell you when that moment is.

**Potential headlines:**
- What Makes a Written End-of-Life Wish Actually Hold Up
- Better Informed, Just as Torn: Why the Booklet Doesn't Settle Anything
- The 2 a.m. Test Every Advance Care Plan Should Pass

**Potential outlets:**
- **Next Avenue** — their readers have largely already been told to write an advance directive; the natural follow-up piece is why the document alone isn't enough, written without scolding.
- **AARP** — a checkable, consumer-rights framing (ask where your plan is stored, who retrieves it, who can override it) fits their service-journalism format and reaches families at scale.
- **LeadingAge** — the provider side of this story is auditable: how often is a resident's plan actually pulled before an ambulance is called, and what does the after-hours pathway look like.
- **Hospice News** — the sedation consent gap and the rural solo-clinician burden are operational and regulatory concerns their provider audience can act on.
