### Study 2: What Actually Counts as Success When Palliative Care Comes Into a Nursing Home? Staff Say the Usual Metrics Miss Most of It
**PMID:** 42742117 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Hospitalization outcomes mischaracterized** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Rather than the hospitalization and utilization numbers health systems typically track, participants described meaningful results in four interlocking domains: residents (care matching their stated goals, symptom relief, understanding their choices)"
- **Abstract says:** "Resident outcomes included goal-concordant care, symptom management, hospitalization rates, and understanding of care choices and control."
- **Problem:** Participants actually named hospitalization rates as one of the meaningful resident outcomes; the digest sets utilization metrics in opposition to participants' views and omits hospitalization from the resident list.
- **Suggested fix:** "Note that participants valued hospitalization rates too, but added resident, family, staff and facility outcomes that utilization metrics alone do not capture."

**Issue B. Overstated share of value missed** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "this work suggests current evaluation frameworks capture only a fraction of its value"
- **Abstract says:** "These findings highlight multi-domain and interdependent PC consultation outcomes across residents, families, staff, and NH facilities that may be overlooked in existing evaluation frameworks."
- **Problem:** The abstract says some outcomes may be overlooked; it does not quantify how much of palliative care's value existing frameworks capture, and the headline's "Miss Most of It" goes further still.
- **Suggested fix:** "Rephrase to "may overlook outcomes that people closest to the work consider central" and soften the headline."

### Study 5: Pharmacy Technicians Visiting Housebound Seniors at Home Found Dozens of Missed Checkups and Cut Emergency Contacts by More Than Half
**PMID:** 42730948 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Causal headline for uncontrolled pre-post study** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Cut Emergency Contacts by More Than Half"
- **Abstract says:** "Pre-post intervention study, in two primary care general practices... unscheduled care contacts reduced from a total of 56 (range 0-7) to 21 (0-4)."
- **Problem:** A single-arm pre-post feasibility evaluation with 25 patients cannot show the visits "cut" contacts; the headline also contradicts the digest's own caveat that causation cannot be established.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to something like "...and Unscheduled Care Contacts Fell by More Than Half" and keep the no-control-group framing."

**Issue B. "Emergency contacts" vs unscheduled care** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Cut Emergency Contacts by More Than Half"
- **Abstract says:** "unscheduled care contacts reduced from a total of 56 (range 0-7) to 21 (0-4)"
- **Problem:** The measured outcome was unscheduled care contacts, a broader category than emergency contacts.
- **Suggested fix:** "Use "unscheduled care contacts" in the headline as the body text already does."

### Study 6: Written Wishes Do Cut Hospital Trips at the End of Life — But Only If Staff Follow Them
**PMID:** 42714187 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Causal headline from a scoping review** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Written Wishes Do Cut Hospital Trips at the End of Life — But Only If Staff Follow Them"
- **Abstract says:** "Advance care plans or advance care directives that included instructions to not resuscitate or not hospitalize resulted in fewer hospital transfers than those that did not. ... Of concern were reports of hospital transfers against resident's wishes"
- **Problem:** The review pooled mostly observational studies; the digest's own caveat says causation cannot be established, and no analysis tested the "only if staff follow them" condition — it is an inference from scattered reports of transfers against documented wishes.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reframe as "Specific Written Instructions Are Linked to Fewer Hospital Trips — But Some Residents Are Transferred Against Their Documented Wishes.""

**Issue B. Reasons for transfer attributed to wish-violating transfers** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the review found transfers happening against documented wishes, often driven by acuity, perceived vulnerability, or family pressure in a crisis"
- **Abstract says:** "Reasons for hospital transfer were classified as age, acuity, vulnerability, and family-related. Of concern were reports of hospital transfers against resident's wishes as documented on advance care plans and advance care directives."
- **Problem:** The abstract lists drivers of hospital transfer generally (including age), not specifically of transfers that contradicted documented wishes.
- **Suggested fix:** "Separate the two findings: transfer drivers were age, acuity, vulnerability and family-related; separately, some transfers occurred against documented wishes."

### Study 8: An IV Drug Hoped to Relieve Cancer Pain That Opioids Couldn't Touch Failed to Beat Placebo
**PMID:** 42536632 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Feasibility design not stated** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "French investigators ran a multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in palliative care units testing 48 hours of continuous low-dose intravenous lidocaine against placebo"
- **Abstract says:** "We conducted a multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled feasibility study... To assess the feasibility and tolerability of continuous low-dose intravenous lidocaine infusion... and to explore its potential impact on pain outcomes"
- **Problem:** The study was designed primarily to test feasibility and tolerability, with pain effects exploratory; presenting it as an efficacy trial that "failed" slightly overstates what it was powered or intended to determine.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add "feasibility" to the design description and note that the pain comparison was exploratory."

### Study 9: Grief After a Child's Cancer Death Can Last Decades — And Poverty Makes It Worse
**PMID:** 42603866 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Causal headline from cross-sectional data** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Grief After a Child's Cancer Death Can Last Decades — And Poverty Makes It Worse"
- **Abstract says:** "This multicenter cross-sectional survey... High poverty risk (β = 11.04, p<0.001) and death in healthcare facility (β = 3.82, p = 0.024) were associated with higher grief intensity"
- **Problem:** "Makes it worse" asserts causation from a single-timepoint survey, contradicting the digest's own caveat that causation cannot be established.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change to "...And Financial Hardship Is Linked to More Intense Grief.""

**Issue B. "Decades" not supported** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Grief After a Child's Cancer Death Can Last Decades"
- **Abstract says:** "Evidence on grief symptoms beyond ten years post-loss remains limited... had died ⩾1 year prior to study participation... Parental grief symptoms can persist long after the death of a child to cancer."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports only that the child died at least a year before participation and that grief can persist long after; the time range supporting "decades" is not verifiable from the abstract.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say grief "can persist for many years" unless full-text confirms time-since-death range."

**Issue C. Denominator described as eligible parents** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "only 103 of 388 eligible parents participated"
- **Abstract says:** "Of 388 identified cases, 103 parents of 81 deceased children participated."
- **Problem:** 388 refers to identified cases (children/families), not individual eligible parents, so the participation fraction is not strictly a parent-level response rate.
- **Suggested fix:** "Write "103 parents, from 388 identified cases, took part.""

**Issue D. "Strongest predictor" claim** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "financial hardship was the strongest predictor of severe long-term grief"
- **Abstract says:** "High poverty risk (β = 11.04, p<0.001) and death in healthcare facility (β = 3.82, p = 0.024) were associated with higher grief intensity, while longer time since death was associated with lower grief intensity (β = -5.46, p = 0.041)."
- **Problem:** Poverty risk had the largest coefficient, but the abstract never ranks predictors, and raw betas across differently scaled variables are not directly comparable.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say poverty risk showed the largest association among the factors examined."

### Study 14: When Fewer Pills Is Better Care: Rethinking Medications at the End of Life
**PMID:** 42519959 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Nurses' role slightly overstated** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "The review positions nurses as the professionals best placed to review medication lists, spot inappropriate drugs, and guide conversations about stopping them."
- **Abstract says:** "Nurses play a central role in medication reconciliation, identifying potentially inappropriate medications, facilitating shared decision-making, and monitoring patient response after medication withdrawal."
- **Problem:** "Best placed" implies a comparative claim of superiority over other professions that the abstract does not make; it says nurses have a central role.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change to "positions nurses as central to reviewing medication lists...""

**Issue B. Tapering advice not in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "may be the first candidates for a planned, monitored taper rather than an abrupt stop"
- **Abstract says:** "Nurses play a central role in ... monitoring patient response after medication withdrawal."
- **Problem:** The abstract supports monitoring after withdrawal but says nothing about tapering versus abrupt discontinuation; this specific clinical detail is unverifiable from the abstract.
- **Suggested fix:** "Drop "taper rather than an abrupt stop" or reword to "a planned, monitored withdrawal with follow-up.""

### Study 21: Caregiver burnout, not the patient's illness, shapes whether families feel a death was a good one
**PMID:** 42804262 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline asserts an untested comparison** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Caregiver burnout, not the patient's illness, shapes whether families feel a death was a good one"
- **Abstract says:** "Multiple regression analysis (R2 = 28.18%) revealed that caregiving burden (β = -0.48, p < .001) and being employed (β = -0.39, p = .009) were negative predictors of good death perceptions. In contrast, filial piety (β = 0.33, p = .001), religious belief (β = 0.35, p = .012), and older age ... were significant positive predictors."
- **Problem:** The regression included caregiver characteristics (burden, employment, filial piety, religion, age) — no patient illness variables appear in the abstract. The headline sets up a contrast the study did not test, and "shapes" reads as causal for a cross-sectional survey.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reframe, e.g., "Caregiver burden is the factor most strongly linked to whether families feel a death was a good one" and drop "not the patient's illness.""

**Issue B. "Burnout" for a burden inventory score** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Caregiver burnout, not the patient's illness"
- **Abstract says:** "Primary caregivers (N = 256) of terminally ill patients completed the Chinese Filial Piety Scale, Caregiver Burden Inventory, and the Good Death Inventory-Short Version."
- **Problem:** The study measured caregiving burden with the Caregiver Burden Inventory; "burnout" is a distinct clinical construct that was not assessed.
- **Suggested fix:** "Use "caregiving burden" or "caregiver strain" instead of "burnout.""

**Issue C. Same untested comparison in Why it matters** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "this suggests that supporting the exhausted caregiver may be as important to that memory as anything done for the patient"
- **Abstract says:** "caregiving burden remains a primary barrier to positive end-of-life perceptions. Palliative care interventions should be culturally sensitive, focusing on alleviating the emotional and physical strain of caregivers"
- **Problem:** The study did not compare caregiver-directed support against patient-directed care, so the relative-importance claim goes beyond the data.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say the findings point to caregiver burden as an important and modifiable factor in how families perceive a death, without ranking it against patient care."

**Issue D. "Working full-time" not specified** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "paying particular attention to those still working full-time and to younger caregivers"
- **Abstract says:** "being employed (β = -0.39, p = .009) were negative predictors of good death perceptions ... older age (41-55 years: β = 0.29, p = .015; >55 years: β = 0.32, p = .013) were significant positive predictors"
- **Problem:** The abstract reports employment status only, not full-time versus part-time work.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change to "those who are employed" rather than "working full-time.""

**Issue E. Unsupported background claim** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "How a family member remembers a death shapes their grief for years"
- **Abstract says:** "(no data on subsequent grief trajectories reported)"
- **Problem:** Unverifiable from abstract only — the study measured good-death perception at one time point and followed no one into bereavement.
- **Suggested fix:** "Attribute this to prior bereavement literature or soften to a general statement."

### Study 22: Adults with intellectual disabilities and advanced lung cancer get far less cancer treatment — though palliative care access held up
**PMID:** 42490651 | **Verdict:** ✅ Accurate

**Issue A. "Any cancer specialist" gloss** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "substantially less likely to see any cancer specialist (hazard ratio 0.62)"
- **Abstract says:** "significantly less likely to receive any cancer-directed consultation (hazard ratio [HR] = 0.62; 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.49-0.78)"
- **Problem:** The outcome was receipt of a cancer-directed consultation with a surgeon, medical oncologist or radiation oncologist; "see any cancer specialist" is a reasonable but looser paraphrase that could be read as including all oncology contact.
- **Suggested fix:** "Use "receive any cancer-directed specialist consultation (surgeon, medical or radiation oncologist).""
