### 1. Four Years of Tracking Frailty: Certain Medication Classes Flagged as Warning Signs

**Journal:** *Arch Gerontol Geriatr* | **Published:** 2026 Dec;151:106388
**PMID:** 42669283 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.archger.2026.106388

**The study:** Researchers followed 973 community-dwelling Mexican adults aged 60 and older for four years, measuring frailty annually and mapping how people moved between robust, prefrail and frail states. Transitions were asymmetric: people slid toward worse frailty far more often than they improved. Older age, being female, having multiple chronic conditions, and taking antihypertensive, psycholeptic/psychoanaleptic (sedative or antidepressant-type), antiparkinsonian or antivertigo drugs were all associated with moving toward a more severe frailty state, while more education and having a partner were protective.

**Why it matters:** Prefrailty is the stage where decline can still be slowed or reversed, and the medication classes linked to worsening frailty are among the most commonly prescribed to older adults — making routine medication review a plausible part of frailty prevention.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you are taking blood pressure pills, sedatives, antidepressants, Parkinson's drugs or dizziness medication, it is reasonable to ask your doctor or pharmacist at your next visit whether each one is still needed at its current dose — and to ask whether you are showing early signs of frailty, such as slower walking, unintentional weight loss or new exhaustion, while there is still time to act.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For care teams and family members: improvement was the rarest transition in this cohort, so waiting for someone to 'bounce back' is a poor strategy. Build annual frailty screening into care planning and treat the prefrail stage — not the frail stage — as the trigger for medication review, exercise and nutrition support.

**Caveats:** Observational cohort — cannot establish that the drugs cause frailty, since the underlying conditions they treat may be the real driver; single-country sample (Mexican social security system enrollees) that may not generalize; secondary analysis of an existing cohort.

### 2. Nearly All Heart Failure Drugs Are Stopped When Patients With Dementia Enter Hospice

**Journal:** *J Palliat Med* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 19 (online ahead of print)
**PMID:** 42897830 | **DOI:** 10.1177/10966218261498525

**The study:** A secondary analysis of 1,338 hospice patients who had both heart failure and Alzheimer's disease or a related dementia, drawn from 12 hospices, examined which heart medications they were taking before admission and what happened at admission. Before hospice, 44% were on beta-blockers, 32% on loop diuretics and 18% on RAAS inhibitors. At hospice admission, discontinuation ranged from 91% to 100% across drug classes — including 97.5% of beta-blockers and 97% of loop diuretics — regardless of whether heart failure was the primary or secondary hospice diagnosis.

**Why it matters:** Families are often blindsided when a long list of heart medications disappears at hospice admission; knowing this is near-universal practice — and that researchers are still studying how it affects symptoms like breathlessness and swelling — helps frame the conversation in advance.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or a loved one is approaching hospice enrollment with heart failure, ask the hospice team upfront which medications will be stopped, why, and what the plan is if symptoms like shortness of breath or fluid buildup return — diuretics in particular can be symptom-relieving, not just preventive.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For hospice and senior living staff: this near-total discontinuation happened regardless of whether heart failure was the primary terminal diagnosis, suggesting a reflexive rather than individualized approach. Documenting the clinical rationale and tracking symptom outcomes after stopping would strengthen both family trust and the evidence base.

**Caveats:** Secondary analysis of existing records; relies on documented medication use, which may undercount actual prehospice use; descriptive only — no data on symptom outcomes after discontinuation, so the study cannot say whether stopping helped or harmed.

### 3. In Palliative Care, Preventive Pills Get Dropped — But Often Abruptly, Without Tapering

**Journal:** *J Pain Symptom Manage* | **Published:** 2026 Dec;72(6):752-762.e3
**PMID:** 42731737 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jpainsymman.2026.09.009

**The study:** Australian researchers tracked medications at five points — before admission through discharge or death — for 386 adults admitted to a metropolitan palliative care unit in 2023 (median age 78). Preventive medications were commonly discontinued while symptom-directed medications increased over time. Notably, drugs that normally require gradual tapering — proton pump inhibitors, beta-blockers and antidepressants — were frequently stopped outright with no observed dose reduction first. Polypharmacy was more likely in younger patients, those admitted for symptom management and those with endocrine conditions, and less likely in those aged 80+ or in the terminal phase.

**Why it matters:** Stopping certain drugs cold can cause rebound symptoms — acid reflux, rapid heart rate, withdrawal effects — that are avoidable with a taper, and these are exactly the symptoms palliative care is meant to prevent.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Shedding unnecessary pills at the end of life is usually a kindness, but if a beta-blocker, acid-reflux drug or antidepressant is being stopped, ask the team whether it should be tapered rather than halted, so you don't trade pill burden for rebound discomfort.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For clinicians and care home staff: this is a concrete, fixable process gap. Deprescribing protocols should specify which classes need a taper plan and who owns it, and medication review should be repeated across the admission rather than done once at the door.

**Caveats:** Single-center retrospective cohort in one Australian city; chart-based, so tapers done before admission may not be captured; no data on whether abrupt cessation actually caused withdrawal symptoms; findings may not generalize to other health systems.

### 4. National Snapshot: How Older Americans' Mix of Prescriptions, OTC Drugs and Supplements Has Shifted

**Journal:** *JAMA* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 20;336(15):1369-1371
**PMID:** 42783349 | **DOI:** 10.1001/jama.2026.17268

**The study:** Using a nationally representative US sample, researchers compared medication use among older adults in 2015-2016 with 2021-2023, counting not just prescription drugs but also over-the-counter products and dietary supplements. The analysis specifically tracked how often older adults were taking combinations flagged as potentially major drug-drug interactions.

**Why it matters:** Most interaction checking happens on the prescription list alone, yet this work counts the supplements and drugstore products that older adults frequently don't mention — the blind spot where many dangerous combinations hide.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Bring everything to your next appointment — prescriptions, aspirin, antacids, fish oil, turmeric, melatonin, herbal products — in a bag or on a single written list, and ask your pharmacist to run an interaction check on the full set, not just what's in your prescription record.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and senior living staff: supplements and OTC products typically sit outside the facility or EHR medication record. Build a periodic 'brown bag review' into care routines so the real medication picture, including what family members bring in from home, gets checked by a pharmacist.

**Caveats:** Published as a brief research report; medication and supplement use in national surveys is typically self-reported and may be incomplete; observational design identifies potential interaction risk, not confirmed harm; several authors report consulting, grant and equity relationships, including funding from NIH, Arnold Ventures and the National Community Pharmacists Association.

### 5. Botox for Depression? Pooled Trials Show Benefit — With Interest for Patients on Many Medications

**Journal:** *J Affect Disord* | **Published:** 2026 Dec 15;415:122443
**PMID:** 42669382 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jad.2026.122443

**The study:** A systematic review and meta-analysis pooled eight randomized controlled trials covering 636 participants that tested facial botulinum toxin A injections for depression. BoNT/A significantly reduced depressive symptoms versus control (SMD -0.81), outperformed placebo (SMD -1.04) and performed comparably to the antidepressant sertraline. Standard glabellar (frown line) protocols worked; high-dose pan-facial injection did not beat control. Overall adverse event rates matched controls, with only transient local reactions and none of sertraline's systemic side effects.

**Why it matters:** For older adults already juggling many prescriptions, a treatment that does not add another systemic drug — with its interaction and side effect risks — is a genuinely different option worth knowing exists.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If oral antidepressants have caused side effects you can't tolerate, or your medication list is already long enough that adding another drug worries you, this is a question worth raising with a psychiatrist — while recognizing the evidence base is still small and mostly not in people over 65.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and care staff: don't read this as a cosmetic fix for low mood. It's early-stage evidence from small trials, and the practical takeaway is simply that non-systemic alternatives to antidepressants are being actively studied for people with heavy pill burdens.

**Caveats:** Meta-analysis of only eight trials with 636 total participants; trials were not conducted specifically in older adults, so generalizability to seniors is unproven; blinding is inherently difficult with an injection that visibly changes facial expression; depression outcomes were rating-scale based; no long-term follow-up data reported.

### 6. AI Chatbots Aren't Ready to Assess Older Adults' Mental Health — and Can Show Ageist Bias

**Journal:** *Am J Geriatr Psychiatry* | **Published:** 2026 Dec;34(12):1536-1544
**PMID:** 42786110 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jagp.2026.08.016

**The study:** Reviewers systematically mapped 47 empirical evaluations published 2023-2025 that tested large language models on clinically relevant tasks in geriatric psychiatry, where delirium, dementia and late-life depression overlap against a backdrop of frailty, polypharmacy and long medical histories. Four recurring failure patterns emerged: unstable diagnoses when reasoning across long patient histories (losing cues buried mid-record), increased hallucination when fed misleading information, video models missing brief safety-critical events such as falls, and systemic ageism that distorted risk estimates and care recommendations. The authors conclude current systems are unsuitable for autonomous assessment and should be limited to low-risk support roles with human oversight.

**Why it matters:** AI tools are already being marketed into clinics, call centers and senior living settings, and this review shows their weakest points are precisely the features that define older patients — long histories, multiple drugs, and subtle, intermittent safety events.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you use an AI chatbot to interpret symptoms or check your medications, treat its answer as a conversation starter, not a verdict — it can sound confident while quietly dropping details from a long health history or echoing age-based assumptions about what you should expect at your age.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living operators and clinical leaders evaluating AI products: ask vendors for evidence the model was stress-tested on long longitudinal records, adversarial or inaccurate inputs, and bias by patient age — and never deploy video-based monitoring as the sole fall-detection safeguard.

**Caveats:** Scoping review of published evaluations rather than original patient-level research; the underlying studies are heterogeneous and mostly simulated rather than real clinical deployments; the field moves fast, so specific model limitations may change; no funding reported and authors declare no conflicts.

### 7. Americans Over 65 Are Taking More Pills Than Ever — and the Frailest Are Taking the Most

**Journal:** *J Am Geriatr Soc* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 14
**PMID:** 42870717 | **DOI:** 10.1111/jgs.70759

**The study:** Researchers analyzed two decades of nationally representative U.S. survey data (NHANES, 1999 through March 2020) covering 12,766 community-dwelling adults aged 65 and older, grouping them as robust, mildly frail, or moderately-to-severely frail. Average prescription counts rose in every group: from 2.0 to 2.9 drugs among robust adults, 3.9 to 5.5 among mildly frail adults, and 6.0 to 7.8 among the most frail. Cardiovascular drugs stayed a steady backbone, while metabolic medications drove increases across the board and central nervous system/psychotherapeutic drugs rose fastest among the frailest adults. Among people taking 10 or more medications, the mix differed sharply: robust adults leaned toward CNS and topical drugs, frail adults toward cardiometabolic ones.

**Why it matters:** Medication burden is climbing fastest in exactly the people least able to tolerate side effects, and this study shows which drug classes are doing the accumulating at each stage of frailty — useful context for any medication review.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If your prescription list has grown over the years, it may be worth asking your doctor or pharmacist for a full medication review — particularly about sedatives, sleep aids, antidepressants and other brain-active drugs, which this study found are piling up fastest among the frailest older adults. Bring every bottle, including over-the-counter products, to the appointment.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living and home care staff: resident medication lists are not static. This research suggests building periodic, frailty-aware medication reconciliation into care planning, with extra scrutiny of CNS/psychotherapeutic agents in residents who have declined functionally.

**Caveats:** Observational, cross-sectional survey data — it cannot show whether the extra medications help or harm. Frailty was derived from a constructed index, prescriptions were captured at a single point in time, and nursing home residents were excluded.

### 8. Not All "Fall Risk" Drugs Carry Equal Risk: Blood Pressure Pills Weren't the Problem

**Journal:** *Arch Gerontol Geriatr* | **Published:** 2026 Nov
**PMID:** 42308749 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.archger.2026.106314

**The study:** Investigators followed 1,273 community-dwelling Black and White older adults (median age 83) in the Health, Aging, and Body Composition Study for two years, verifying medications brought to the study visit and collecting fall reports every six months. Using the STEADI-Rx list of fall risk increasing drugs, they found antihypertensives — used by 81% of participants — were not associated with fall injuries. Other fall-risk drugs, including antidepressants, anticonvulsants, opioids, antihistamines and muscle relaxants, were: any use raised the odds of fall injury 35%, and each additional such drug raised the odds of a non-injurious fall by 18%.

**Why it matters:** Fall-prevention conversations often focus on blood pressure medication, but this study points the finger at a different set of drugs — several of them sedating, and some available over the counter.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you've been told to worry about falls, ask your prescriber specifically about antihistamines, muscle relaxants, opioids, antidepressants and anticonvulsants on your list — the drugs linked to injury here. Don't stop anything on your own, and don't assume an over-the-counter sleep or allergy product is harmless.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For caregivers and senior living teams: fall-risk medication screening that lumps all drug classes together may miss the signal. This evidence supports targeting the non-antihypertensive classes — including OTC antihistamines families may be supplying themselves — during falls huddles and post-fall reviews.

**Caveats:** Observational cohort — cannot establish causation. Falls and injuries were self-reported every six months; follow-up was only two years; median age 83 means findings may not generalize to younger seniors. One author reports grant funding from Amgen Inc.

### 9. One in Five Hospital Readmissions in Rural Australia Traced Back to Medications

**Journal:** *Int J Clin Pharm* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 7
**PMID:** 42842153 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11096-026-02228-7

**The study:** Researchers reviewed records for 2,220 adults admitted to rural and remote hospitals in New South Wales between June 2022 and August 2023, applying a validated tool (AT-HARM10) to judge whether 30-day readmissions were medication-related. Of the 133 patients readmitted within 30 days, 22.5% had a medication-related cause. Older age, higher comorbidity burden, insulin use, and each additional regular medication (7% higher odds per drug) were associated with medication-related readmission.

**Why it matters:** Medication problems after discharge are considered largely preventable, and they appear to hit older, multi-medicated patients in rural areas hardest — where pharmacy follow-up and continuity of care are thinnest.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Before you leave the hospital, ask for a written, reconciled list of exactly what to take, what was stopped, and what changed — and arrange a pharmacist or GP review within a week or two, especially if you take insulin or many daily medications.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and discharge planners, particularly outside metro areas: the handoff window is the vulnerable point. Consider making post-discharge medication reconciliation and a follow-up call a standard part of bringing a relative home.

**Caveats:** Retrospective chart review at rural hospitals in one Australian health district; only 30 medication-related readmissions were identified, and associations were unadjusted odds ratios, so confounding is likely. Findings may not generalize to other health systems.

### 10. Persistent Dizziness May Be an Early Warning Sign for Dementia

**Journal:** *J Appl Gerontol* | **Published:** 2026 Nov
**PMID:** 41264877 | **DOI:** 10.1177/07334648251398097

**The study:** Using six waves of the U.S. Health and Retirement Study (2000-2020), researchers compared older adults who reported dizziness in at least two waves against those who did not, adjusting for age, sex, education, multiple chronic conditions and polypharmacy. Those with persistent dizziness started with lower cognitive scores and declined significantly faster, and had a 36% higher hazard of developing dementia over follow-up.

**Why it matters:** Dizziness is often brushed off as a minor nuisance of aging or a medication side effect, but repeated episodes may flag people who warrant closer cognitive monitoring and a careful review of balance-affecting drugs.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If dizziness keeps coming back year after year, mention it explicitly at your next visit rather than treating it as normal aging — ask for a workup that includes a review of your medications, blood pressure and inner ear, and ask whether cognitive screening makes sense for you. This study does not show dizziness causes dementia.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For caregivers and senior living staff: a resident's recurring complaints of dizziness deserve documentation and follow-up, not just a grab bar. The link here is to cognitive trajectory as well as fall risk.

**Caveats:** Observational cohort study — association only, no causation. Dizziness was self-reported, its underlying causes (medication effects, inner ear disease, blood pressure) were not distinguished, and reverse causation from early undiagnosed brain changes is possible.

### 11. Delirium Plus Dementia Plus Poor Nutrition: A Combination That Cut Survival in Half

**Journal:** *Maturitas* | **Published:** 2026 Nov
**PMID:** 42896356 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.maturitas.2026.109154

**The study:** A UK hospital followed 554 patients aged 61 to 105 (mean 85) admitted with acute delirium, tracking the 497 who survived to discharge for an average of 6.2 years. After adjusting for age, sex, polypharmacy, anticholinergic drug burden and other conditions, patients who had malnutrition plus delirium superimposed on dementia had six times the odds of dying within a month of discharge and a 48% higher long-term death risk than well-nourished patients without dementia. Median survival after discharge was 26 months for the well-nourished group without dementia versus 13 months for the malnourished dementia group.

**Why it matters:** It identifies a simple, checkable combination — nutrition status plus dementia plus delirium — that hospitals and families can use to flag who needs intensive follow-up, nutritional support and realistic conversations about goals of care after discharge.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or a relative is hospitalized with confusion, ask whether nutrition has been formally assessed and whether the medication list, including anticholinergic drugs, has been reviewed — and ask what the follow-up plan looks like for the first month after discharge, which was the highest-risk window.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and senior living professionals: this gives a concrete framework for post-hospital planning. A resident returning after a delirium episode with dementia and poor nutritional status is in a high-risk group that warrants closer monitoring, dietitian involvement, and earlier advance care planning discussions.

**Caveats:** Single-center, retrospective, observational study in one UK hospital — it shows association, not causation, and cannot prove that treating malnutrition would extend survival. Admissions predate and span the start of the pandemic period.

### 12. Dementia Caregivers Say the Hardest Part of Managing Pills Isn't the Pills — It's Getting Answers

**Journal:** *Disabil Rehabil* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 19
**PMID:** 42897099 | **DOI:** 10.1080/09638288.2026.2747161

**The study:** Researchers in Ontario used a mixed-methods design with 13 family caregivers managing medications for a person living with dementia, combining the Family Caregiver Medication Administration Hassles Scale with in-depth follow-up questions on each item. Caregivers rated information sharing and information seeking as their biggest source of hassle, and the sheer number of medications (polypharmacy) as the least. Their accounts centered on unmet needs for stage-specific education about what each drug is for, what effects to expect, and how to actually give it at home.

**Why it matters:** It reframes caregiver medication support: the problem families report isn't primarily the length of the list but the absence of clear, practical, dementia-stage-appropriate guidance from the health system.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're helping manage someone's dementia medications, you're entitled to ask the prescriber or pharmacist three plain questions for each drug: what is it for, what should I expect to see, and what should make me call you. Ask for it in writing if that helps.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For clinicians, pharmacists and senior living staff: caregivers in this study wanted information in multiple formats — written, verbal, digital and non-digital — and wanted it to change as the dementia progresses. Building caregiver education and symptom-monitoring guidance into routine visits is a low-cost lever on medication safety.

**Caveats:** Very small sample (N=13), single region in Ontario, qualitative and self-reported, no control group — findings describe experiences rather than quantify them and may not generalize.

### 13. More Pills, More Risk: Each Added Medication Raised Odds of a Risky Prescription by 55%

**Journal:** *BMC Geriatr* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 22
**PMID:** 42934542 | **DOI:** 10.1186/s12877-026-08533-0

**The study:** Researchers reviewed records from 397 geriatric outpatients at a Turkish hospital, screening their medication lists against three widely used lists of drugs considered risky in later life — PRISCUS 2.0, STOPP version 3, and the Beers criteria. Combining all three screens, 58.7% of patients were taking at least one potentially inappropriate medication. In a fully adjusted model covering 362 patients, each additional drug was associated with 55% higher odds of having an inappropriate medication (OR 1.55), while better independence in daily activities (higher Katz ADL score) was associated with lower odds (OR 0.73). The three screening tools often disagreed about which drugs counted as inappropriate.

**Why it matters:** Nearly six in ten older outpatients carried at least one drug that experts flag as more likely to harm than help, and the risk climbed steeply with each extra prescription — making a periodic, full medication review one of the most concrete safety steps available in later life.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Bring every pill, patch, drop, and over-the-counter supplement you take to your next appointment — a literal bag, not a memory list — and ask your doctor or pharmacist to check the whole set against geriatric prescribing criteria. If you're on many medications, ask specifically whether any could be stopped or reduced, and don't stop anything on your own.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For care staff and family members, the finding that lower functional independence tracked with inappropriate prescribing is a flag: the residents and relatives who most need help managing daily life may also be the ones carrying the riskiest drug lists, and they are least able to advocate for a review themselves.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional and observational — it cannot show that the medications caused functional decline, or vice versa. Single-center, retrospective, conducted in Turkish geriatric outpatients, so results may not generalize elsewhere. The three screening tools gave notably different answers, which the authors themselves flag as a limitation of the field.

### 14. What Actually Predicts a Serious Fall? Hip Strength and Water Pills Topped the List

**Journal:** *Arch Gerontol Geriatr* | **Published:** 2026 Nov
**PMID:** 42296796 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.archger.2026.106333

**The study:** Australian researchers followed 952 adults aged 65 and over from the Geelong Osteoporosis Study, identifying injurious falls through linkage to a statewide emergency department dataset. Starting from a baseline prediction model using age, sex, mobility, walking aids and the Timed Up and Go test, they added modifiable risk factors one at a time to see which improved prediction. Diuretic (water pill) use and hip abductor strength produced the largest improvements, followed by smoking; notably, overall polypharmacy counts and fear-of-falling scores added almost nothing to prediction.

**Why it matters:** It suggests that counting the number of pills someone takes is a blunt tool for fall risk — the specific drug class matters more — and that a single, trainable muscle group around the hip carries real predictive weight.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you take a diuretic for blood pressure or heart failure, it's worth asking your prescriber at your next review whether the dose and timing are still right for you, given fall risk. Separately, ask a physiotherapist about hip abductor strengthening — side-lying leg raises and lateral band walks are the kind of exercise this study points toward.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living operators and care teams, this is an argument for moving beyond generic "high-risk medication count" flags in fall-prevention protocols toward drug-class-specific review plus targeted lateral hip strengthening in group exercise programming.

**Caveats:** Observational secondary analysis — it identifies predictors, not causes, and does not show that stopping diuretics or strengthening hips reduces falls. Single Australian region; improvements in model discrimination were modest in absolute terms. Falls were captured only when they reached an emergency department, missing less severe events.

### 15. Dementia Patients and Carers Say Yes to AI in Medication Reviews — With Conditions

**Journal:** *Age Ageing* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 31
**PMID:** 42969554 | **DOI:** 10.1093/ageing/afag320

**The study:** UK researchers interviewed 12 people living with dementia who were taking multiple medications and ran two focus groups with 14 carers, exploring their experiences of polypharmacy, their views of NHS Structured Medication Reviews, and their attitudes toward using generative AI to support those reviews. Participants described carers doing much of the work of tracking adherence and spotting side effects, and said reviews felt worthwhile mainly when the clinician was empathetic and offered personalized advice. Most were open to AI assistance — seeing it as a way to cut paperwork and free clinicians for person-centered conversation — but only if tools were well validated, supervised by a human, and secure with their data.

**Why it matters:** As health systems move to deploy AI in prescribing and review workflows, this is early direct evidence of what the people affected actually want: AI as a backstage assistant, never a replacement for the conversation.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or someone you care for is offered a structured medication review, you can ask two fair questions: whether any AI tool was used to prepare the recommendations, and how the clinician verified them. You are entitled to a human discussion about what each medicine is still for.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Carers in this study described themselves as the de facto side-effect monitors — a role that is rarely formally acknowledged. Care organizations rolling out AI-assisted medication review should design the process so carer observations are an explicit input, not an afterthought.

**Caveats:** Small qualitative study (12 interviews, 14 carers in focus groups) with no quantitative outcomes; self-reported views only. UK NHS context and self-selected participants may not represent people with more advanced dementia or those without engaged carers. Findings describe attitudes, not whether AI-assisted reviews actually improve prescribing.

### 16. What Helps Doctors Safely Stop Medications at the End of Life — a Review of 23 Studies

**Journal:** *Palliat Med* | **Published:** 2026 Oct
**PMID:** 42533730 | **DOI:** 10.1177/02692163261465769

**The study:** Dutch researchers conducted a qualitative systematic review of 23 studies examining what drives — and blocks — deprescribing for community-dwelling patients with palliative care needs, drawing on the perspectives of patients, caregivers and primary care clinicians. They identified four interacting layers: people's starting attitudes toward stopping medicines, the quality of the deprescribing conversation itself (individualized deliberation, shared decision-making, tailored communication), organizational factors like teamwork and systematic work processes, and wider system conditions. The authors conclude that deprescribing succeeds through a multidimensional strategy rather than any single tool or checklist.

**Why it matters:** Many people near the end of life continue taking preventive medications — statins, bone drugs, tight glucose control — whose benefits take years to arrive, and this review maps exactly where those conversations break down.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If your priorities have shifted toward comfort and quality of life, you can open the conversation yourself: ask your GP which of your medicines are meant to prevent problems years from now, and whether any could be stopped to reduce side effects and pill burden. This review found those conversations go best when they are individualized and shared, not delivered as a verdict.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and care teams, the review highlights that deprescribing often stalls on organizational factors — unclear ownership between GP, specialist and pharmacist. Asking "who is responsible for reviewing the whole list?" can be more useful than debating individual drugs.

**Caveats:** Qualitative evidence synthesis — it describes influences and perceptions, not measured outcomes, and cannot quantify how much deprescribing improves quality of life. Included studies vary in setting and quality; several review authors had co-authored papers included in the synthesis.

### 17. Pelvic Fractures Look More Like Hip Fractures Than We Treat Them — 7,120-Patient National Study

**Journal:** *Osteoporos Int* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 5
**PMID:** 42832039 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s00198-026-08221-0

**The study:** Using national claims and population data covering everyone in the Netherlands aged 50 and over diagnosed with a pelvic or acetabular fracture in 2019, researchers compared 6,091 pelvic with 1,029 acetabular fracture patients. Pelvic fracture patients were older (mean 77 vs 74), overwhelmingly female (79% vs 36%), and had higher pre-injury rates of osteoporosis, connective tissue disease and polypharmacy. Despite being far less likely to be admitted (35% vs 53%) or operated on (4.4% vs 28%), their one-year mortality was comparable (15% vs 17%), and the authors argue pelvic fractures should be counted among major osteoporotic fractures.

**Why it matters:** A pelvic fracture is often managed conservatively and sent home, yet roughly one in seven of these patients died within a year — a signal that this injury deserves the same osteoporosis workup and follow-up that a hip fracture triggers.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or someone close to you is treated for a pelvic fracture and sent home without surgery, ask whether a bone health assessment, fracture-prevention treatment and a medication review have been arranged. Don't assume that "no surgery needed" means "no follow-up needed."
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living and home care teams, this is a case for treating a pelvic fracture as a frailty event: the patients carried more osteoporosis and more polypharmacy before the injury, and conservative management may mean they slip through fracture liaison service pathways built around hip fractures.

**Caveats:** Retrospective analysis of administrative claims data — no information on fracture severity, functional outcomes or cause of death, and coding errors are possible. Observational design cannot establish causation. Single country (Netherlands) and a single year (2019), so care pathways may differ elsewhere.

### 18. Not All Pills Carry Equal Risk: Japanese Study Separates Dangerous Drugs From Simply Numerous Ones

**Journal:** *Drugs Aging* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 26 (online ahead of print)
**PMID:** 42931882 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40266-026-01344-3

**The study:** Using a large national administrative claims database in Japan, researchers ran a population-based matched case-control analysis comparing several ways of measuring medication risk in older adults: the number of anticholinergic drugs, total anticholinergic burden, number of potentially inappropriate medications (PIMs), and the raw count of medications. Drugs with anticholinergic properties and PIMs showed stronger associations with hip fracture and dementia than the simple total number of medications. For constipation and urinary retention, all three measures performed about the same.

**Why it matters:** It suggests that which medications an older person takes may matter more for the most feared outcomes — broken hips and dementia — than how many they take, shifting the conversation from pill counting to pill scrutiny.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you take several daily medications, consider asking your doctor or pharmacist for a review that specifically flags anticholinergic drugs — common in older sleep aids, bladder medications, antihistamines, and some antidepressants — rather than focusing only on how long your list is. Do not stop anything on your own.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For care teams and senior living medication reviewers, this supports targeting anticholinergic burden scales and PIM lists in fall-prevention and cognitive-decline programs, instead of relying on a blunt "five or more medications" trigger.

**Caveats:** Observational claims-database analysis — cannot establish causation; relies on prescription records rather than verified ingestion; conducted entirely in Japan, where prescribing patterns and the Japanese Anticholinergic Risk Scale may not translate directly to other countries.

### 23. Your Medication Count Is Probably Higher Than Your Record Shows — Patches, Creams and Eye Drops Add Up

**Journal:** *Int J Clin Pharm* | **Published:** 2026 Oct;48(5):2158-2168
**PMID:** 42536289 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11096-026-02194-0

### 24. What Makes Families Open to Stopping a Nursing Home Resident's Medications

**Journal:** *BMC Geriatr* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 1;26(1):1276

### 25. Stopping Blood Pressure Pills in Care Homes: The Evidence Is Thinner Than You'd Hope

**Journal:** *Drugs Aging* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 16 (online ahead of print)
**PMID:** 42888381 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40266-026-01342-5

**The study:** Researchers systematically searched for randomized trials of planned antihypertensive deprescribing in institutional long-term care and found just three studies covering 1,548 residents. The trials differed so much in population, strategy, monitoring, and follow-up that the authors judged pooling them inappropriate. Individually, supervised withdrawal did what it was designed to do — in DANTON it cut antihypertensive drugs by 1.2 per resident and raised systolic pressure by 11 mmHg at 16 weeks — but effects on death, hospitalization, falls, cognition, function, and quality of life remain unresolved, with one trial showing a neutral mortality signal and another a non-significant hazard ratio of 1.65.

**Why it matters:** Blood pressure medications are among the most commonly targeted drugs for deprescribing in frail care home residents, yet this review shows the randomized evidence cannot tell us whether stopping them helps, harms, or does neither on the outcomes residents care about.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If a clinician proposes reducing your blood pressure medicines, that can be a reasonable individualized decision — but ask what the plan is for monitoring your pressure afterward and under what circumstances the drug would be restarted, because the research shows outcomes depend heavily on how the withdrawal is supervised.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For long-term care medical directors and nursing staff: this review argues against applying a single blanket deprescribing rule, and in favor of following a specific, documented protocol with defined monitoring and restart criteria — the element that differed most across the trials.

**Caveats:** Systematic review of only three heterogeneous randomized trials; no meta-analysis was possible. Outcome data for falls, hospitalization, cognition, and quality of life were sparse or at risk of bias. Follow-up ranged from 32 weeks to about three years. Generative AI was used for language editing of the manuscript.

## Research Trends & Continuity

Nine studies appear in both digests — the frailty transition cohort (PMID 42669283), pelvic fractures (PMID 42832039), botulinum toxin for depression (PMID 42669382), the JAMA national snapshot (PMID 42783349), the non-oral medication count (PMID 42536289), the qualitative deprescribing review (PMID 42533730), the deprescribing tools review (PMID 42799555), fear of falling (PMID 42628444) and the hospice nursing review (PMID 42519959). The genuine movement is in what has been added around them.

**Convergent findings**
- **Medication burden concentrates in the frailest, and brain-active drugs lead the climb.** The carried-over frailty cohort, *Four Years of Tracking Frailty* (PMID 42669283), linked sedative and antidepressant-type drugs to worsening frailty. The new NHANES analysis, *Americans Over 65 Are Taking More Pills Than Ever — and the Frailest Are Taking the Most* (PMID 42870717), supplies the national trend behind it: central nervous system and psychotherapeutic drugs rose fastest among the frailest adults over two decades. Together with the previous digest's Chinese prescription audit (PMID 42594508) and German/Austrian psychiatric prescribing survey (PMID 42503320), the psychoactive-drug signal is now consistent across four countries.
- **Deprescribing at the end of life remains achievable but unmeasured.** The previous run's conclusion from PMIDs 42533730, 42519959 and 42799555 is reinforced by *Stopping Blood Pressure Pills in Care Homes* (PMID 42888381), which found only three randomized trials, too heterogeneous to pool, and no resolution on death, falls, function or quality of life.
- **Medication count still earns its keep as a trigger.** Last run flagged this (PMIDs 42594508, 42701177, 42628444). The new batch adds *More Pills, More Risk* (PMID 42934542), where each additional drug raised the odds of a potentially inappropriate prescription by 55%, and *One in Five Hospital Readmissions in Rural Australia Traced Back to Medications* (PMID 42842153), with 7% higher odds of a medication-related readmission per drug.

**Advances**
- **From "the conversation never happens" to "here is what happens when it does."** The previous digest mapped why deprescribing stalls. This batch shows the execution: *Nearly All Heart Failure Drugs Are Stopped When Patients With Dementia Enter Hospice* (PMID 42897830) documents 91%–100% discontinuation regardless of diagnosis, with no symptom outcomes tracked, and *In Palliative Care, Preventive Pills Get Dropped — But Often Abruptly, Without Tapering* (PMID 42731737) finds beta-blockers, proton pump inhibitors and antidepressants stopped cold. The problem is no longer only inertia; it is also reflexiveness.
- **From counting to characterizing.** The previous run's non-oral counting study (PMID 42536289) started this. Now *Not All Pills Carry Equal Risk* (PMID 42931882) shows anticholinergic burden and inappropriate-medication counts beat raw totals for predicting hip fracture and dementia, and *Taking More Drugs Than Your Conditions Call For* (PMID 42885111) replaces the count with a score comparing drugs used against drugs the person's disease mix would predict.
- **Who owns the medication list.** The previous digest's assisted living accountability gap (PMID 42645013) now has a workforce measurement behind it: *Survey of Nearly 5,000 European Clinicians* (PMID 42969553) found pharmacists scored highest on polypharmacy knowledge at a modest 3.51 out of 5, with more than a third rating geriatric topics as low relevance to their own role.

**Counterpoints**
- **Polypharmacy as a cause of cognitive decline takes a hit.** *In Black Older Adults, Disease Burden — Not Pill Count — Tracked With Weaker Thinking Scores* (PMID 42842789) found no independent association for a five-or-more-drug threshold once chronic disease burden was accounted for, cutting against the assumption behind much of the previous digest's framing.
- **Blood pressure drugs get partially exonerated for falls.** *Not All "Fall Risk" Drugs Carry Equal Risk* (PMID 42308749) found antihypertensives were not associated with fall injuries, while antidepressants, anticonvulsants, opioids, antihistamines and muscle relaxants were — complicating the antihypertensive signal in PMID 42669283. *What Actually Predicts a Serious Fall?* (PMID 42296796) splits the difference: diuretics specifically did predict injurious falls, while overall polypharmacy counts added almost nothing.

**New themes**
- **Artificial intelligence enters the medication-review conversation** with nothing comparable in the previous digest: *AI Chatbots Aren't Ready to Assess Older Adults' Mental Health* (PMID 42786110), *Dementia Patients and Carers Say Yes to AI in Medication Reviews — With Conditions* (PMID 42969554) and *Geriatrics Society Draws Lines on AI in Elder Care* (PMID 42478489).
- **The family caregiver's own account of medication work**, newly measured rather than inferred: *Dementia Caregivers Say the Hardest Part of Managing Pills Isn't the Pills* (PMID 42897099) and *What Makes Families Open to Stopping a Nursing Home Resident's Medications* (PMID 42850595).

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** A striking share of this batch argues that the number everyone uses — how many pills someone takes — is the wrong number. *Not All Pills Carry Equal Risk* (PMID 42931882) found anticholinergic burden and inappropriate-medication lists outperformed raw counts for predicting hip fracture and dementia; *Not All "Fall Risk" Drugs Carry Equal Risk* (PMID 42308749) cleared antihypertensives while implicating antihistamines, muscle relaxants and opioids; *What Actually Predicts a Serious Fall?* (PMID 42296796) found overall polypharmacy counts added almost nothing to fall prediction while diuretic use and hip strength did; *In Black Older Adults, Disease Burden — Not Pill Count — Tracked With Weaker Thinking Scores* (PMID 42842789) found no independent effect of a five-drug threshold on cognition; and *Taking More Drugs Than Your Conditions Call For* (PMID 42885111) proposes measuring medication intensity against a person's actual disease mix instead. Meanwhile *Your Medication Count Is Probably Higher Than Your Record Shows* (PMID 42536289) shows the count isn't even accurate, since patches, creams and eye drops are routinely left out.

**Why pitch this now:** "Five or more medications" is the threshold printed on care-plan forms, quality measures and consumer checklists, and within a single quarter five independent teams reported that it predicts less than assumed — while naming specific drug classes that predict a great deal.

**Angle:** A practical piece for older adults and the families who help manage their medicines, replacing "how long is your list?" with "which names are on it?" — what an anticholinergic is, why an over-the-counter allergy or sleep tablet can matter more than a fifth prescription, and how to ask for a review that looks at drug classes rather than totals. Secondary value for senior living clinical leaders whose fall-prevention and medication-review protocols still trigger on a number.

**Potential headlines:**
- Five or More Pills Isn't the Risk Line Researchers Once Thought
- Your Blood Pressure Pills May Not Be the Fall Risk — the Allergy Tablet Might Be
- Counting Your Pills Won't Protect Your Independence, but Knowing Which Ones to Question Might

**Potential outlets:**
- **SeniorLiving.org** — their consumer guidance format suits a "what to ask your pharmacist" piece built around drug classes rather than a pill tally, and the OTC angle reaches readers managing medicines at home.
- **McKnight's Senior Living** — assisted living fall-prevention and medication-review protocols are typically built on a count-based flag; this is a direct argument for redesigning that trigger.
- **McKnight's Long-Term Care News** — anticholinergic burden scales, inappropriate-medication screening and the thin evidence on antihypertensive withdrawal in care homes are squarely a clinical-operations story for skilled nursing.
- **Managed Healthcare Executive** — polypharmacy quality measures and pharmacy benefit flags lean on the five-drug threshold, so a piece on better risk stratification speaks directly to their payer and population-health readership.
