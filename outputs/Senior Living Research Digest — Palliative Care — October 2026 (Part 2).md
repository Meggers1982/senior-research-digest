### 1. Mapping What Actually Eases Pain at the End of Life for Older Adults

**Journal:** *Ageing Res Rev* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 18
**PMID:** 42893776 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.arr.2026.103423

**The study:** Researchers reviewed 117 studies of pain management strategies for older adults receiving palliative care, sorting them into opioids, non-opioid drugs, procedures, non-drug approaches, education, and multi-part programs. Pain reduction was most consistently reported for procedural interventions (such as nerve blocks or local anesthesia), opioids, and multifaceted interventions; favorable results also showed up for cannabis, music or massage therapy, exercise, and biopsychosocial or care-organization approaches. But 71% of studies focused on cancer patients, most participants averaged only 65 to 74 years old, and 68% of the evidence was rated moderate to low quality.

**Why it matters:** It gives older adults and their families a realistic menu of pain options beyond opioids alone — while showing that the people most likely to need palliative pain care, the oldest-old and those with non-cancer illnesses like heart failure or dementia, have barely been studied.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If pain is part of your serious illness, you can ask your palliative care team about more than one tool at a time — combinations of medications, a nerve block, and non-drug options like music or massage therapy were all among the approaches with reported benefit. It is reasonable to ask how much evidence supports any option for someone your age and with your specific diagnosis.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living and hospice staff: the review highlights that 'multifaceted' approaches — combining medication with care organization and biopsychosocial support — were among the most consistently favorable, suggesting pain protocols built around a single drug class may be leaving comfort on the table.

**Caveats:** Scoping review, not a meta-analysis — it maps evidence rather than proving which strategy works best; 68% of included studies were moderate to low quality; heavily skewed toward cancer patients and the 'younger old'; funded in part by the Canadian Institutes of Health Research and Quebec Pain Research Network.

### 2. Older Cancer Patients Got Lower Opioid Doses — and Had the Same Pain Relief

**Journal:** *Drugs Aging* | **Published:** 2026 Oct
**PMID:** 42853544 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40266-026-01328-3

**The study:** Italian researchers analyzed 113 patients with advanced cancer admitted to an acute palliative care unit, split into under 65 (n=46), 65 to 75 (n=43), and over 75 (n=24). Patients under 65 ended up on significantly higher opioid doses and more complex regimens — methadone was used mostly in the younger group — and received higher rescue doses for breakthrough pain. Yet pain intensity improved significantly from admission to discharge in all three age groups, with no difference between them.

**Why it matters:** It counters the common fear that older patients are automatically undertreated for pain: in a specialist unit, careful lower-dose titration delivered equal comfort, suggesting dose size matters less than how attentively it is adjusted.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or your doctor are worried that a lower opioid dose means you will be left in pain, this study suggests it does not have to: what mattered was frequent reassessment and adjustment. Ask your team how often your pain will be re-evaluated and what the plan is for breakthrough pain, rather than focusing on the number on the prescription.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families advocating for a relative in palliative care: a smaller dose is not evidence of age bias on its own — but the absence of regular re-titration would be. Ask what the pain score was at admission and what it is now.

**Caveats:** Small N (113, with only 24 in the oldest group); single-center in Italy; secondary analysis of an observational study, so it cannot prove lower doses cause equal outcomes; all patients were in a specialist acute palliative care unit, so results may not generalize to nursing homes or general wards.

### 3. A Hospital Pharmacy Audit Found Preventive Pills Often Stopped Abruptly at the End of Life

**Journal:** *J Pain Symptom Manage* | **Published:** 2026 Dec
**PMID:** 42731737 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jpainsymman.2026.09.009

**The study:** Researchers tracked medications at five timepoints for 386 adults admitted to an Australian metropolitan palliative care unit in 2023 (median age 78, interquartile range 69 to 87). Polypharmacy was more common in patients admitted for symptom management and those with endocrine conditions, and less common in the terminal phase and in patients aged 80 and over. Preventive medications were frequently discontinued, symptom-directed medications increased over time — but drugs that are generally supposed to be tapered, including proton pump inhibitors, beta-blockers, and antidepressants, were often stopped outright with no observed dose reduction first.

**Why it matters:** Stopping certain medications abruptly can cause rebound symptoms, withdrawal, or discomfort at precisely the moment comfort is the whole point of care — and this audit suggests it happens routinely.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** When your care goals shift toward comfort, it is fair to ask which of your medications are being stopped and whether any of them need to be tapered rather than halted — antidepressants, beta-blockers, and stomach-acid drugs are the common ones that can cause problems if dropped suddenly.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and senior living nurses: a formal medication review at each transition — admission, day five, and the shift to the terminal phase — is a concrete thing to request, and this study shows tapering plans are the step most likely to be skipped.

**Caveats:** Retrospective and observational, single palliative care unit in Melbourne; chart-based, so a taper may sometimes have occurred before admission without being documented; cannot show whether abrupt cessation caused harm.

### 4. Letting ICU Nurses Trigger Palliative Care Referrals Nearly Tripled Consults — and Code Blues Fell

**Journal:** *J Hosp Palliat Nurs* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 21
**PMID:** 42904749 | **DOI:** 10.1097/NJH.0000000000001276

**The study:** A New Jersey medical center introduced a protocol in which ICU nurses screened every admitted patient against standardized palliative care referral criteria and triggered a consult when criteria were met. Palliative care consults rose from 179 in 2023 to 471 in 2024 (a 163% increase) and 435 in 2025. Code blues in the ICU dropped from 33 to 26 in 2024 and 29 in 2025, and 87% of ICU staff said the screening process positively affected their practice.

**Why it matters:** Delayed palliative care in intensive care means untreated symptoms, unwanted invasive procedures, and missed hospice time — this shows a simple nurse-driven checklist can unlock access without waiting for a physician to think of it.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or a loved one lands in intensive care, you do not have to wait to be offered a palliative care consult — you can ask for one directly, and ask whether the hospital screens every ICU patient for it. Palliative care is about symptom control and clarifying your wishes; it is not the same as giving up on treatment.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living operators and hospital leaders: this is a low-cost, replicable quality improvement model — a standardized screening tool in nursing hands — with measurable effects on consults and resuscitation events, and strong staff buy-in.

**Caveats:** Quality improvement project at a single medical center with no control group; before-and-after design means other changes over the same period could explain the results; the drop in code blues is not proven to be caused by the protocol; staff satisfaction is self-reported.

### 5. Noisy Breathing at the Bedside: 1 in 4 Bereaved Families Show PTSD Symptoms After Witnessing It

**Journal:** *J Pain Symptom Manage* | **Published:** 2026 Dec;72(6):742-751
**PMID:** 42727789 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jpainsymman.2026.09.006

**The study:** Researchers surveyed 1,068 bereaved family members of people who died of cancer at 140 palliative care units and inpatient hospices across Japan, asking about two common end-of-life breathing changes: death rattle (gurgling from airway secretions) and agonal breathing (irregular gasping in the final hours). Among the 311 who recalled death rattle and 392 who recalled agonal breathing, about 27% of those who found the experience very distressing screened positive for PTSD symptoms, and higher distress roughly doubled the odds of a positive screen. Receiving an explanation from staff helped families accept the signs as part of natural dying, but did not by itself reduce PTSD screening rates.

**Why it matters:** These breathing changes are nearly universal at the end of life and are often dismissed as harmless to the patient — but this is some of the strongest evidence yet that what families see and hear at the bedside can leave lasting psychological marks.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you are planning your own end-of-life care or sitting vigil with a spouse or friend, you can ask the hospice team in advance what the last hours may look and sound like, whether noisy breathing is causing the patient distress, and what repositioning or medication options exist — and give yourself permission to step out of the room.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For hospice and senior living staff: a one-time explanation may not be enough. Consider checking back with families during and after the dying process, and building bereavement follow-up that specifically screens for intrusive memories of the death scene.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional, observational design that cannot establish causation; relies on self-reported recall after the death and a brief 3-item PTSD screening tool rather than clinical diagnosis; conducted entirely in Japanese inpatient hospices among cancer deaths, so cultural and setting differences may limit generalizability.

### 6. What Actually Makes or Breaks a Family Caregiver During Home Hospice

**Journal:** *J Pain Symptom Manage* | **Published:** 2026 Dec;72(6):e477-e490
**PMID:** 42680076 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jpainsymman.2026.08.016

**The study:** Researchers screened nearly 8,000 records and synthesized 19 qualitative studies from 13 countries to map what determines the well-being of family members caring for a dying relative at home. They found four interconnected layers: personal factors (coping skills, health literacy, prior caregiving experience), relational factors (relationship quality, family dynamics, social support), care demands (symptom management, nighttime needs, intensity), and system factors (service access, quality of interactions with clinicians, money, cultural expectations). The quality of the relationship with professional caregivers and the availability of comprehensive support emerged as pivotal.

**Why it matters:** Most home deaths depend on an unpaid family member, and this synthesis points to concrete fixes — a named point of contact, overnight support, caregiver-specific assessment — rather than vague advice to "take care of yourself."

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're planning to die at home, ask early who will support your family member overnight and who their single named contact will be when something goes wrong at 3 a.m. — the research suggests that answer shapes whether home care holds together.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For agencies and senior living operators: the findings argue for formally assessing the caregiver as a second client — across personal, relational, practical, and financial domains — and tailoring support to cultural expectations about family duty, not just handing over a symptom-management checklist.

**Caveats:** Qualitative evidence synthesis, not primary data; findings reflect themes across 19 studies of varying quality and cannot quantify how common or strong any determinant is; included studies span 13 countries with differing health systems, so applicability to any single setting varies.

### 7. Delirium Is Common at the End of Life — and Often Mistaken for Dementia

**Journal:** *J Hosp Palliat Nurs* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 14 (online ahead of print)
**PMID:** 42873521 | **DOI:** 10.1097/NJH.0000000000001261

**The study:** This clinical review synthesizes what is known about acute delirium in hospice and palliative care, where it is highly prevalent. The authors describe its three forms — hypoactive (quiet, withdrawn), hyperactive (agitated), and mixed — note that it fluctuates through the day and is frequently confused with dementia, and link it to longer hospital stays, higher costs, greater odds of institutionalization, and shorter survival. They argue for routine screening with validated tools and a non-drug-first approach: early mobility, hydration, sleep-wake regulation, and careful medication stewardship.

**Why it matters:** A sudden change in attention or awareness in an older adult is often treatable and is not the same as dementia progressing — recognizing the difference can change both the care plan and the final weeks of life.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If your thinking or alertness changes abruptly over hours or days — rather than gradually over months — ask your care team to screen for delirium and review your medication list, because the cause is often reversible even during palliative care.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and senior living staff: the quiet, withdrawn form of delirium is the one most often missed. Flag sudden fluctuating confusion to the nurse the same day, and support the basics — daylight, hydration, familiar faces, consistent sleep — that the evidence favors over sedating medication.

**Caveats:** This is a narrative clinical review, not original research; it presents no new patient data, effect sizes, or control group, and recommendations reflect expert synthesis of existing literature.

### 8. Talking About Grief Helps Cancer Doctors in Training — And Costs Almost Nothing

**Journal:** *J Palliat Med* | **Published:** 2026 Nov;29(11):1606-1609
**PMID:** 42634408 | **DOI:** 10.1177/10966218261483467

**The study:** At one large academic hematology/oncology fellowship program, a palliative care physician led four structured debriefing sessions for trainees, who completed a survey after each (43 surveys total). More than 80% reported professional grief in the prior six months and nearly half reported personal grief; 88% said the sessions gave them time and space to discuss grief, 79% reported benefit after just the first session, and every respondent wanted the sessions to continue.

**Why it matters:** Clinician burnout directly affects the quality and continuity of care older adults receive at the end of life, and this suggests a low-cost structural fix rather than asking individuals to be more resilient.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Little here calls for action on your part, but it's a reminder that the oncologist or hospice physician sitting with you is also carrying accumulated loss — and that programs supporting them are part of what keeps experienced clinicians at the bedside.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living and hospice administrators: a recurring, protected debriefing hour led by someone with palliative care expertise is an inexpensive, well-received model that could be adapted for nursing and direct-care staff facing repeated resident deaths.

**Caveats:** Pilot study with no control group, single-center, 43 surveys from a small fellowship cohort, and outcomes are entirely self-reported satisfaction and perceived benefit rather than validated burnout measures or longer-term retention.

### 9. When a Hospital Asks a Court to Take Over Your Decisions: What the Records Show

**Journal:** *J Am Geriatr Soc* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 26
**PMID:** 42929333 | **DOI:** 10.1111/jgs.70782

**The study:** Researchers reviewed county probate court records and medical charts for 236 adults aged 60 and older for whom a large academic health system filed guardianship petitions between January 2019 and September 2024. Of the 217 petitions actually heard in court, 215 (99%) ended with a guardian being appointed. Medicaid recipients were less likely to get a guardian they already knew personally, and people with less formal education were more likely to receive full rather than limited guardianship; more restrictive guardianship was linked to a lower chance of going home at discharge.

**Why it matters:** Guardianship strips away the right to make your own medical, financial, and housing decisions, and these data suggest that once a hospital files, the outcome is nearly automatic and that poorer, less-educated older adults fare worst.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** The single most powerful protection against hospital-initiated guardianship is naming your own decision-maker before a crisis: ask your doctor to help you complete a durable power of attorney for health care and a financial power of attorney, name a backup, and make sure your hospital has copies on file. If a guardianship petition is ever filed on your behalf, you have the right to legal representation and to argue for a limited rather than full guardianship.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For hospital social workers, discharge planners, and senior living staff: this study points to guardianship as a discharge tool with consequences — more restrictive guardianships were tied to fewer discharges home. Consider auditing how often your organization petitions, whether ethics and palliative care consults happen first, and whether less restrictive alternatives (supported decision-making, surrogate statutes, representative payees) were genuinely exhausted.

**Caveats:** Observational, retrospective chart and court-record review from a single academic health system in one state; cannot establish causation, and guardianship law and practice vary widely by state, so findings may not generalize.

### 10. Frailer Cancer Patients Are Getting Palliative Care Only After They're Hospitalized

**Journal:** *J Pain Symptom Manage* | **Published:** 2026 Dec
**PMID:** 42607784 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jpainsymman.2026.08.008

**The study:** Researchers analyzed electronic health records for 1,053 patients with advanced solid-tumor cancer who received palliative care at Parkland Health, a large safety-net system in Dallas, between 2018 and 2023 (mean age 57.7; 75% non-White). Nearly 70% had at least mild frailty. Patients with moderate-to-severe frailty had roughly 3.5 times the odds of first encountering palliative care as a hospital inpatient consultation rather than an outpatient clinic referral, and 4.5 times the odds of only ever getting palliative care in the hospital.

**Why it matters:** The people most likely to benefit from early, planned palliative care — the frailest patients — appear to be reaching it only during an acute hospital stay, when there is less time for symptom control, goals-of-care conversations, and advance planning.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or someone you love has advanced cancer and has been slowing down — losing weight, falling, needing more help with daily tasks — don't wait for a hospitalization to meet the palliative care team. Ask your oncologist directly for an outpatient palliative care referral; it is not hospice, and it can run alongside active cancer treatment.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For clinicians and care navigators in safety-net and community settings: frailty measures already embedded in the chart could be used as an automatic trigger for outpatient palliative referral, rather than letting an emergency admission become the entry point. The findings also raise access questions — transportation, insurance, and clinic capacity — worth examining locally.

**Caveats:** Observational, single-system safety-net population with a relatively young mean age (57.7), so results may not generalize to typical older adult or insured populations; frailty was measured with an electronic index derived from billing and chart data; design cannot establish causation.

### 11. Why Families Change Their Minds About Dying at Home

**Journal:** *J Pain Symptom Manage* | **Published:** 2026 Dec
**PMID:** 42617989 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jpainsymman.2026.08.004

**The study:** Researchers systematically pooled findings from nine qualitative studies of palliative care patients and their caregivers who moved from home care to an inpatient setting near the end of life. Three themes emerged: what triggered the move (symptom crises, caregiver exhaustion, treatment preferences, and reluctance to actually die at home), worries about inpatient care (continuity, communication, comfort), and looking back — caregivers generally judged the transfer the right decision and described a calmer stretch of time with their loved one afterward. The authors conclude that preferred place of death is not a fixed choice but shifts with illness, caregiver capacity, and available services.

**Why it matters:** The widespread assumption that everyone wants to die at home can leave families feeling they have failed when a transfer becomes necessary — this synthesis reframes the move as a legitimate, often relieving decision.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have said you want to die at home, it's worth saying out loud what should happen if symptoms become uncontrollable or your caregiver can no longer cope — give the people around you explicit permission to change the plan, and revisit that conversation as your illness changes.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For caregivers and senior living staff: the findings validate what many feel but rarely say — that a transfer to an inpatient unit can restore the relationship by taking the nursing work off the family member's shoulders. Framing transfer as a planned contingency rather than a failure, and addressing the specific fears identified (loss of continuity, poor communication, discomfort), can ease guilt.

**Caveats:** Qualitative evidence synthesis of only nine studies across varied health systems and cultures; findings describe experiences rather than measure outcomes and may not generalize to all settings.

### 12. Bedside Signs Still Beat Scoring Systems for Predicting the Final Days

**Journal:** *J Pain Symptom Manage* | **Published:** 2026 Dec
**PMID:** 42660371 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jpainsymman.2026.08.005

**The study:** Researchers reviewed records of 290 people who died on palliative care beds at a tertiary referral center, comparing three prognostic tools — the Palliative Prognostic Index, Palliative Performance Scale, and Short-Term Prognosis Signs — for predicting death within three days. In 166 cancer patients, combining PPI with observed clinical signs performed best (area under the curve 0.72), only modestly better than the bedside signs alone. In 124 noncancer patients, the tools performed similarly but modestly (AUC about 0.69), meaning accuracy remains limited for people dying of heart, lung, kidney, or neurologic disease.

**Why it matters:** Families routinely ask "how long?" and these data show that even the best available tools give clinicians only a rough estimate — especially outside of cancer.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you are told a loved one has "days" left, understand that even validated tools are only moderately accurate — ask what specific signs the team is seeing rather than fixating on a number, and make the visits, calls, and goodbyes you want sooner rather than later.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For hospice and senior living staff: this supports using observable clinical signs at the bedside as a practical core of short-term prognostication while being transparent with families about uncertainty, and it highlights how much weaker the evidence base is for noncancer dying trajectories common in long-term care.

**Caveats:** Retrospective, single-center study of patients who had already died on a palliative care unit; modest sample sizes in each group; discrimination was only moderate and the authors call for prospective validation.

### 13. Pancreatic Cancer Patients Say Palliative Care Was Explained to Them Badly — or Not at All

**Journal:** *J Hosp Palliat Nurs* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 8
**PMID:** 42842360 | **DOI:** 10.1097/NJH.0000000000001272

**The study:** Researchers conducted semistructured interviews in 2024–2025 with 21 adults in Southern California — patients with advanced pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma and their family caregivers, many of Latino background — about what made their care feel effective and compassionate. Four themes emerged: a diagnostic journey marked by delays and sustained by family and faith; navigation barriers including financial strain and difficult interactions with staff; end-of-life and palliative care conversations that were frequently misunderstood; and a call for holistic, practical, culturally responsive support.

**Why it matters:** A recurring finding is that patients and families misread what palliative care is, which delays symptom relief and support that could be running alongside treatment.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If a clinician raises "palliative care" and you hear "giving up," stop and ask them to spell out exactly what the team would do for you — pain and nausea control, nutrition, financial and transportation help, family support — and whether you can keep receiving cancer treatment at the same time. Bring a family member and ask for information in your preferred language.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For care teams and family caregivers: the interviews point to concrete fixes — plain-language explanations of palliative care, bilingual and culturally matched navigators, upfront discussion of financial strain, and acknowledging the role of faith and extended family in decision-making.

**Caveats:** Small exploratory qualitative study (N=21) at a single health system in Southern California with a largely Latino sample; findings are experiential, not generalizable statistics, and no comparison group.

### 14. Letting Nurses Make the Referral Nearly Tripled Palliative Care Consults on One Hospital Unit

**Journal:** *J Hosp Palliat Nurs* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 9
**PMID:** 42853191 | **DOI:** 10.1097/NJH.0000000000001273

**The study:** A quality improvement project on a 31-bed adult medical-surgical unit trained 20 nurses to use a standardized screening tool for unmet palliative care needs during admission and transfer assessments. Referrals rose from 3 in the 8 weeks before implementation to 8 in the 8 weeks after, and the palliative care team judged every one of the new referrals appropriate. Nurses' knowledge scores improved substantially after the education session.

**Why it matters:** Palliative care consults often come late because nurses who spot unmet needs wait for a physician to act; a simple screening checklist appears to close part of that gap without generating inappropriate referrals.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Nurses often notice uncontrolled pain, breathlessness, or distress before anyone else does — if you are hospitalized and struggling with symptoms, you or your family can ask any member of the care team, including your nurse, to request a palliative care consultation.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For nurse leaders and senior living clinical directors: a brief education session plus a structured screening prompt at admission and transfer is a low-cost, testable change that strengthened nursing advocacy and produced referrals the specialist team deemed appropriate.

**Caveats:** Small single-unit quality improvement project with no control group, tiny absolute numbers (3 versus 8 referrals over 8 weeks), very short follow-up, and knowledge measured by self-administered testing; results may not generalize to other units or facilities.

### 15. For Agitated Delirium at the End of Life, Lorazepam Hit Families' Comfort Goals Better Than Haloperidol Alone

**Journal:** *J Pain Symptom Manage* | **Published:** 2026 Dec
**PMID:** 42586325 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jpainsymman.2026.08.003

**The study:** In a planned secondary analysis of the multicenter, double-blind RECORD randomized trial, researchers asked caregivers of patients with advanced cancer and persistent agitation to define a "personalized sedation goal" — how calm or alert they wanted their loved one to be — and then compared four treatments: escalated haloperidol, lorazepam, both combined, or placebo. Among 67 patients with caregiver-defined goals, treated in acute palliative care units in the United States and Taiwan, the goal was met at 24 hours in 77% of the lorazepam group and 62% of the combination group, versus just 21% with haloperidol alone (lorazepam vs. haloperidol odds ratio 12.2). Placebo achieved the goal 54% of the time.

**Why it matters:** Agitated delirium in the final days is one of the most distressing things families witness, and this is direct randomized evidence that the drug choice — and the family's own stated comfort goal — meaningfully changes whether that distress is relieved.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you are thinking ahead about your own end-of-life care, know that "how awake versus how settled do I want to be if I become agitated" is a question you can answer in advance. Raising it with your clinician or writing it into an advance directive gives your family and care team a target to aim for rather than a guess.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and hospice staff: this trial validates a practical tool — asking the caregiver to define the sedation goal up front — and shows that haloperidol alone frequently fails to reach it. Caregivers can reasonably ask the palliative team what medication plan is being used and whether a benzodiazepine-based regimen has been considered when agitation persists.

**Caveats:** Small sample (67 patients analyzed, roughly 13-14 per treatment arm, from an original 111 enrolled), secondary analysis rather than the trial's primary endpoint, conducted in specialized acute palliative care units that may not reflect nursing home or home hospice settings, and short 24-hour outcome window.

### 16. Most Patients With Advanced Cancer Change Opioids at Least Once — and Doses Roughly Double Each Time

**Journal:** *J Pain Symptom Manage* | **Published:** 2026 Dec
**PMID:** 42607783 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jpainsymman.2026.08.006

**The study:** Australian researchers tracked complete opioid prescribing records for 200 adults with advanced cancer from diagnosis through death. Fifty-eight percent switched opioids at least once, and some switched up to four times; the starting dose at each successive switch approximately doubled (20 mg morphine-equivalent for the first opioid, then 30, 60, and 180 mg), while the time spent on each drug grew shorter (39 days, then 25, then 18). Inadequate pain relief drove 58% of switches, and palliative care specialists initiated a rising share of them — 31% of first opioids but 89% of third switches.

**Why it matters:** It normalizes something patients and families often read as failure: needing to change pain medicines, sometimes repeatedly, is the usual trajectory in advanced cancer, not a sign that something went wrong.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If your pain medicine stops working as well as it used to, that is common and expected — not a sign you are "addicted" or have run out of options. Ask whether switching to a different opioid is worth trying, and ask for a referral to specialist palliative care, since these were the clinicians handling the most complex switches in this study.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and care home staff: escalating doses and more frequent medication changes late in the illness reflect a documented pattern of disease progression rather than overprescribing. Knowing that the interval between changes shortens can help you anticipate when to push for a palliative care review rather than waiting for the next crisis.

**Caveats:** Retrospective observational cohort — describes patterns but cannot show that switching causes better pain control; single health service in Melbourne, Australia; prescribing practices and opioid availability differ substantially by country; findings apply to advanced cancer and may not generalize to non-cancer pain in older adults.

### 17. Linking Nursing Homes to Palliative Care Teams Cut Hospital Trips and Eased Symptoms, Review Finds

**Journal:** *Palliat Med* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 26
**PMID:** 42929149 | **DOI:** 10.1177/02692163261485105

**The study:** Australian researchers systematically reviewed 32 studies (37 publications) published between 2000 and August 2025 that tested formal ways of connecting specialist palliative care services with aged care providers. Three strategies emerged: designated "linkage workers" who bridge the two sectors, structured communication pathways, and collaborative care processes — with twelve distinct models of care combining elements of all three. Reported outcomes included stronger interprofessional communication, higher staff confidence, reduced use of acute hospital care, and less symptom distress for residents near the end of life, though outcomes reported directly by residents and family carers were sparse.

**Why it matters:** Older adults in residential aged care are consistently underserved by palliative care, and this review points to concrete organizational arrangements — not just more training — that appear to keep dying residents more comfortable and out of the emergency department.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you are choosing a care home or assisted living community, ask a specific question: what is the formal arrangement with a palliative care service? Look for a named link nurse or liaison, set communication channels, and joint case reviews — not just a promise that "we can call someone."
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living operators and administrators: the review identifies three replicable building blocks — a designated linkage worker, defined communication pathways, and shared care processes — and documents reduced acute care utilization as a system-level return. For family caregivers, it is a reminder to ask who coordinates between the facility and outside specialists when a resident's condition declines.

**Caveats:** Systematic review of heterogeneous studies of varying quality rather than new primary data; resident- and carer-reported outcomes such as quality of life were minimally measured; studies drawn from varied health systems that may not map onto US nursing home or assisted living regulation.

### 18. Feeding Tube Decisions in Advanced Cancer Hinge on a Narrow 'Window of Opportunity,' Clinicians Say

**Journal:** *Palliat Med* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 23
**PMID:** 42922280 | **DOI:** 10.1177/02692163261496702

**The study:** Researchers interviewed 21 UK healthcare professionals — dietitians, clinical nurse specialists, and speech and language therapists — about how they manage nutrition in patients with advanced head and neck cancer, a group at high risk of malnutrition because of tumor location and treatment side effects. Four themes emerged: the need for individualized nutritional care; complex feeding tube decisions shaped by prognosis, patient preference, and a time-limited "window of opportunity" when a tube can still be safely placed; mismatched expectations between patients and their families; and nutritional goals that shift as disease progresses, moving from maintaining weight toward comfort and pleasure in eating.

**Why it matters:** Families often experience food and feeding as the central battleground of late-stage illness, and this study names the specific tensions — timing, differing expectations, and changing goals — that make those conversations so hard.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If a feeding tube has been mentioned, ask directly about timing: clinicians describe a limited window when placement is still feasible, and delaying a decision can close options. Ask too what the goal of feeding is now — weight maintenance, comfort, or the enjoyment of eating — because that goal legitimately changes as illness advances.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and caregivers: professionals in this study repeatedly described gaps between what patients want and what relatives expect around eating and tube feeding. Naming that difference out loud — and asking the dietitian or nurse specialist to mediate — may prevent food from becoming a source of conflict at the bedside.

**Caveats:** Small qualitative study (N=21) of clinicians only — no patients or family carers were interviewed; UK-based and specific to advanced head and neck cancer, so findings may not generalize to dementia-related feeding decisions or other populations; descriptive design cannot establish which approaches produce better outcomes.

### 19. When Patients Say They Want to Die, Loss of Meaning — Not Pain — Is the Strongest Signal

**Journal:** *J Palliat Med* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 22
**PMID:** 42918739 | **DOI:** 10.1177/10966218261500244

**The study:** Researchers at a university hospital in Argentina interviewed 100 consecutive patients with stage IV cancer who were under active palliative care follow-up between January 2024 and December 2025. Using a structured interview, 35% expressed some wish to hasten death, though most scored at the low end of severity and were not actively planning anything. Patients with a stronger wish to hasten death had higher overall symptom burden (median ESAS-r 41 vs. 31) and far higher rates of demoralization (62.9% vs. 23.1%); in the statistical model, demoralization — a sense of meaninglessness, hopelessness and lost dignity — was the only independent predictor (odds ratio 6.56).

**Why it matters:** A stated wish to die is common in advanced illness and is more often a signal of eroded meaning and feeling like a burden than a settled decision — meaning it can be responded to with dignity-focused and existential support, not just more medication.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If thoughts of wanting your illness to end faster have crossed your mind, you are far from alone — roughly one in three people in this study felt the same, and most were not planning anything. Saying it out loud to your palliative care team opens the door to help with the things that actually drive it: feeling useless, feeling like a burden, losing a sense of purpose.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and hospice staff, this reframes a frightening statement as information rather than an emergency: ask what has changed in the person's sense of meaning or dignity, and press the care team for a formal demoralization or existential assessment rather than assuming the problem is purely physical.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional observational design at a single Argentine university hospital with N=100, so no causal conclusions; all participants had stage IV cancer, and findings may not generalize to non-cancer illness, other cultures, or very old frail adults. Wish to hasten death and symptom burden were self-reported.

### 20. Advance Care Planning Conversations in ALS Deliver What Patients Asked For — 91% of the Time

**Journal:** *J Pain Symptom Manage* | **Published:** 2026 Dec
**PMID:** 42586324 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jpainsymman.2026.08.002

**The study:** Italian researchers reviewed records for 1,219 people with ALS in a population-based register in Turin covering 2008 to 2020. Just over half (53.7%) had documented advance care planning discussions, and among those, what actually happened at the end of life matched the person's stated wishes 90.8% of the time. Advance care planning was linked to palliative care activation, feeding-tube placement and dying at home; it was less likely to happen in people with cognitive impairment or frontotemporal dementia, and rates rose over the decade from 51.6% to 61.9% after Italy's 2017 self-determination law.

**Why it matters:** It is rare to see hard evidence that documented conversations actually translate into the care people said they wanted, and the finding that cognitive impairment cuts people out of the process argues for having these talks early, before thinking changes.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you are living with a progressive illness, having a documented advance care planning conversation is the single most reliable way to make sure your preferences — including dying at home — are the ones carried out. Ask for that conversation earlier than feels necessary, while you can fully participate in it.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and care staff, the gap is the people with cognitive or behavioral changes, who were significantly less likely to have these discussions at all; flag anyone showing early thinking changes for a tailored, simplified planning conversation before the window closes.

**Caveats:** Retrospective, observational register study — it cannot prove that advance care planning caused the outcomes, since people who engage in planning may differ in other ways. Single Italian region with a well-organized ALS center and a specific national law, so results may not generalize to other health systems or to non-ALS populations.

### 21. The Words Hospice Staff Use for End-of-Life Confusion May Change the Treatment Patients Get

**Journal:** *Palliat Med* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 22
**PMID:** 42910992 | **DOI:** 10.1177/02692163261491837

**The study:** Researchers reanalyzed qualitative interviews with 25 clinicians — doctors, nurses, healthcare assistants and allied health professionals — at two UK hospices, examining how they described delirium, agitation and withdrawal in patients' final days. The word 'delirium' was rarely used, especially by non-medical staff, who described behavior instead. When staff did name delirium, decision-making about assessment and treatment was more transparent; when terms like 'terminal agitation' or 'confusion' were used, sedation was emphasized more heavily. Quiet, withdrawn (hypoactive) delirium was poorly recognized and almost never named or treated.

**Why it matters:** Delirium at the end of life often has reversible contributors such as pain, infection, constipation or medication, and the label a clinician reaches for can determine whether anyone looks for those causes or simply reaches for a sedative.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If a loved one — or you, in your own advance planning — may face confusion in the final days, it is reasonable to ask the care team directly: 'Is this delirium, and have you looked for a cause such as pain, infection or a medication?' Naming it appears to change what happens next.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For hospice and senior living staff, this is a prompt to standardize terminology across the whole team, not just physicians, and to specifically train for quiet, withdrawn delirium, which this study found was almost invisible in everyday conversation and care planning.

**Caveats:** Small qualitative study (25 clinicians) and a secondary analysis of data collected for another purpose; only two UK hospices, so findings may not generalize. It documents how clinicians talk, not measured patient outcomes, and cannot establish that terminology causes differences in care.

### 22. Agitation in the Final Days of Dementia Has No Agreed Definition — and No Validated Way to Measure It

**Journal:** *J Alzheimers Dis* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 16
**PMID:** 42885674 | **DOI:** 10.1177/13872877261496316

**The study:** Australian dementia and palliative care specialists reviewed what is known about terminal agitation — also called terminal restlessness or terminal delirium — in people dying with dementia. They report that no validated clinical tool exists to identify it, that clinicians use inconsistent terminology, and that it is frequently confused with the behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia or with untreated pain, leaving prevalence estimates all over the map. The authors lay out research priorities, starting with agreed diagnostic criteria and an assessment tool.

**Why it matters:** Families often witness distressing restlessness in the last days of a relative's dementia and are told it is simply 'part of dying,' when it may reflect untreated pain or delirium that could be identified and eased if clinicians had tools to tell the difference.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you are planning ahead for advanced dementia, it is worth writing into your care wishes that pain and delirium should be actively checked for in your final days, since this review shows agitation at the end of life is easily misread.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and dementia care staff, the practical takeaway is to document and describe the behavior you are seeing in detail — timing, triggers, possible pain — rather than accepting a vague label, because there is currently no standard test and your observations are the primary evidence clinicians have.

**Caveats:** This is a narrative review and commentary setting research priorities, not new primary data; it presents no patient-level findings, prevalence figures or treatment comparisons, and the authors are affiliated with a single Australian aged- and palliative-care provider (HammondCare).

## Research Trends & Continuity

Three studies carry over with identical PMIDs — the age-stratified opioid dosing analysis (PMID 42853544), the pancreatic cancer interview study (PMID 42842360), and the medical-surgical unit nurse screening project (PMID 42853191). The comparison below focuses on what is genuinely new.

**Convergent findings**

- *Nurse-triggered screening keeps producing referrals that specialists judge appropriate.* "Letting ICU Nurses Trigger Palliative Care Referrals Nearly Tripled Consults — and Code Blues Fell" (PMID 42904749) lands on the same mechanism as the previous digest's "When Nurses Are Empowered to Flag Patients, Palliative Referrals Rise" (PMID 42853191) and reinforces the lesson from "Flagged but Not Followed Up" (PMID 42860763): a screen works when a named person is authorized to act on it.
- *Organizational arrangements, not training alone, keep frail residents out of the hospital.* "Linking Nursing Homes to Palliative Care Teams Cut Hospital Trips and Eased Symptoms, Review Finds" (PMID 42929149) generalizes the single-system result in the previous digest's Modena pathway (PMID 42580497) and the transfer drivers described in the advance care planning scoping review (PMID 42714187).
- *Misunderstanding, not availability, is the barrier for many families.* "Pancreatic Cancer Patients Say Palliative Care Was Explained to Them Badly — or Not at All" (PMID 42842360) carries forward and sits alongside the previous run's Chinese immigrant hospice study (PMID 42833695) and dharmic-faith outcomes study (PMID 42556759).
- *Age-tailored lower opioid doses still delivered equal pain relief.* "Older Cancer Patients Got Lower Opioid Doses — and Had the Same Pain Relief" (PMID 42853544) repeats, and the new pain scoping review (PMID 42893776) supplies the surrounding context — the oldest-old and people with non-cancer illness remain barely studied.

**Advances**

- Deprescribing moves from "inconsistent and reactive" to a named failure mode. The previous digest's rapid review "Stopping Pills Can Be as Important as Starting Them in Hospice Care" (PMID 42519959) said medication review happens too late; "A Hospital Pharmacy Audit Found Preventive Pills Often Stopped Abruptly at the End of Life" (PMID 42731737) shows the specific gap — beta-blockers, proton pump inhibitors, and antidepressants stopped outright with no documented taper.
- Advance care planning gains outcome evidence. The previous review (PMID 42714187) showed specific written instructions track with fewer transfers; "Advance Care Planning Conversations in ALS Deliver What Patients Asked For — 91% of the Time" (PMID 42586324) shows documented conversations matching what actually happened, while flagging that people with cognitive impairment are left out.
- Prognostication gets a bedside counterweight. Against the previous digest's machine learning intake model (PMID 42748398) and clinician referral signal in heart failure (PMID 42841449), "Bedside Signs Still Beat Scoring Systems for Predicting the Final Days" (PMID 42660371) shows observed signs holding their own against validated scores — and performing weakly in non-cancer dying.
- Caregiver burden deepens from strain to measurable harm. "Noisy Breathing at the Bedside: 1 in 4 Bereaved Families Show PTSD Symptoms After Witnessing It" (PMID 42727789) and "What Actually Makes or Breaks a Family Caregiver During Home Hospice" (PMID 42680076) move past the previous run's loneliness finding (PMID 42492599) into bereavement outcomes and concrete support structures.

**Counterpoints**

- Dying at home is not the universal goal the previous digest's in-place-death results implied. "Why Families Change Their Minds About Dying at Home" (PMID 42617989) reframes transfer to an inpatient unit as a legitimate, often relieving decision — a useful corrective to reading the Modena pathway's 98% in-facility death figure (PMID 42580497) as the only good outcome.
- On sedation, the evidence pushes in an unexpected direction. "For Agitated Delirium at the End of Life, Lorazepam Hit Families' Comfort Goals Better Than Haloperidol Alone" (PMID 42586325) supports benzodiazepine-based regimens, while "The Words Hospice Staff Use for End-of-Life Confusion May Change the Treatment Patients Get" (PMID 42910992) warns that reaching for sedation too readily means nobody looks for a reversible cause. Both are right; the sequence matters.

**New themes**

- *Delirium and terminal agitation as a cluster* — PMIDs 42873521, 42910992, 42885674, and 42586325 — territory no study in the previous run occupied.
- *Guardianship and the loss of decision-making rights.* "When a Hospital Asks a Court to Take Over Your Decisions" (PMID 42929333) is entirely new: 215 of 217 petitions granted, with poorer and less-educated older adults faring worst.
- *Existential distress as the driver of a wish to die* (PMID 42918739), where demoralization — not pain — was the only independent predictor.
- *Clinician grief as a workforce intervention* (PMID 42634408), a different kind of workforce story than the previous digest's supply projections (PMID 42546987).
- *Frailty as the trigger that should come earlier* (PMID 42607784), *opioid switching as a normal trajectory* (PMID 42607783), and *feeding tube timing in advanced cancer* (PMID 42922280).

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Five studies in this batch converge on the last days of life — the confusion, restlessness, and noisy breathing that families actually witness — and all five say the same thing from different angles: nobody has agreed on what to call it, how to measure it, or what to do first. "Delirium Is Common at the End of Life — and Often Mistaken for Dementia" (PMID 42873521) and "Agitation in the Final Days of Dementia Has No Agreed Definition — and No Validated Way to Measure It" (PMID 42885674) document the gap; "The Words Hospice Staff Use for End-of-Life Confusion May Change the Treatment Patients Get" (PMID 42910992) shows the label a clinician picks determines whether anyone looks for a reversible cause; the RECORD secondary analysis (PMID 42586325) shows the drug choice changes whether a family's own comfort goal is met; and "Noisy Breathing at the Bedside" (PMID 42727789) shows about 27% of families who found these scenes very distressing screened positive for PTSD symptoms afterward.

**Why pitch this now:** Individually these read as technical hospice papers, but together they describe something millions of families will sit through and almost nobody is prepared for — and they point to specific questions a family can ask in the room that appear to change what happens next.

**Angle:** A plain-language guide to the final days for adult children and spouses — what confusion, restlessness, and noisy breathing actually mean, which of them may have a treatable cause, what to ask the hospice team, and why the memory of those hours deserves follow-up care of its own. Written for family caregivers, with a sidebar for hospice and senior living staff on terminology and bereavement check-ins.

**Potential headlines:**
- Confusion in the Final Days Is Often Delirium, and It Is Often Treatable
- The Word a Nurse Chooses May Decide Whether Anyone Looks for the Cause
- What Nobody Prepares Families to See in the Last Hours

**Potential outlets:**
- **Next Avenue** — the sitting-at-the-bedside story told through families, with the research underneath, is squarely their register; the delirium-versus-dementia distinction alone would serve their dementia-caregiving readership.
- **AARP** — converts cleanly into checkable service journalism: the exact questions to ask a hospice team about confusion, sedation goals, and noisy breathing.
- **Hospice News** — terminology standardization across non-medical staff, screening for quiet delirium, and bereavement follow-up that asks about the death scene are live operational problems for their provider audience.
- **LeadingAge** — nonprofit nursing home and assisted living members face the dementia-agitation version of this directly, where no validated tool yet exists and staff observation is the evidence.

## Story Ideas by Study

- **Mapping What Actually Eases Pain at the End of Life for Older Adults (PMID 42893776):** The menu beyond opioids — nerve blocks, music and massage therapy, exercise, combined approaches — paired with the blunt finding that the oldest-old and people with heart failure or dementia have barely been studied, for a consumer health explainer.
- **Older Cancer Patients Got Lower Opioid Doses — and Had the Same Pain Relief (PMID 42853544):** Stop comparing milligrams; ask how often pain is reassessed and what the breakthrough plan is, for a patient-advocacy or caregiver-advice column.
- **A Hospital Pharmacy Audit Found Preventive Pills Often Stopped Abruptly at the End of Life (PMID 42731737):** The three drug classes that need tapering rather than a hard stop, and how to request a medication review at every transition, for a consumer health explainer with a long-term care nursing sidebar.
- **Letting ICU Nurses Trigger Palliative Care Referrals Nearly Tripled Consults — and Code Blues Fell (PMID 42904749):** A low-cost, replicable screening protocol with staff buy-in and measurable consult growth, for a hospital or senior-living trade brief, plus a consumer line on asking for a consult yourself in intensive care.
- **Noisy Breathing at the Bedside: 1 in 4 Bereaved Families Show PTSD Symptoms After Witnessing It (PMID 42727789):** A one-time explanation may not be enough — the case for checking back with families during and after the dying process, for a hospice trade brief and a caregiver piece on giving yourself permission to step out of the room.
- **What Actually Makes or Breaks a Family Caregiver During Home Hospice (PMID 42680076):** The two questions to settle before a home death — who supports the caregiver overnight, and who is the single named contact at 3 a.m. — for a caregiver-advice column, with a provider angle on assessing the caregiver as a second client.
- **Delirium Is Common at the End of Life — and Often Mistaken for Dementia (PMID 42873521):** Sudden over hours versus gradual over months, and why the quiet, withdrawn form is the one most often missed, for a consumer health explainer or family caregiver column.
- **Talking About Grief Helps Cancer Doctors in Training — And Costs Almost Nothing (PMID 42634408):** A protected debriefing hour as a staff-retention tool adaptable to nursing homes and hospice teams facing repeated resident deaths, for a senior living or hospice workforce trade brief.
- **When a Hospital Asks a Court to Take Over Your Decisions (PMID 42929333):** Once a petition is filed it is granted 99% of the time — naming your own decision-maker now, and what rights you have if a petition is filed, for a consumer legal-and-money explainer, with a trade angle on auditing how often an organization petitions and whether alternatives were exhausted.
- **Frailer Cancer Patients Are Getting Palliative Care Only After They're Hospitalized (PMID 42607784):** Don't wait for a hospitalization — ask for an outpatient palliative referral, plus the provider angle of using frailty scores already in the chart as an automatic trigger, for a consumer health explainer and a care-navigation trade brief.
- **Why Families Change Their Minds About Dying at Home (PMID 42617989):** Permission to change the plan — transfer as a planned contingency rather than a failure, and the calmer stretch of time caregivers described afterward, for a caregiver-advice column.
- **Bedside Signs Still Beat Scoring Systems for Predicting the Final Days (PMID 42660371):** Why "how long?" has no precise answer, especially outside cancer, and what to ask instead, for a consumer explainer, with a hospice trade angle on being transparent about uncertainty.
- **Pancreatic Cancer Patients Say Palliative Care Was Explained to Them Badly — or Not at All (PMID 42842360):** The script to ask for — what the team would actually do, and whether treatment continues alongside — plus the case for bilingual navigators and upfront financial conversations, for a caregiver column or a cancer care trade piece.
- **Letting Nurses Make the Referral Nearly Tripled Palliative Care Consults on One Hospital Unit (PMID 42853191):** Education plus a structured screen at admission and transfer, no new staffing, for a nursing leadership or senior-living clinical trade piece.
- **For Agitated Delirium at the End of Life, Lorazepam Hit Families' Comfort Goals Better Than Haloperidol Alone (PMID 42586325):** "How settled versus how awake do I want to be?" is a question you can answer in advance and write down, for a consumer advance-planning explainer, with a hospice trade angle on asking caregivers to define the sedation goal up front.
- **Most Patients With Advanced Cancer Change Opioids at Least Once — and Doses Roughly Double Each Time (PMID 42607783):** Needing a different pain medicine is the expected trajectory, not a failure or a sign of addiction — and the shortening interval between switches is a cue to ask for a palliative review, for a consumer health explainer.
- **Linking Nursing Homes to Palliative Care Teams Cut Hospital Trips and Eased Symptoms (PMID 42929149):** The question to ask when touring a care home — what is the formal arrangement with a palliative care service, and is there a named link nurse — for a consumer housing-decision piece and a senior-living operations brief.
- **Feeding Tube Decisions in Advanced Cancer Hinge on a Narrow 'Window of Opportunity' (PMID 42922280):** Timing closes options, and the goal of feeding legitimately changes from weight to comfort — plus how to name the gap between what a patient wants and what relatives expect, for a caregiver-advice column.
- **When Patients Say They Want to Die, Loss of Meaning — Not Pain — Is the Strongest Signal (PMID 42918739):** Reframing a frightening statement as information, and asking for an existential or demoralization assessment rather than only more medication, for a consumer health explainer or a hospice trade piece on spiritual care staffing.
- **Advance Care Planning Conversations in ALS Deliver What Patients Asked For — 91% of the Time (PMID 42586324):** Rare hard evidence that documented conversations translate into the care people wanted — and the warning that people with early thinking changes get left out, for a consumer advance-planning explainer with a neurology-clinic trade angle.
- **The Words Hospice Staff Use for End-of-Life Confusion May Change the Treatment Patients Get (PMID 42910992):** One question families can ask — "Is this delirium, and have you looked for a cause?" — plus a trade angle on standardizing terminology beyond physicians, for a caregiver column and a hospice staff-training brief.
- **Agitation in the Final Days of Dementia Has No Agreed Definition — and No Validated Way to Measure It (PMID 42885674):** Describe what you see in detail — timing, triggers, possible pain — because your observations are currently the main evidence available, for a dementia caregiver column or a long-term care trade brief.
