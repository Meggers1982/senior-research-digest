### 1. More Than Half of Hospitalized Older Adults and Nearly Half of Nursing Home Residents Need Palliative Care — Most Don't Have Cancer

**Journal:** *Aging Clinical and Experimental Research* | **Published:** June 28, 2026
**PMID:** 42366281 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s40520-026-03445-w

**The study:** Italian researchers conducted a nationwide point-prevalence study across 235 hospitals and long-term care facilities, screening 5,389 patients and residents (median ages 81 and 86, respectively) for palliative care needs using a validated assessment tool. They found 58.3% of hospitalized patients and 48.1% of nursing home residents had unmet palliative care needs. While rates were highest among cancer patients, over half of those *without* cancer still needed palliative support. Common symptoms included functional decline, pain, delirium, and difficulty swallowing.

**Why it matters:** This large-scale study demonstrates that palliative care should not be reserved for cancer patients alone — older adults with heart disease, dementia, and other chronic conditions often have equally pressing comfort and support needs.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or someone you love is hospitalized or in a care facility with serious health problems — even without cancer — you can ask the care team whether a palliative care consultation might help with pain, comfort, or planning for the future.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Nursing homes and hospitals may need to systematically screen all older patients for palliative care needs, not just those with terminal diagnoses, to ensure comfort-focused care isn't overlooked.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design (single time point); Italian healthcare context may not fully generalize to other countries.

### 2. Aged Care Staff Say Timing End-of-Life Conversations Is a Gradual Process — But Many Lack Confidence to Start

**Journal:** *Australasian Journal on Ageing* | **Published:** June 2026
**PMID:** 42163024 | **DOI:** 10.1111/ajag.70184

**The study:** Researchers interviewed 64 aged care staff (nurses, care managers, support workers) across 14 Australian facilities about their experiences communicating with families about end-of-life planning. Five themes emerged: timing conversations as a staged process, building family expectations gradually, managing perception gaps about decline, navigating family dynamics and culture, and the role of confidence and medical endorsement in initiating discussions.

**Why it matters:** Many aged care staff want to have early conversations about end-of-life wishes but feel unprepared or unsupported — suggesting a need for better training and resources for both staff and families.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're living in residential care, you can help staff by expressing your care preferences early — even before a health crisis — so conversations feel less rushed when decisions matter most.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living professionals should consider structured training and family-facing resources to help staff initiate end-of-life discussions earlier, when families have more time to process and plan.

**Caveats:** Qualitative study (staff perspectives only); Australian context; no direct measurement of family or resident outcomes.

### 3. Many Older Adults Receive Elective Procedures in Their Final Months of Life — Raising Questions About Care Alignment

**Journal:** *European Geriatric Medicine* | **Published:** August 3, 2026
**PMID:** 42545407 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s41999-026-01560-5

**The study:** Dutch researchers analyzed nationwide hospital records for 104,544 adults aged 65 and older who died in 2019. In the final three months of life, 15.9% were electively admitted to hospitals, and many received planned treatments including chemotherapy, dialysis, radiotherapy, and surgeries (hip replacements, heart valve procedures, amputations). A substantial portion of these elective interventions occurred in the final month before death.

**Why it matters:** When elective treatments are scheduled close to death, patients may have little time to benefit — prompting calls for earlier conversations about whether planned procedures align with patients' priorities.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're scheduling a planned surgery, treatment, or procedure, consider discussing with your doctor how it fits with your overall health picture and what you hope to gain from it — especially if you have a serious illness.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Clinicians and families should revisit goals of care before scheduling elective procedures in patients with limited life expectancy, ensuring interventions truly reflect what matters most to the patient.

**Caveats:** Retrospective observational study; cannot determine whether procedures were inappropriate for individual patients; Dutch healthcare system may differ from others.

### 4. Families, Nurses, and Doctors See Dying Differently — Especially When It Comes to Physical Symptoms

**Journal:** *European Geriatric Medicine* | **Published:** July 30, 2026
**PMID:** 42530751 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s41999-026-01564-1

**The study:** In a Belgian study of 103 patients dying on acute geriatric wards, researchers had relatives, nurses, and doctors complete daily comfort assessments during the last days of life. Doctors rated patient comfort significantly higher than relatives, with the biggest gap around dying symptoms (breathing, restlessness). Correlations between relatives' and healthcare providers' scores were weak and not statistically significant.

**Why it matters:** Family members may perceive their loved one's distress differently than medical staff — underscoring the importance of proactive communication to understand and address families' concerns during the dying process.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're supporting a dying loved one, don't hesitate to share what you're observing with the care team — your perspective on their comfort matters and can guide how care is adjusted.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Care teams should actively check in with families about their perceptions of comfort during end-of-life care, rather than assuming clinical assessments capture the full picture.

**Caveats:** Small sample of relatives (42 completed scales); single assessment tool; Belgian hospital context.

### 5. Palliative Care "Needs Rounds" in Nursing Homes Cut Emergency Visits and Hospital Stays

**Journal:** *Age and Ageing* | **Published:** June 1, 2026
**PMID:** 42315168 | **DOI:** 10.1093/ageing/afag173

**The study:** An Australian health service implemented palliative care "needs rounds" — specialist outreach visits — in 39 of 56 nursing homes over nine months. Comparing outcomes to the same period in 2019, emergency department visits dropped 20% and hospital admissions fell 64%. Estimated monthly cost savings reached nearly $500,000 AUD in reduced hospital use.

**Why it matters:** Regular specialist palliative care check-ins in nursing homes may help residents receive comfort-focused care in place, avoiding stressful and costly hospital transfers.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or a family member lives in a nursing home, ask whether palliative care specialists visit regularly to review residents' comfort and care needs — this can help prevent unnecessary hospital trips.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Aged care facilities and health systems may benefit from implementing routine palliative care rounds as a scalable strategy to improve resident care and reduce acute hospitalizations.

**Caveats:** Pre-post design during COVID-19 pandemic makes causal attribution uncertain; full implementation costs not captured; single Australian region.

### 6. Dementia as a Co-Occurring Condition Linked to Poorer End-of-Life Communication and Quality of Death

**Journal:** *Archives of Gerontology and Geriatrics* | **Published:** June 2026
**PMID:** 41856050 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.archger.2026.106218

**The study:** A Japanese nationwide survey of bereaved families (N=45,509 decedents aged 65+) compared end-of-life outcomes for older adults with and without co-occurring dementia across major causes of death. Among cancer patients with dementia, the odds of understanding their condition, communicating with loved ones, and dying in their preferred place were significantly lower. Similar patterns appeared in heart disease. Patients with dementia also had fewer end-of-life discussions about care preferences.

**Why it matters:** Older adults with dementia face distinct barriers to meaningful end-of-life communication and planning — highlighting the need for earlier and more inclusive conversations while cognitive capacity remains.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you've been diagnosed with early-stage memory problems, consider having conversations now about your future care wishes and who you'd want to speak on your behalf.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Families and clinicians should prioritize advance care planning discussions early in the dementia trajectory, before communication becomes more difficult.

**Caveats:** Retrospective proxy-reported data; observational design; Japanese cultural context may influence findings.

### 7. Virtual Reality Shows Promise for Managing Pain and Anxiety in Palliative Care Across All Ages

**Journal:** *Psychology and Aging* | **Published:** July 23, 2026
**PMID:** 42490252 | **DOI:** 10.1037/pag0000981

**The study:** A systematic review of 54 studies examined virtual reality (VR) use in palliative care settings for patients across the lifespan, including older adults. Most studies used highly immersive VR systems. Researchers found VR to be safe and feasible for managing physical and psychological symptoms (such as pain and anxiety) in people with life-limiting conditions. Adverse reactions were reported in only 9% of studies. VR also showed promise for training palliative care professionals and supporting family caregivers.

**Why it matters:** Non-drug approaches like VR may offer an additional tool for symptom relief in seriously ill patients, with relatively few side effects.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're receiving palliative care and interested in non-medication options for managing pain or anxiety, ask your care team whether virtual reality programs are available.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Hospices and palliative care programs may consider piloting VR interventions as a complement to standard symptom management, particularly for patients seeking alternatives to additional medications.

**Caveats:** Most included studies had mixed adult/older adult samples; few studies focused exclusively on older adults; study quality and designs varied.

### 8. Informal Caregivers Prioritize Pain Relief and Compassionate Communication When Deciding on Non-Surgical Hip Fracture Care

**Journal:** *European Geriatric Medicine* | **Published:** June 2026
**PMID:** 41784868 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s41999-026-01432-y

**The study:** Dutch researchers interviewed informal caregivers of frail older adults who received a specialized non-operative pain management procedure (SPING block) for hip fracture. Four themes emerged: prioritizing pain relief, navigating complex decisions with moral burden, coping with witnessing decline, and managing shifting caregiving roles. Caregivers valued rapid pain control and the possibility of early discharge but reported gaps in communication and uncertainty about long-term effects.

**Why it matters:** When aggressive surgery isn't appropriate, family caregivers need clear, compassionate communication and continuity of support to manage both their loved one's comfort and their own emotional burden.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or a family member faces a serious injury where surgery may not be the right option, ask the medical team to explain alternative pain management approaches clearly — and what to expect afterward.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Healthcare teams should develop multidisciplinary protocols that address not only patient pain but also caregiver information needs and emotional support during and after non-surgical palliative interventions.

**Caveats:** Single-center qualitative study; small sample size; findings may not generalize to other settings or procedures.

### 9. Specialist Palliative Care Referrals for the "Oldest-Old" (90+) Come Late — and Often Only Near Death

**Journal:** *Geriatric Nursing* | **Published:** August 11, 2026
**PMID:** 42580255 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.gerinurse.2026.104223

**The study:** A Canadian hospital reviewed 735 palliative care consultations for patients aged 90 and older from 2018–2022. Most had non-cancer diagnoses (74%), severe functional impairment at consult (74%), and no prior palliative care involvement (93%). Median time from admission to palliative consult was four days; median time from consult to death or discharge was also four days. Nearly two-thirds died in hospital or were transferred to a palliative unit for end-of-life care.

**Why it matters:** For the oldest patients, palliative care involvement often occurs only when death is imminent — leaving little time to optimize comfort or align care with patient goals.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you are in your late 80s or 90s, consider discussing your care preferences with your doctor or family now — before a hospitalization — so your wishes are known in advance.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Hospitals should consider earlier palliative care screening for very old patients with functional decline, and nurses can play a key role in advocating for timely referrals and advance care planning.

**Caveats:** Single-center retrospective study; Canadian healthcare context; data span pandemic years which may have influenced patterns.

### 10. Older Adults Living Alone or With Dementia Less Likely to Have Non-Medical End-of-Life Needs Met

**Journal:** *Age and Ageing* | **Published:** July 2, 2026
**PMID:** 42407095 | **DOI:** 10.1093/ageing/afag202

**The study:** Using proxy interviews from England's Longitudinal Study of Ageing (N=507 decedents aged 55+), researchers examined whether social, psychological, and spiritual needs were met in the last three months of life. While most people had these needs addressed (around 75–82%), those who lived alone or had dementia were significantly less likely to have their non-medical needs met. Family members, not formal services, provided most of this support.

**Why it matters:** Reliance on informal networks for emotional and spiritual support at end of life creates gaps — especially for those without nearby family or with cognitive impairment.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you live alone, consider building connections with community groups, faith organizations, or volunteer visitors who might offer companionship and support if you become seriously ill.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Policymakers and care providers should develop public health strategies to ensure older adults without strong informal networks — particularly those with dementia — receive adequate psychosocial and spiritual support at end of life.

**Caveats:** Proxy-reported outcomes; English population may not generalize; cross-sectional analysis within longitudinal study.

### 11. Training Helps Home-Based Clinicians Feel More Prepared to Discuss Life-Sustaining Treatment Orders

**Journal:** *Geriatric Nursing* | **Published:** June 2026
**PMID:** 41932056 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.gerinurse.2026.104017

**The study:** Forty-five advanced practice clinicians (APCs) providing home-based care to older adults participated in a one-hour online training on Physician Orders for Life-Sustaining Treatment (POLST) discussions. At three-month follow-up, statistically significant improvements were seen in POLST documentation, comfort addressing religious/cultural perspectives, and use of structured communication frameworks. Referrals to palliative care and hospice showed modest gains.

**Why it matters:** Even brief, targeted training can boost clinician confidence and documentation practices for end-of-life planning — an important step for ensuring patient wishes are captured and respected.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If a nurse practitioner or physician assistant visits you at home, you can ask them about completing a POLST form to document your wishes about CPR, hospitalization, and other life-sustaining treatments.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Home health agencies should prioritize scalable training on advance care planning tools like POLST to ensure clinicians feel equipped to initiate these critical conversations.

**Caveats:** Pre-post design without control group; modest sample size; three-month follow-up may not reflect sustained behavior change.

### 12. Nurses Identify Key Strategies for Improving Pain Management in People With Dementia

**Journal:** *Geriatric Nursing* | **Published:** July 6, 2026
**PMID:** 42407320 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.gerinurse.2026.104153

**The study:** Researchers used a structured group technique with 28 nurses from Norwegian nursing homes to generate and rank ideas for improving pain management in residents with dementia. The highest-ranked priorities were thorough pain assessment when pain expression is clear and ensuring staff have appropriate competence. Nurses rated the relevance of proposed strategies slightly higher than their feasibility, highlighting implementation challenges.

**Why it matters:** Nurses on the front lines of dementia care see a need for comprehensive, person-centered pain management models — but also recognize barriers to putting best practices into action.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have a loved one with dementia in a nursing home, ask staff how they assess and manage pain — especially since your family member may not be able to clearly express discomfort.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Nursing homes should invest in staff training and relational continuity to support nuanced pain assessment and management for residents who cannot easily communicate their needs.

**Caveats:** Qualitative study; Norwegian context; nurse perspectives only, not direct patient outcomes.

### 13. Home-Based Dementia Care Models Using Nurse Practitioners Show Promise for Aging in Place

**Journal:** *Research in Gerontological Nursing* | **Published:** June 23, 2026
**PMID:** 42343547 | **DOI:** 10.3928/19404921-20260612-01

**The study:** A scoping review identified 17 articles describing 10 models of home-based dementia care that include nurse practitioners (NPs) in ongoing care. Outcomes included high-quality care, reduced emergency department use, shorter hospital stays, delayed nursing home placement, and higher rates of advance care planning and palliative care transitions.

**Why it matters:** NP-led home care programs may help people with advancing dementia remain safely at home longer while receiving coordinated medical and palliative support.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or a loved one has dementia and wants to stay at home as long as possible, ask your doctor or health plan whether home-based care programs with nurse practitioners are available.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Health systems and policymakers should consider expanding NP-staffed home-based dementia care models to support aging in place and reduce costly acute care utilization.

**Caveats:** Scoping review (summarizes available models, not effectiveness); heterogeneous study designs; generalizability depends on local healthcare systems.

### 14. Palliative Care Intervention in Skilled Nursing Facilities May Benefit Residents With Dementia Differently Than Those Without

**Journal:** *Research in Gerontological Nursing* | **Published:** June 23, 2026
**PMID:** 42343545 | **DOI:** 10.3928/19404921-20260615-01

**The study:** In a pilot trial (N=52), nurse practitioners in skilled nursing facilities delivered a primary palliative care intervention to older adults with and without dementia. Quality of life scores trended toward improvement in the dementia group but toward worsening in the non-dementia group, though differences were not statistically significant.

**Why it matters:** Palliative care approaches may need to be tailored based on illness trajectory — what helps residents with dementia may not have the same effect for those with other conditions.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or a loved one is receiving short-term rehabilitation in a nursing facility, ask about palliative care services that focus on comfort and quality of life — not just recovery goals.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Skilled nursing facilities should explore how to adapt palliative care interventions for residents with different diagnoses and trajectories to maximize benefit.

**Caveats:** Pilot study with small sample; results not statistically significant; single intervention model tested.

### 15. Patients and Families in Palliative Care Units See Oral Health as Important — But Integration Remains Rare

**Journal:** *Gerodontology* | **Published:** June 2026
**PMID:** 41404855 | **DOI:** 10.1111/ger.70035

**The study:** A Chilean qualitative study interviewed seven healthcare professionals, four caregivers, and four patients in a palliative care unit about oral health. All groups recognized oral health's role in quality of life, but barriers to dental care — including lack of integration with palliative teams — were common. Participants called for including dental professionals in interdisciplinary palliative care.

**Why it matters:** Mouth pain, dry mouth, and oral infections can significantly affect comfort and eating at the end of life, yet dental care is often overlooked in palliative settings.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're receiving palliative care and have mouth discomfort, difficulty eating, or dental concerns, ask your care team about options for oral care support.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Palliative care programs should consider integrating dental professionals or oral health protocols to address a commonly neglected aspect of comfort care.

**Caveats:** Small qualitative study; single Chilean palliative care unit; findings may not generalize to other settings.

### 16. Seriously Ill Older Adults in Skilled Nursing Facilities Have High Palliative Care Needs Regardless of Dementia Status

**Journal:** *Journal of Applied Gerontology* | **Published:** July 2026
**PMID:** 40820287 | **DOI:** 10.1177/07334648251363646

**The study:** Baseline data from a pilot trial (N=52) compared palliative care needs of older adults with and without dementia receiving skilled nursing facility care. Functional decline was the most common indicator of palliative need in both groups. Top concerns included pain (64%), anxiety (60%), family worry (58%), and information needs (46%), with no significant differences by dementia status.

**Why it matters:** Older adults admitted for rehabilitation often have unmet palliative care needs — screening everyone, not just those with dementia, could improve comfort and care planning.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're recovering in a skilled nursing facility after a hospital stay, don't hesitate to tell staff about pain, worry, or questions you have about your care — these concerns can be addressed alongside your rehab goals.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Skilled nursing facilities should implement routine palliative care screening for all seriously ill residents, not just those with cognitive impairment.

**Caveats:** Small pilot study; cross-sectional baseline data only; single geographic region.

### 17. Older Adults With Hearing Loss and Chronic Illness Must Constantly Self-Advocate — A Burden That Could Be Reduced

**Journal:** *Geriatric Nursing* | **Published:** July 2026
**PMID:** 42167081 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.gerinurse.2026.104083

**The study:** Researchers interviewed 15 older adults with hearing loss and chronic illness, along with their partners, about healthcare experiences. A key finding was the exhausting routine of self-advocacy: participants were rarely asked about their hearing loss and had to repeatedly disclose it, often without accommodations. They reported fear of missing critical information and misunderstandings with providers.

**Why it matters:** Healthcare systems that fail to routinely ask about and accommodate hearing loss place an unfair burden on patients — particularly those managing serious illness who need clear communication most.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have hearing loss, consider bringing a written note to appointments stating your needs — and don't hesitate to ask providers to face you, speak clearly, or use written instructions.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Healthcare organizations should routinely screen for hearing loss and implement system-wide accommodations (such as visual alerts, captioning, or communication aids) so patients aren't forced to self-advocate at every visit.

**Caveats:** Small pilot study; qualitative design; findings reflect experiences in the U.S. healthcare system.

### 18. Polypharmacy and Potentially Inappropriate Medications Remain Common in Older Adults Needing Palliative Care

**Journal:** *Maturitas* | **Published:** June 2026
**PMID:** 41950773 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.maturitas.2026.108928

**The study:** An Italian multicenter study assessed prescribing patterns among 294 hospitalized and nursing home patients aged 65+ with identified palliative care needs. Polypharmacy (5+ medications) was present in 94%, and 68% were taking potentially inappropriate medications. Drug burden and inappropriate prescribing did not decline even in those closest to death.

**Why it matters:** Many older adults approaching the end of life continue taking medications that may offer little benefit and could cause harm — suggesting a need for more proactive medication review and deprescribing.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or a loved one has a serious illness and takes multiple medications, ask your doctor or pharmacist whether any could be safely stopped to reduce side effects and simplify care.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Clinicians should systematically review medications in patients with palliative care needs, aligning prescribing with comfort goals rather than disease prevention.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional point-prevalence design; Italian healthcare context; no direct measurement of patient outcomes.

### 19. Italian Nursing Homes for People With Dementia Show Major Regional Disparities in Staffing, Training, and Services

**Journal:** *Journal of Alzheimer's Disease* | **Published:** June 2026
**PMID:** 42089822 | **DOI:** 10.1177/13872877261442226

**The study:** A national survey of 1,671 Italian nursing homes accommodating people with dementia found significant regional disparities. Northern facilities had more beds, staff, training programs, digital systems, and integration with palliative care services. Southern facilities had shorter wait times but fewer resources. Special dementia units and tailored environments were lacking across the country.

**Why it matters:** Where a person with dementia lives in Italy may strongly influence the quality and comprehensiveness of care they receive — pointing to a need for national standards and investment.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're considering nursing home care for yourself or a loved one with dementia, ask about staff training, specialized dementia programs, and access to palliative care support.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Policymakers and senior living organizations should work to reduce regional inequities in dementia care, ensuring all facilities meet baseline standards for staffing, training, and supportive services.

**Caveats:** Self-reported facility data; Italian context; descriptive analysis without direct quality-of-care outcomes.

### 20. Infections Are the Leading Cause of Death in Hospitalized Dementia Patients — But Palliative Care Documentation Lags

**Journal:** *Journal of Alzheimer's Disease* | **Published:** June 2026
**PMID:** 42081122 | **DOI:** 10.1177/13872877261445578

**The study:** A Brazilian study reviewed 122 patients with dementia who died in a geriatric hospital between 2015 and 2023. Infectious diseases — mainly pneumonia and sepsis — caused 67% of deaths. Antibiotics were prescribed in 76% of cases, but palliative care measures were documented for only 41%.

**Why it matters:** Despite the predictable trajectory of advanced dementia, many patients receive aggressive antibiotic treatment without documented palliative care goals — suggesting opportunities to better align care with comfort-focused priorities.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're caring for someone with advanced dementia, discuss with their doctor what comfort-focused care would look like if infections or other complications arise.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Hospitals should develop integrated end-of-life protocols for patients with advanced dementia, ensuring palliative care needs are recognized and documented alongside medical treatment decisions.

**Caveats:** Single-center retrospective study; Brazilian healthcare context; palliative care may have been provided but not documented.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**

The NEW and PREVIOUS digests share 14 identical studies (PMIDs 42366281, 42163024, 42545407, 42530751, 42315168, 41856050, 42490252, 41784868, 42407095, 41932056, 42407320, 42343547, 41404855, 41950773, 42081122), reinforcing several established messages:

- Palliative care needs extend far beyond cancer — the Italian NECPAL prevalence study (PMID 42366281) appears again, confirming most hospitalized older adults and nursing home residents needing palliative support do not have cancer diagnoses.
- Dementia complicates end-of-life planning — the Japanese bereaved-family survey (PMID 41856050) reappears in both digests, consistently showing lower rates of awareness, communication, and documented preferences among older adults with dementia.
- Families and clinicians perceive dying differently — the Belgian comfort-rating study (PMID 42530751) is repeated, underscoring that doctors rate patient comfort higher than relatives, particularly around breathing and restlessness.
- Polypharmacy persists at end of life — the Italian prescribing study (PMID 41950773) appears in both runs, confirming medication burden does not decline even in advanced illness.
- Palliative care "needs rounds" reduce hospitalizations — the Australian outreach intervention (PMID 42315168) is covered again with consistent findings, though COVID-era confounding remains a caveat.

**Advances**

- The NEW digest adds a Canadian study on palliative referrals for the "oldest-old" (PMID 42580255), showing that patients aged 90+ receive specialist consultations only days before death — extending the evidence on late referrals to the very oldest segment of the population.
- A NEW pilot trial (PMID 42343545) suggests palliative care interventions in skilled nursing facilities may affect residents with dementia differently than those without, introducing the concept of diagnosis-specific tailoring of palliative approaches.
- A NEW study on hearing loss and chronic illness (PMID 42167081) highlights the exhausting self-advocacy burden placed on older adults with sensory impairment — a communication barrier relevant to end-of-life settings.
- The NEW Italian nursing home survey (PMID 42089822) documents major regional disparities in dementia care resources, including palliative care integration, providing system-level data not present in the PREVIOUS digest.

**Counterpoints**

- The PREVIOUS digest included a scoping review (PMID 42497095) noting that economic evidence for nursing home palliative care remains limited and inconclusive. The NEW digest does not include that study but continues to feature the "needs rounds" intervention (PMID 42315168) with optimistic utilization findings. The tension between enthusiasm for these programs and weak cost-effectiveness evidence persists unresolved.

**New themes**

- **Skilled nursing facility residents have high palliative care needs regardless of dementia status.** Two NEW studies (PMIDs 40820287, 42343545) focus on SNF populations specifically, finding that pain, anxiety, and family worry are common across diagnoses — expanding the palliative care conversation beyond long-term care to short-stay rehabilitation settings.
- **Infection management in advanced dementia lacks palliative documentation.** The Brazilian study (PMID 42081122) appears in both digests, but the NEW digest pairs it with the prevalence data (PMID 42366281) to reinforce that aggressive antibiotic treatment often occurs without documented comfort-care goals.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Several studies in this batch converge on a theme of palliative care arriving too late for those who need it most. The Canadian study of patients aged 90+ (PMID 42580255) shows specialist referrals come just days before death. The Dutch analysis of elective procedures (PMID 42545407) finds planned interventions continuing into the final month of life. The Australian aged care staff study (PMID 42163024) describes clinicians wanting to start conversations earlier but lacking confidence. And the SNF baseline data (PMID 40820287) reveal high unmet needs among rehabilitation patients who aren't even being screened for palliative support.

**Why pitch this now:** The hospice benefit's six-month prognosis requirement and the cultural association of palliative care with "giving up" continue to delay referrals.
