### 1. Daily low-dose aspirin didn't protect older adults' physical and mental capacity — and women fared slightly worse

**Journal:** *Geroscience* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 29
**PMID:** 42955660 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11357-026-02624-x

**The study:** Researchers re-analyzed a large randomized trial of 100 mg daily aspirin versus placebo in 18,581 community-dwelling adults aged 65 and older, scoring each participant annually on a four-part "intrinsic capacity" index covering cognition, mobility, vitality and psychological well-being. The index tracked well against chronological age, accumulated health deficits and epigenetic aging clocks, and people in the lowest fifth of baseline scores were more than four times as likely to die or develop dementia or disability. Over five years, aspirin did nothing to slow the decline in capacity in men, and in women it was linked to a small but statistically significant acceleration of decline.

**Why it matters:** Many older adults take low-dose aspirin hoping for a general anti-aging or protective benefit; this analysis of trial data finds no preservation of day-to-day function and hints at a sex-specific downside for women.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you are taking daily low-dose aspirin purely as a preventive measure rather than because a doctor prescribed it after a heart attack or stroke, this is worth raising at your next appointment — ask what specific benefit it is meant to deliver for you, and whether that benefit still applies at your age. Don't stop a prescribed aspirin on your own.
- **About older adults and seniors:** The intrinsic capacity score used here — a simple composite of thinking, walking, energy and mood — predicted dementia- and disability-free survival powerfully, which makes it a plausible framework for senior living teams to track residents' functional trajectory rather than treating each domain in isolation.

**Caveats:** Post-hoc secondary analysis of a trial designed for a different primary outcome; the aspirin effect in women was very small and the three-way sex-by-treatment interaction was borderline (p = 0.06), so it could be chance. One co-author holds patents on the epigenetic clocks used and is affiliated with a biotech company.

### 2. For people with memory problems, group classes beat one-on-one sessions — especially for thinking skills

**Journal:** *Gerontologist* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 29
**PMID:** 42954294 | **DOI:** 10.1093/geront/gnag251

**The study:** Researchers pooled randomized controlled trials published between 2015 and 2026 that tested programs for older adults with cognitive impairment and measured at least two of three outcomes: physical capability, cognitive function, and psychosocial well-being. Using a multilevel meta-analysis, they found that group-delivered programs produced benefits across all three domains and significantly larger gains in cognitive function than the same kinds of programs delivered individually. Multicomponent programs — combining exercise, cognitive activity and social elements — helped cognitive and psychosocial outcomes in particular.

**Why it matters:** It suggests the social format of an activity is not just a scheduling convenience but part of what makes it work, which is directly actionable for anyone choosing a memory-support program.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or your partner has been diagnosed with mild cognitive impairment or early dementia, ask whether the program on offer is run as a group and whether it combines several kinds of activity — movement, mental challenge and social contact — rather than drilling a single skill alone at home.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living and adult day programs can use this to justify group programming budgets, and to audit whether their cognitive-support offerings are single-component worksheets or genuinely multicomponent; the evidence points toward matching the delivery format to the outcome being targeted rather than one-size-fits-all.

**Caveats:** Meta-analysis of heterogeneous trials with substantial statistical heterogeneity and wide prediction intervals, meaning results in any individual program could differ markedly; effects were pooled across varied populations and intervention designs.

### 3. Walking while doing a mental task reveals hidden brain changes that normal walking misses

**Journal:** *J Neurol* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 28
**PMID:** 42955373 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s00415-026-14229-7

**The study:** Investigators pooled nine studies of dual-task walking (walking while performing a mental task such as counting backward) and eight of ordinary walking in people with cognitive impairment caused by cerebral small vessel disease — a common form of age-related brain blood-vessel damage. Under dual-task conditions, affected patients walked significantly more slowly, had greater stride-to-stride variability, and paid a much higher "dual-task cost" than cognitively healthy comparison participants. Dual-task walking distinguished the two groups more effectively than walking alone.

**Why it matters:** A low-tech walking test done while talking or counting may flag vascular brain changes earlier than plain gait assessment, pointing toward a cheap screening tool that doesn't require a scanner.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Notice whether you slow down, become unsteady, or stop talking when you walk and converse at the same time — that pattern is worth mentioning to your doctor, not because it diagnoses anything on its own, but because it's a signal clinicians can follow up with proper assessment.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For care staff, the "stops walking when talking" observation is something anyone can notice during a routine hallway escort; this review supports treating it as a prompt for a formal gait and cognitive evaluation rather than a quirk of personality.

**Caveats:** Small number of included studies with inconsistent imaging criteria, gait metrics and cognitive task paradigms; the authors themselves call subgroup findings exploratory and note the screening and predictive value of these measures has not yet been established.

### 4. The 'good days and bad days' of Lewy body dementia don't fade in advanced disease — and two standard scales barely agree

**Journal:** *Alzheimers Dement (Amst)* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 27
**PMID:** 42953268 | **DOI:** 10.1002/dad2.70519

**The study:** Most research on cognitive fluctuations — the hallmark swings in alertness and attention in dementia with Lewy bodies — has focused on early stages, so researchers followed a cohort with advanced DLB over time using two established measures, the Mayo Fluctuations Scale and the Clinician Assessment of Fluctuations. Fluctuations were present in more than 70% of participants at every visit, and were linked to hallucinations, daytime sleepiness, parkinsonism, autonomic symptoms including orthostatic hypotension, overall symptom burden and medication burden. Strikingly, the two measurement scales correlated only very weakly with each other.

**Why it matters:** Families often assume marked day-to-day variation is an early-stage feature or doubt what they are seeing; this confirms fluctuations persist into advanced disease and cluster with other treatable symptoms like sleepiness, blood-pressure drops and medication load.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If your alertness varies dramatically from hour to hour, that is a recognized feature of Lewy body dementia rather than inconsistency on your part — and it's worth asking your clinician to review your medication list and check for blood-pressure drops when you stand, both of which traveled with worse fluctuations in this study.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Caregivers and care staff should document when the good and bad hours occur and schedule demanding activities, visits and assessments accordingly; also be aware that a clinic assessment using one fluctuation scale may not match what another captures, so your own observations carry real weight in the conversation.

**Caveats:** Observational design, so associations cannot establish cause; sample size not reported in the abstract and the cohort is drawn from specialty academic centers, which may not represent all people with advanced DLB; several investigators report industry consulting and foundation funding.

### 5. Three simple office tests can flag which Parkinson's patients will fall in the next year

**Journal:** *Ann Neurol* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 30
**PMID:** 42960478 | **DOI:** 10.1002/ana.78400

**The study:** Researchers built fall-prediction models using the Parkinson's Progression Markers Initiative cohort and then tested them in separate University of Pennsylvania cohorts. For people who had never fallen, a combination of a motor exam score, a cognitive screen (MoCA) and a depression scale predicted next-year first falls; for those already falling, disease stage, depression score and a processing-speed test predicted recurrent falls. In the external validation group, first-fall risk rose from about 7% to 18.5% from the lowest to highest risk tier, and recurrent-fall rates rose from 22% to 65%. Expensive imaging and fluid biomarkers added little beyond the clinical measures.

**Why it matters:** Fall risk in Parkinson's can be estimated with inexpensive tests that most movement-disorder clinics already administer, meaning risk conversations and prevention efforts don't have to wait for scans or lab panels.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have Parkinson's, ask at your next visit whether your motor exam, memory screen and mood questionnaire together suggest you are entering a higher-risk year for falls — and note that depression scores mattered as much as motor severity, so mood is worth raising even if you think of falls as a purely physical issue.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For care teams and senior living staff, this suggests building routine fall-risk review into existing Parkinson's visits using data already on the chart, and treating a rising depression or cognitive score as a prompt to reassess the home environment and supervision level — not just the medication list.

**Caveats:** Observational cohort data; prediction accuracy was only moderate and dropped in the external validation cohorts (AUROC 0.63–0.64), the recurrent-fall validation sample was small (137 visit-pairs), and falls were assessed in research cohorts that may not reflect general Parkinson's populations.

### 6. After hip fracture surgery, the real decline often starts in year two — and longer rehab was linked to less of it

**Journal:** *J Am Med Dir Assoc* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 29
**PMID:** 42956453 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jamda.2026.106413

**The study:** Using linked medical and long-term care insurance claims for Kyoto City, researchers followed 6,556 adults aged 75 and older who had hip fracture surgery between 2013 and 2018 and survived at least 180 days. Care-need level worsened in 10.6% by 12 months, 28.3% by 24 months and 37.4% by 36 months after surgery. Each additional month of continuous rehabilitation during the first six months after surgery was associated with about a 4% lower risk of later worsening, though that benefit disappeared when death was included in the outcome.

**Why it matters:** More than a third of older hip-fracture survivors lost functional ground within three years, and most of that loss happened well after the usual post-surgery follow-up window had closed.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or your spouse has had hip fracture surgery, don't assume the recovery story ends once you're home and walking — ask about keeping rehabilitation going for as many consecutive months as your plan allows, and request a formal reassessment of your care needs at the one- and two-year marks, not just in the first few months.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and long-term care professionals, the practical takeaway is scheduling: care planning for hip fracture survivors should extend to 24 and 36 months, when the majority of care-level deterioration occurred, rather than concentrating resources in the first 90 to 180 days.

**Caveats:** Retrospective observational study using administrative claims — it cannot establish that rehabilitation caused better outcomes; the authors explicitly warn against a causal reading, and no benefit appeared on the composite outcome that included death. Single-city Japanese population with a national long-term care insurance system that may not generalize elsewhere.

### 7. A hospital fracture follow-up service cut repeat breaks by more than half — but only after the first year

**Journal:** *Osteoporos Int* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 28
**PMID:** 42955080 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s00198-026-08276-z

**The study:** Researchers at Westmead Hospital in Western Sydney compared 279 patients enrolled in a Fracture Liaison Service — a program that identifies people arriving at the emergency department with a low-trauma fracture and ushers them into osteoporosis assessment and treatment — with 127 similar patients who declined, didn't attend or were lost to follow-up. At one year, repeat fracture rates were essentially identical (4.3% vs 5.5%). By three years, they diverged sharply: 10.4% in the service group versus 23.6% in the controls, roughly a 70% lower risk between years one and three.

**Why it matters:** A fracture from a minor fall is the single strongest warning sign of the next one, yet these programs are often judged on one-year results — a window this study suggests is too short to see the payoff.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you've broken a bone from a fall at standing height or less, ask the hospital whether it has a fracture liaison service or osteoporosis follow-up clinic and get yourself referred — and stick with the treatment even if nothing seems to be happening in the first year, because that's when the benefit in this study had not yet appeared.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and senior living operators, this is an argument for actively tracking whether a resident who fractured a wrist, hip or spine was ever connected to secondary fracture prevention — many in the comparison group had been approached and simply dropped out of follow-up.

**Caveats:** Observational with a non-randomized control group made up of people who declined or were lost to follow-up, who may differ in motivation and health engagement; single center; modest sample (406 patients); the service group actually had more prior fractures at baseline.

### 8. Video and console-based balance training beats doing nothing — but not a regular exercise class

**Journal:** *JMIR Aging* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 28
**PMID:** 42954170 | **DOI:** 10.2196/101231

**The study:** This network meta-analysis pooled 60 randomized controlled trials involving 3,469 older adults (mean age 73) to compare five categories of technology-based balance training against conventional exercise and against no treatment. Telerehabilitation, exergaming with balance platforms and exergaming with motion capture all improved balance compared with no treatment. Crucially, no technology-based approach outperformed conventional exercise in any analysis, and certainty of evidence was rated moderate for only two comparisons out of 39.

**Why it matters:** As gyms, senior communities and insurers push app- and console-based balance programs, this analysis suggests their main value is as an accessible alternative to inactivity rather than an upgrade over an ordinary in-person exercise class.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If an in-person balance or strength class works for your schedule and budget, there's no evidence here that a screen-based program would serve you better — but if you can't get to a class, a telerehabilitation or exergaming program is meaningfully better than doing nothing, and dropout rates were no higher than in conventional programs.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living activity directors weighing the purchase of exergaming equipment, the honest framing is reach, not superiority: the technology's advantage lies in engaging residents who won't attend a traditional class, since none of it beat conventional exercise head to head.

**Caveats:** Evidence certainty was low or very low for 37 of 39 comparisons; the top-ranked telerehabilitation estimate rested on just five trials totaling 198 participants; no included trial actually measured falls, so these are balance-test results, not fall-prevention evidence.

### 9. Acupuncture after knee replacement eased pain and improved function in older patients, review finds

**Journal:** *Geriatr Nurs* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 29
**PMID:** 42956696 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.gerinurse.2026.104406

**The study:** Researchers pooled 15 randomized controlled trials covering 1,037 patients who received acupuncture or electroacupuncture after total knee replacement. Pain scores were substantially lower and knee function scores (Hospital for Special Surgery scale) substantially higher in the acupuncture groups compared with controls. No serious adverse events were reported across the included trials.

**Why it matters:** Postoperative pain control in older knee replacement patients often means opioids and their side effects, so a low-risk add-on with evidence behind it is worth discussing with a surgical team.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're scheduled for a knee replacement, it's reasonable to ask your surgeon or anesthesia team whether acupuncture is available as an add-on to your pain plan — the review found no serious adverse events, but treat it as a complement to standard care, not a replacement for it.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For rehab coordinators and nursing staff, the authors are explicit that this evidence does not support acupuncture as a nurse-led intervention; the relevance is in pain assessment and coordinating with practitioners if a patient pursues it.

**Caveats:** Meta-analysis of modest-sized trials; effect sizes were very large (SMD -1.08 for pain), which in acupuncture research often signals inadequate blinding and sham-control problems; most trials appear to originate from a single research region, limiting generalizability, and the review does not report publication-bias or risk-of-bias results in the abstract.

### 10. Older drivers aren't reckless — they slow down, brake earlier and crash less in simulator tests

**Journal:** *J Gerontol B Psychol Sci Soc Sci* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 29
**PMID:** 42958638 | **DOI:** 10.1093/geronb/gbag217

**The study:** Spanish researchers put 103 drivers through a high-fidelity motorcycle simulator in three groups: middle-aged (30-54, n=40), aged 65-74 (n=34), and aged 75-86 (n=29), also measuring risk perception, personality and risky decision-making. Both older groups drove more conservatively — lower speeds, fewer speeding violations, longer braking maneuvers, steadier throttle control — and had fewer simulated crashes than the middle-aged drivers. Drivers 75+ reported higher perceived risk, though they were less sharp at telling risky from non-risky situations in a braking decision task. Steering variability and self-reported risk-taking explained much of the age difference in accidents.

**Why it matters:** The findings push back on the blanket assumption that aging automatically makes a driver dangerous, suggesting that self-regulation and compensatory habits can offset age-related slowing — a point that matters in family conversations about when to hand over the keys.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you've noticed yourself driving slower, braking sooner or avoiding busy roads, that may be skill rather than decline — but this study also found that drivers over 75 were less able to distinguish genuinely risky situations from safe ones, so it's worth asking your doctor or an occupational therapist about a driving evaluation rather than relying on your own sense of how you're doing.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and senior living staff, the useful question shifts from 'should they still be driving?' to 'what adaptations are they already making, and are those adaptations enough?' — look for whether the person self-limits night, highway or rush-hour driving, and treat the loss of those self-imposed limits as a more meaningful warning sign than age alone.

**Caveats:** Simulated driving on a motorcycle simulator, not real-world road crashes; modest sample sizes per group (29-40); single research center in Spain; risk perception and risk-taking measures were self-reported; cross-sectional design cannot establish causation; findings may not generalize to drivers with cognitive impairment, who were likely not represented.

### 11. Retiring makes people more helpful — European study tracks the same individuals before and after

**Journal:** *Res Aging* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 29
**PMID:** 42956395 | **DOI:** 10.1177/01640275261502786

**The study:** Researchers used eight waves of the Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe spanning 19 countries from 2004 to 2022, analyzing 7,422 observations from 3,711 people aged 50 and over. Unlike earlier work that simply compared retirees with workers, this study followed the same individuals across the retirement transition using fixed-effects models. Moving from employment into retirement was associated with providing more practical help — things like household chores, errands and paperwork — to people outside one's own household. The increase appeared stronger in countries with higher social spending.

**Why it matters:** It counters the idea that retirement means withdrawal from useful roles, and suggests that generous public services free retirees up to help others rather than replacing informal help.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're approaching retirement and worried about losing purpose, the evidence suggests most people naturally shift their energy toward helping family, friends and neighbors — think now about which kinds of help you actually want to give, before requests start arriving by default.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living operators and community organizations, newly retired residents are an under-tapped volunteer pool at precisely the moment their capacity to help is rising; structured volunteer roles offered in the first year after retirement may stick better than later outreach.

**Caveats:** Observational design — cannot establish that retirement causes increased helping, though within-person modeling strengthens the inference; support provision was self-reported; the country-level social spending finding is described by the authors as only suggestive; European sample may not generalize to the US or other systems.

### 12. Getting frail seniors online may not lift their mood, large British study finds

**Journal:** *Aging Ment Health* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 29
**PMID:** 42954453 | **DOI:** 10.1080/13607863.2026.2753022

**The study:** Researchers at the London School of Economics analyzed data on 1,834 adults aged 65 and older in the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing, 376 of whom met criteria for frailty. Frail participants reported markedly higher depression scores than their non-frail peers. But how often someone used the internet showed no significant association with depression, in either group, once frailty was accounted for.

**Why it matters:** Digital inclusion programs for older adults are often sold as a mental health intervention; this analysis suggests that simply getting people online, without attention to what they actually do there, may not be enough to shift mood.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have been told that getting online will help with low mood, this study suggests frequency of use alone isn't the active ingredient. It may be worth thinking about which online activities genuinely connect you to other people — video calls with family, an online group tied to an interest you already have — rather than counting screen hours, and raising persistent low mood with your doctor regardless of your internet habits.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living staff and family members running tablet or Wi-Fi initiatives: these findings argue for measuring meaningful engagement rather than logins, and for not treating digital access as a substitute for addressing frailty itself, which was the far stronger correlate of depression here.

**Caveats:** Observational secondary analysis that cannot establish causation; depression and internet use were self-reported; the measure captured frequency rather than type of online activity, which the authors flag as a key limitation; findings are from an English cohort and may not generalize elsewhere.

### 13. Three in four families didn't bring their relative back for a dementia work-up — even after the cost barrier was removed

**Journal:** *J Elder Abuse Negl* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 29
**PMID:** 42954618 | **DOI:** 10.1080/08946566.2026.2755005

**The study:** Neurologists and psychiatrists at a Turkish state hospital prospectively followed 28 people with suspected but undiagnosed dementia who had been referred for a health board evaluation — a process that unlocks disability benefits and health care access. Although caregivers brought all of them to the health board appointment, 75% were never taken to the outpatient clinic for the diagnostic work-up and treatment that followed. The authors argue that this pattern of caregiver-driven non-attendance may itself be a warning sign of neglect.

**Why it matters:** It reframes missed appointments from an administrative nuisance into a possible clinical red flag, and suggests that removing financial and access barriers alone does not guarantee an older person actually gets diagnosed and treated.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you suspect memory problems in yourself, try to secure your own route to a diagnostic appointment — ask the clinic to contact you directly, bring a second trusted person to appointments, and follow up yourself rather than relying on a single relative to carry the process forward.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For clinicians and care professionals: a family that shows up for the benefits appointment but never returns for the diagnostic one deserves a closer look, not just a rescheduling letter. Consider building active outreach and follow-up calls into dementia referral pathways, and treating repeated non-attendance as a trigger for a neglect assessment.

**Caveats:** Very small sample (N=28), single-center, observational, and conducted in a specific Turkish health system where informal family caregiving carries heavy legal and cultural weight — the 75% figure should not be generalized to other countries. Motives for non-attendance were inferred rather than directly established.

### 14. Arts and museum visits leave a measurable fingerprint in the blood — and track with lower dementia and heart disease risk

**Journal:** *Brain Behav Immun* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 29
**PMID:** 42957065 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.bbi.2026.107088

**The study:** Researchers at University College London analyzed blood protein data and survey responses from 6,180 participants in the UK Household Longitudinal Study, scoring how often and how widely people took part in arts, cultural events, libraries, museums and heritage sites. Greater engagement was consistently linked to differences in 17 serum proteins involved in immune regulation, inflammatory signaling and cell proliferation. Over follow-up, more engaged participants had lower incidence of 11 chronic conditions including dementia, stroke, cardiovascular and ischemic heart disease, arthritis, hypertension, diabetes, COPD and clinical depression — with the identified proteins statistically accounting for 16% to 38% of several of those associations.

**Why it matters:** This is the first attempt to map a biological signature for arts and cultural participation, lending mechanistic weight to the growing case that cultural engagement belongs alongside exercise and diet in conversations about healthy aging.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If concerts, museums, choirs, libraries or craft groups are things you enjoy, this research suggests they may be doing more for you than passing the time — and that variety of engagement, not just frequency, is part of what the researchers measured. Look into local concession rates, library programs and community arts groups, but treat this as a reason to keep doing what you like, not as a replacement for medical care.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living operators and activity coordinators: this gives a biological talking point for protecting arts programming budgets and for prioritizing diversity of cultural offerings and genuine outings over a single recurring activity. For family caregivers, arranging transport to a gallery or concert may be a more defensible use of a free afternoon than it once appeared.

**Caveats:** Observational cohort; cannot establish causation, and the authors explicitly state the mediation analysis does not prove a causal protein pathway because engagement and proteins were measured at roughly the same time. Arts engagement and many disease outcomes were self-reported, and healthier, wealthier people engage more with the arts — residual confounding is likely despite adjustment. UK sample spanning adulthood rather than an exclusively older cohort.

### 15. Old head injuries may accelerate memory decline decades later, veteran study suggests

**Journal:** *Brain Inj* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 29
**PMID:** 42958736 | **DOI:** 10.1080/02699052.2026.2752523

**The study:** Ohio State University researchers used the Department of Defense–Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative repository to track cognition in 203 Vietnam War veterans aged 60 to 84 (99% male) with varying histories of traumatic brain injury, testing them at baseline and 12 months. Among those with a TBI history, older age was linked to steeper decline in episodic memory — the ability to recall specific events. No such interaction appeared for executive function or language, and the authors note the effect explained only a modest share of the variance.

**Why it matters:** It suggests a head injury sustained decades earlier may change how memory ages, which bears on who should be monitored more closely as they grow older.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have a history of head injury — from military service, a car crash, a fall or sport — it is worth telling your doctor, even if it was 40 years ago, and mentioning any new difficulty recalling recent events or conversations. This study does not say decline is inevitable, but it does suggest your memory history deserves a baseline and attention over time.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and care staff: head injury history belongs in the intake record alongside medications and chronic conditions, and may help explain why memory specifically — rather than reasoning or language — appears to be slipping in a particular resident.

**Caveats:** Observational analysis that cannot establish causation; only 12 months of follow-up; sample was almost entirely male Vietnam-era veterans and may not generalize to women or civilians; TBI histories were retrospective; the authors themselves emphasize the modest proportion of variance explained.

### 16. Hearing loss plus memory trouble: brain scans show who is most likely to progress to dementia

**Journal:** *AJNR Am J Neuroradiol* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 29
**PMID:** 41916749 | **DOI:** 10.3174/ajnr.A9326

**The study:** Researchers analyzed three serial MRI scans taken about a year apart from 93 participants in the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative: 51 with mild cognitive impairment and normal hearing, 20 with MCI plus age-related hearing loss who stayed stable, and 22 with MCI plus hearing loss who progressed to dementia. Those who progressed showed significantly faster annual thinning in the left entorhinal cortex, left middle temporal gyrus and both superior temporal gyri. Notably, even the stable hearing-loss group lost temporal-lobe thickness faster than those with normal hearing.

**Why it matters:** Hearing loss is one of the largest modifiable dementia risk factors, and this adds physical evidence that it travels with accelerated shrinkage in exactly the brain regions Alzheimer's attacks first.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If your hearing has declined, don't file it away as a cosmetic inconvenience — get it formally tested, ask about hearing aids, and mention both your hearing and any memory concerns at the same appointment so they're considered together rather than in separate silos.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For care professionals: residents with both hearing loss and mild cognitive impairment may warrant closer cognitive monitoring. Practically, ensuring hearing devices are working, charged and actually worn is a low-cost intervention with potential cognitive stakes.

**Caveats:** Small sample (N=93), with only 20 and 22 people in the two hearing-loss groups; observational design cannot show that hearing loss causes the atrophy; ADNI participants are volunteers who tend to be more educated and less diverse than the general population; the predictive accuracy reported is preliminary and not validated for clinical use.

### 17. After a minor stroke, how well you move at discharge predicts how far you'll go six months later

**Journal:** *J Stroke Cerebrovasc Dis* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 29
**PMID:** 42957010 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jstrokecerebrovasdis.2026.108778

**The study:** Japanese researchers followed 184 patients aged 65 and older (median age 77, 64 women) hospitalized with mild ischemic stroke, measuring their physical function at discharge and assessing "life-space mobility" — how far and how often they actually get out into the world — before hospitalization and again six months after the stroke. Physical function at discharge was significantly associated with mobility at six months, while the pathway running through psychological function was small and not statistically significant. The authors caution that their overall statistical model fit the data poorly.

**Why it matters:** Life-space mobility is tightly linked to independence, social contact and quality of life, and this suggests that physical capability at the moment of hospital discharge carries forward into whether an older stroke survivor stays housebound or re-enters their community.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or a partner are being discharged after a mild stroke, ask specifically what your walking and balance capacity is at discharge and what therapy is arranged for the weeks afterward — the gap between "well enough to go home" and "able to get back out into your neighborhood" is where this study puts the risk.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For rehabilitation teams and families: discharge readiness assessments that focus on physical function may be worth pairing with explicit goals about community re-entry — errands, visits, outings — rather than assuming mobility will rebuild on its own at home.

**Caveats:** Observational study that cannot establish causation; conducted at Japanese centers and may not generalize to other health systems; the authors report their hypothesized structural model showed inadequate fit and call for more comprehensive modeling, so the findings should be treated as preliminary.

### 18. Your phone can measure your balance — but it still can't predict whether you'll fall

**Journal:** *Gait Posture* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 27
**PMID:** 42956683 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.gaitpost.2026.110680

**The study:** Researchers tested 580 community-dwelling adults aged 55 and older between 2023 and 2026, comparing a smartphone's built-in accelerometer against a laboratory-grade swaymeter during four standing tasks (eyes open and closed, firm and foam surfaces), then tracked actual falls for 12 months. The phone measurements tracked the lab device closely (correlations of 0.71 for sway area and 0.80 for path length) and distinguished people who had fallen in the past from those who had not. But adding the smartphone measure to established fall risk factors barely budged prediction of future falls, and the phone measure was not a significant independent predictor.

**Why it matters:** Fall-risk apps are proliferating, and this large prospective study suggests that a phone can credibly stand in for expensive lab equipment to measure sway — yet measuring sway alone is not the same as knowing who will fall next year.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you try a balance-measuring app, treat the number as one snapshot of how steady you are today, not a forecast — the established basics (fall history, medications, vision, strength and home hazards) still carry more predictive weight, and those are what to raise with your clinician.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living operators weighing investment in smartphone-based screening tools, this is a useful reality check: the technology appears accurate enough for population-level balance measurement and flagging past fallers, but it should not replace comprehensive multifactorial fall risk assessment in care planning.

**Caveats:** Observational cohort, so causation cannot be established; the smartphone app's developer is an author and serves as president of the company that makes it (Control One LLC); single-region US community sample that may not generalize; fall history was self-reported.

### 19. Brief brain games played over several days picked up cognitive aging within three years

**Journal:** *JMIR Aging* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 27
**PMID:** 42954140 | **DOI:** 10.2196/100917

**The study:** Researchers gave 209 cognitively unimpaired older adults (mean age 73.6, SD 8.2; 65% women) from the Harvard Aging Brain Study a smartphone-based face-name memory task repeated across multiple days, in up to four testing intervals over an average of 2.8 years. Using computational modeling of response times, they found that participants' best expected speed slowed by about 0.04 seconds per year — roughly 2% annually. Older participants slowed faster, were more variable day to day, learned less across testing intervals, and forgot more between them.

**Why it matters:** Conventional memory testing once a year is often too blunt to show change over short periods; repeated at-home digital testing may detect subtle decline much earlier, which matters as treatments and trials increasingly target the earliest stages of Alzheimer's disease.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're offered a short at-home digital memory assessment through a research study or memory clinic, know that repeating brief tasks over several days can reveal patterns a single office visit can't — and that normal aging itself produces measurable slowing, so one slow day means little.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and clinicians, the useful signal here is pattern, not a single score: increasing day-to-day variability and more forgetting between sessions were features of older age, which may help distinguish genuine change from an off day during an office visit.

**Caveats:** Observational, no clinical outcomes (participants were cognitively unimpaired and not followed to diagnosis); highly educated, research-engaged volunteer cohort that may not generalize; relatively modest sample size; results reflect group-level trends, not individual diagnostic accuracy.

### 20. Portugal's dementia clinics reach fewer than 1 in 10 patients — a warning for every country rolling out new Alzheimer's drugs

**Journal:** *Alzheimers Dement (Amst)* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 28
**PMID:** 42953259 | **DOI:** 10.1002/dad2.70513

**The study:** A nationwide survey of 51 Portuguese units found only 20 organized memory clinics (17 within the National Health System), covering about 61% of the population. In 2024 those centers delivered 21,721 consultations — expert care for under 10% of the country's estimated Alzheimer's patients — and only 26.2% of patients received confirmatory biomarker testing via spinal fluid or amyloid PET. Experts estimated that just 701 patients nationwide could currently start a disease-modifying anti-amyloid drug.

**Why it matters:** The arrival of lecanemab and donanemab has dominated headlines, but this is a concrete measurement of the gap between approval and access: without biomarker testing, infusion capacity, MRI monitoring and trained specialists, most eligible patients cannot be identified, let alone treated.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or a partner are pursuing a memory evaluation, ask early and specifically about waiting times and whether biomarker confirmation (spinal fluid testing or amyloid PET) is available where you live — eligibility for the new drugs depends on getting that confirmation within a narrow window of early disease.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For care professionals and advocacy groups, this provides a replicable template for auditing local readiness — consultation volume versus estimated prevalence, biomarker access rates, and realistic treatment-start counts — and ammunition for arguing that diagnostic infrastructure, not drug supply, is the binding constraint.

**Caveats:** Survey-based, cross-sectional service-capacity study relying on expert estimates rather than patient-level data; single-country findings that may not transfer to other health systems; response rate and non-responding units could bias the capacity picture.

### 21. Which MRI scan your clinic uses could change whether you qualify for a new Alzheimer's drug

**Journal:** *Alzheimers Dement (Amst)* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 28
**PMID:** 42959393 | **DOI:** 10.1002/dad2.70524

**The study:** Mayo Clinic researchers retrospectively reviewed 55 adults (mean age 72.1) evaluated for cognitive concerns who had received both susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI) and gradient echo (GRE) MRI sequences. Three neuroradiologists independently counted tiny brain microbleeds, which determine eligibility and safety risk for anti-amyloid therapy. SWI detected more microbleeds than GRE (83 versus 54), and risk categories differed between the two sequences in about 38% of participants — higher on SWI in nearly a third.

**Why it matters:** Microbleed counts decide who can safely start lecanemab or donanemab and who is at elevated risk of amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA); if the answer depends on which scan type a center happens to use, identical patients could be told different things at different hospitals.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're being evaluated for an anti-amyloid Alzheimer's drug and the MRI result is borderline, it is reasonable to ask which MRI sequence was used and whether the microbleed count would change on a more sensitive scan — the thresholds in the drug labels were based largely on older GRE imaging.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For clinicians and memory-care programs, the practical takeaway is to document the sequence used alongside the microbleed count, since safety thresholds for SWI-based readings have not yet been established prospectively and cross-center comparisons may not be apples to apples.

**Caveats:** Small sample (N=55), single-center, retrospective design; no treatment or ARIA outcomes were measured, so the clinical consequences remain unproven; several authors report consulting, honoraria or trial roles with Eisai and Eli Lilly, the makers of the relevant drugs.

### 22. Falling blood pressure in later life may signal frailty — and frailty may lead to financially devastating medical bills

**Journal:** *J Nutr Health Aging* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 28
**PMID:** 42950708 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jnha.2026.101021

**The study:** Using 3,996 participants from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study, researchers mapped systolic blood pressure patterns from 2011 to 2015 and identified three groups: low-stable (50%), moderate-increasing (41%), and high-decreasing (8.7%). The high-decreasing group had significantly higher frailty scores in 2015, and that frailty statistically explained a link to catastrophic health expenditure in 2018 — household health costs exceeding 40% of capacity to pay. Notably, the direct and total effects of the blood pressure pattern on costs were not significant; only the indirect pathway through frailty was.

**Why it matters:** A blood pressure reading that drifts downward over years is often treated as good news, but it can reflect accumulating physical decline — and this analysis suggests that decline, not the blood pressure itself, is what drives families toward financial catastrophe.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If your blood pressure has been drifting down on its own without a medication change, mention it at your next visit and ask whether a frailty or functional check (walking speed, grip strength, weight loss, fatigue) is warranted — don't assume a lower number automatically means better health.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and care coordinators, this reframes frailty as a financial risk factor as well as a clinical one, supporting early attention to function, nutrition and mobility as part of household financial planning rather than a separate medical issue.

**Caveats:** Observational cohort with mediation analysis — it cannot establish causation, and the overall effect of blood pressure trajectory on expenditure was not statistically significant; the high-decreasing group was small (8.7%); data come from Chinese households and a health financing system that may not generalize elsewhere; expenditure and some health measures were self-reported.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**

- **Mood keeps turning out to be a physical risk factor in Parkinson's.** Last week's *In Parkinson's disease, depression may explain much of the link between advancing disease and memory problems* (PMID 42949295) is reinforced from a different direction by *Three simple office tests can flag which Parkinson's patients will fall in the next year* (PMID 42960478), where a depression score carried as much predictive weight as motor severity for both first and repeat falls. Two consecutive weeks now point to depression screening as a routine part of Parkinson's care rather than an optional extra.
- **Everyday function, cheaply measured, keeps outpredicting the expensive tests.** The previous digest's muscle-as-unrecorded-vital-sign cluster — *Weak bones plus weak muscles* (PMID 41818462), *Grip strength tracked with bone risk in adults over 40* (PMID 42946838), *Grip strength or memory* (PMID 42950710) — extends this week into *Daily low-dose aspirin didn't protect older adults' physical and mental capacity* (PMID 42955660), where a four-part capacity score predicted death, dementia and disability more than fourfold; *Walking while doing a mental task reveals hidden brain changes that normal walking misses* (PMID 42955373); and *After a minor stroke, how well you move at discharge predicts how far you'll go six months later* (PMID 42957010). In PMID 42960478, imaging and fluid biomarkers added almost nothing over clinic measures already on the chart.
- **Secondary fracture prevention gains another plank.** Last week's *Preventing fractures with zoledronate saves more money than the drug costs* (PMID 41968301) and *A broken hip or spine years ago may raise your fracture risk more than the standard calculator says* (PMID 41810867) are joined by *A hospital fracture follow-up service cut repeat breaks by more than half — but only after the first year* (PMID 42955080). Together they make the same argument from three angles: identify the people at real risk, treat them, and the economics and the outcomes both follow.
- **Household money is becoming a measurable clinical outcome.** *Caregivers are going broke, and almost nobody has studied whether help with money helps* (PMID 41371759) named the gap; *Falling blood pressure in later life may signal frailty — and frailty may lead to financially devastating medical bills* (PMID 42950708) now puts a pathway to it, with frailty — not the blood pressure trajectory itself — carrying the statistical link to catastrophic household health costs.
- **The weeks and months after a handoff remain the weak point.** *A single nurse making follow-up phone calls can free up dozens of hospital beds* (PMID 42946540) is echoed by *After hip fracture surgery, the real decline often starts in year two* (PMID 42956453) and PMID 42957010, both of which locate the risk well past the point where most follow-up stops.
- **"That's just aging" keeps being wrong.** Last week's autoimmune encephalitis study (PMID 42951877) and PMID 42949295 are matched by *The 'good days and bad days' of Lewy body dementia don't fade in advanced disease* (PMID 42953268), *Old head injuries may accelerate memory decline decades later* (PMID 42958736), and *Hearing loss plus memory trouble* (PMID 41916749) — fluctuating alertness read as inconsistency, a 40-year-old head injury left off the chart, hearing loss filed as a cosmetic nuisance.

**Advances**

- **Elder mistreatment moves from response to detection.** Last week's *A community program for elder abuse and self-neglect improved well-being in a randomized trial* (PMID 42951664) showed something can help once a case is identified. *Three in four families didn't bring their relative back for a dementia work-up* (PMID 42954618) addresses the step before that, arguing that caregiver-driven non-attendance may itself be a warning sign — though on a very small single-center sample.
- **Digital cognition shifts from intervention to measurement.** The chatbot training trial (PMID 42950244) tested whether an app could improve memory; *Brief brain games played over several days picked up cognitive aging within three years* (PMID 42954140) asks instead whether repeated brief digital testing can detect change a once-a-year office visit would miss, and finds measurable slowing of about 2% a year.
- **The individual-versus-group gap in biomarkers now has a service-capacity counterpart.** Running alongside the established pattern of biomarkers that work at group level and stall at the individual, *Portugal's dementia clinics reach fewer than 1 in 10 patients* (PMID 42953259) and *Which MRI scan your clinic uses could change whether you qualify for a new Alzheimer's drug* (PMID 42959393) show the problem is also logistical: without biomarker confirmation and consistent imaging, eligibility depends on where you happen to live and what machine protocol your center uses.

**Counterpoints**

- **Last week's good news for digital tools does not generalize.** *A 12-week chatbot "brain workout"* (PMID 42950244) found both test and imaging gains. This week, *Video and console-based balance training beats doing nothing — but not a regular exercise class* (PMID 42954170), *Your phone can measure your balance — but it still can't predict whether you'll fall* (PMID 42956683), and *Getting frail seniors online may not lift their mood* (PMID 42954453) all land on the same sober note: technology for older adults is often an access solution, not a better outcome.
- **A one-year evaluation window can make an effective program look useless.** PMID 42955080 found repeat fracture rates essentially identical at one year and sharply divergent by three — a caution to pair with last week's economic modeling studies (PMID 41968301, PMID 42946540), which assume you are measuring over the right horizon.
- **A popular preventive that didn't hold up.** PMID 42955660 found daily low-dose aspirin preserved no day-to-day capacity at all, and in women was linked to slightly faster decline — the clean-null pattern that keeps recurring for appealing self-directed interventions.

**New themes**

- A biological signature for arts and cultural participation: *Arts and museum visits leave a measurable fingerprint in the blood* (PMID 42957065).
- Delivery format as an active ingredient: *For people with memory problems, group classes beat one-on-one sessions* (PMID 42954294).
- Pushback on ageist assumptions about capability: *Older drivers aren't reckless* (PMID 42958638) and *Retiring makes people more helpful* (PMID 42956395).
- Infrastructure, not drug supply, as the binding constraint on new Alzheimer's treatments (PMID 42953259, PMID 42959393).
- Acupuncture as an add-on to postoperative pain plans after knee replacement (PMID 42956696).

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Three studies this week measure the same thing — recovery and prevention over a longer horizon than anyone usually looks — and all three find that the important part happens after the follow-up stops. *After hip fracture surgery, the real decline often starts in year two* (PMID 42956453) found care needs worsened in 10.6% of survivors by 12 months but 37.4% by 36; *A hospital fracture follow-up service cut repeat breaks by more than half — but only after the first year* (PMID 42955080) showed a program that looked like a failure at 12 months and a clear success at three years; and *After a minor stroke, how well you move at discharge predicts how far you'll go six months later* (PMID 42957010) locates the gap between "well enough to go home" and "able to get back out into the neighborhood." *The 'good days and bad days' of Lewy body dementia don't fade in advanced disease* (PMID 42953268) makes the same point for dementia care: the symptom everyone studies early is still there late, and still worth managing.

**Why pitch this now:** Families are routinely told the recovery window is 90 days, insurers and quality programs often measure at one year, and these studies suggest both horizons are too short — which means the decline that costs an older adult their independence tends to land after everyone has stopped watching.

**Angle:** A practical guide to the second and third year after a fracture, a stroke, or a dementia diagnosis — what to ask for at the 12- and 24-month marks, how to keep rehabilitation and bone-health treatment going when nothing dramatic seems to be happening, and why "no change yet" is not the same as "not working." Written for adult children and spouses doing the follow-up, with a shorter section for care coordinators on extending care plans past the usual review points.

**Potential headlines:**

- Most Hip Fracture Patients Who Lose Ground Lose It in Year Two
- The Bone Clinic That Looked Like a Failure at One Year Had Cut Repeat Breaks by Half at Three
- When the Check-Ins Stop, the Decline Starts — and How Families Can Keep the Clock Running

**Potential outlets:**

- **Next Avenue** — its readers are the adult children managing a parent's recovery calendar, and this gives them specific dates to put on it rather than a general warning to stay vigilant.
- **Caring.com** — decision-stage families comparing rehab and care options can use "what happens at month 18?" as a concrete question when they tour or interview providers.
- **LeadingAge** — directly actionable for nonprofit providers: care plans for fracture and stroke survivors built around 24- and 36-month reassessment, and secondary fracture prevention tracked as a resident-level metric.
- **National Council on Aging** — falls and fracture prevention sit at the center of its practical guidance, and the fracture-liaison finding strengthens the case for sticking with osteoporosis treatment through a quiet first year.

## Story Ideas by Study

- **Daily low-dose aspirin didn't protect older adults' physical and mental capacity — and women fared slightly worse (PMID 42955660):** For the many people taking daily aspirin on their own initiative rather than a prescription, a clear script for the conversation to have with a doctor — including why you should never stop a prescribed aspirin unilaterally, for a consumer health explainer or Medicare-age medication column.
- **For people with memory problems, group classes beat one-on-one sessions — especially for thinking skills (PMID 42954294):** Format is part of the treatment — how to tell whether a memory-support program is genuinely multicomponent or just worksheets, for a caregiver-advice column with an activity-director trade sidebar.
- **Walking while doing a mental task reveals hidden brain changes that normal walking misses (PMID 42955373):** The "stops walking when talking" observation anyone can make in a hallway, framed as a prompt for proper assessment rather than a diagnosis, for a consumer health explainer or a senior-living clinical-staff brief.
- **The 'good days and bad days' of Lewy body dementia don't fade in advanced disease — and two standard scales barely agree (PMID 42953268):** Validation for families who are told they're imagining the swings, plus the practical move of charting good hours and scheduling visits and assessments around them, for a care-partner column.
- **Three simple office tests can flag which Parkinson's patients will fall in the next year (PMID 42960478):** Mood scored as heavily as motor severity in predicting falls — so a rising depression score belongs in the falls conversation, for a consumer neurology explainer or a care-team trade brief on fall-risk review.
- **After hip fracture surgery, the real decline often starts in year two — and longer rehab was linked to less of it (PMID 42956453):** A concrete scheduling ask — formal reassessment at 12 and 24 months, not just 90 days — for a caregiver-advice column or a long-term care trade piece on care-plan horizons.
- **A hospital fracture follow-up service cut repeat breaks by more than half — but only after the first year (PMID 42955080):** Ask the hospital whether it has a fracture liaison service, get referred, and don't quit when year one feels uneventful, for a consumer health explainer plus an operator brief on tracking residents after any low-trauma fracture.
- **Video and console-based balance training beats doing nothing — but not a regular exercise class (PMID 42954170):** The honest buying guide for exergaming and telerehab — reach, not superiority — with the caveat that no trial measured actual falls, for a senior-living wellness trade brief and a consumer technology-and-health item.
- **Acupuncture after knee replacement eased pain and improved function in older patients, review finds (PMID 42956696):** A reasonable question to raise with a surgical team as an add-on to the pain plan, with a clear-eyed note on why acupuncture effect sizes are often inflated, for a consumer health explainer ahead of elective surgery.
- **Older drivers aren't reckless — they slow down, brake earlier and crash less in simulator tests (PMID 42958638):** Reframe the family conversation from "should they still drive?" to "what adaptations are they making, and are they holding?" — with the loss of self-imposed limits as the real warning sign, for a caregiver-advice column or local-news segment.
- **Retiring makes people more helpful — European study tracks the same individuals before and after (PMID 42956395):** Purpose after retirement, with an operator takeaway — newly retired residents are an untapped volunteer pool in their first year, for a Next Avenue–style aging-and-purpose piece or a community-engagement trade brief.
- **Getting frail seniors online may not lift their mood, large British study finds (PMID 42954453):** For families and communities running tablet and Wi-Fi programs: measure meaningful connection, not logins — and don't let digital access stand in for addressing frailty or treating depression, for a consumer health explainer or senior-living technology trade piece.
- **Three in four families didn't bring their relative back for a dementia work-up — even after the cost barrier was removed (PMID 42954618):** A missed appointment as a possible red flag rather than an administrative nuisance, plus how an older adult can secure their own route to a diagnostic appointment, for a caregiver-advice column or an aging-services trade piece (with explicit framing that the 75% figure is from one small Turkish clinic).
- **Arts and museum visits leave a measurable fingerprint in the blood — and track with lower dementia and heart disease risk (PMID 42957065):** Variety of cultural engagement, not just frequency — and a defensible reason to budget for real outings instead of one recurring activity, for a consumer lifestyle-and-health feature plus an activity-programming trade brief.
- **Old head injuries may accelerate memory decline decades later, veteran study suggests (PMID 42958736):** Head injury history belongs on the intake form next to medications — relevant to veterans, former athletes and anyone with an old fall or crash, for a consumer health explainer or a veteran-focused outlet.
- **Hearing loss plus memory trouble: brain scans show who is most likely to progress to dementia (PMID 41916749):** Raise hearing and memory at the same appointment instead of in separate silos — and for care staff, the low-cost work of making sure hearing aids are charged and actually worn, for a consumer health explainer with a senior-living operations note.
- **After a minor stroke, how well you move at discharge predicts how far you'll go six months later (PMID 42957010):** The questions to ask at discharge about walking and balance capacity, and why "getting back out" deserves to be an explicit therapy goal, for a consumer discharge-planning explainer or rehab trade brief.
- **Your phone can measure your balance — but it still can't predict whether you'll fall (PMID 42956683):** A reality check on fall-risk apps for both consumers and operators — accurate enough to measure sway, not a substitute for a full falls assessment, and note the developer sits among the authors, for a consumer technology explainer or senior-living procurement brief.
- **Brief brain games played over several days picked up cognitive aging within three years (PMID 42954140):** Why pattern over several days beats a single office score, and what a slow day does and doesn't mean, for a consumer health explainer on at-home cognitive testing.
- **Portugal's dementia clinics reach fewer than 1 in 10 patients — a warning for every country rolling out new Alzheimer's drugs (PMID 42953259):** Ask early about waiting times and whether biomarker confirmation is available locally, since eligibility for the new drugs depends on a narrow window — and for advocates, a replicable template for auditing local readiness, for a consumer explainer plus a policy-and-advocacy trade piece.
- **Which MRI scan your clinic uses could change whether you qualify for a new Alzheimer's drug (PMID 42959393):** Identical patients can get different answers at different hospitals — a specific, polite question to ask if a microbleed result is borderline, for a consumer neurology explainer with a memory-care clinical brief (flag the authors' industry ties).
