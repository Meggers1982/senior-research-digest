### 1. Belly Fat Plus Weak Muscles: A Double Threat for Falls in Older Adults

**Journal:** *Australasian Journal on Ageing* | **Published:** June 2026
**PMID:** 42223007 | **DOI:** 10.1111/ajag.70188

**The study:** Researchers tracked 9,364 Chinese adults aged 45 and older over seven years, examining how the combination of weak grip strength (dynapenia) and abdominal obesity affected fall risk. People with both conditions had a 28% higher fall risk overall, but the pattern differed dramatically by sex: men with both conditions faced more than double the fall risk (126% increase), while women's risk was driven primarily by muscle weakness alone.

**Why it matters:** This research identifies a specific, modifiable combination of risk factors—belly fat plus muscle weakness—that dramatically increases fall danger, particularly for men and people with diabetes.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you carry weight around your middle and have noticed declining strength, this combination may put you at higher fall risk than either condition alone. Ask your doctor about simple grip strength tests and discuss whether targeted exercise and dietary changes might help reduce your risk.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living fitness programs may need to move beyond general exercise classes to address body composition screening, particularly for male residents and those with diabetes, who showed the strongest risk associations.

**Caveats:** Observational design (cannot establish causation); self-reported falls; population is Chinese adults, which may not generalize to all ethnic groups.

### 2. The Winning Combination: Exercise Plus Protein for Preventing Sarcopenia-Related Falls

**Journal:** *Journal of Nutrition, Health and Aging* | **Published:** June 2026
**PMID:** 42208412 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jnha.2026.100862

**The study:** This umbrella review synthesized findings from 41 systematic reviews covering approximately 4,475 primary studies. Researchers found that combining resistance and balance training with protein intake of at least 1.2 grams per kilogram of body weight daily produced the best improvements in muscle mass and strength. Balance and multicomponent exercise programs reduced fall rates by 23-34%, while vitamin D supplementation (at least 700 IU daily) reduced falls by 13%.

**Why it matters:** This comprehensive analysis provides the clearest evidence yet for what works in preventing sarcopenia-related falls: a three-pronged approach combining specific exercises, adequate protein, and vitamin D.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** The research points to a practical formula: aim for balance exercises plus strength training, protein at each meal (roughly 25-30 grams for a 150-pound person three times daily), and vitamin D supplementation. Discuss these targets with your healthcare provider.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Dining programs in senior living communities should evaluate whether meal plans deliver adequate protein distribution throughout the day, as this appears critical for maintaining the muscle mass that prevents falls.

**Caveats:** Umbrella review relies on previously published meta-analyses; direct evidence that reversing sarcopenia reduces falls remains limited.

### 3. Virtual Counseling Shows Promise for Fall Prevention

**Journal:** *JMIR Aging* | **Published:** May 2026
**PMID:** 42190243 | **DOI:** 10.2196/78444

**The study:** This scoping review examined 13 studies of digital counseling interventions for fall prevention in adults 60 and older across Asia, Europe, and North America. Interventions included virtual reality, smartphone apps, teleconsultations, and wearable devices with feedback systems. Fall reductions ranged from 15% to over 90% across studies, with improvements also seen in balance, mobility, and self-confidence.

**Why it matters:** For older adults who cannot easily access in-person fall prevention programs—due to transportation barriers, mobility limitations, or geographic isolation—technology-based alternatives may offer effective support.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If getting to fall prevention classes is difficult, ask your doctor about telehealth options or app-based programs. Programs that include digital literacy support showed higher engagement, so don't hesitate to ask for help learning new technology.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living communities in rural areas or those serving residents with transportation challenges should consider adding technology-based fall prevention options, with attention to cultural tailoring and tech support.

**Caveats:** Scoping review (not a meta-analysis); wide variation in study quality and reported outcomes; most studies conducted in high-income settings.

### 4. Smartphone App Plus Group Classes: A Feasible Approach for Fall-Prone Older Adults

**Journal:** *JMIR Aging* | **Published:** May 2026
**PMID:** 42150160 | **DOI:** 10.2196/87577

**The study:** Forty-five community-dwelling adults aged 65+ with fall histories participated in a 24-week program combining cognitive training via smartphone app with balance and strength exercises. The first 12 weeks featured weekly physiotherapist-led group classes plus home sessions; the final 12 weeks were entirely home-based. Adherence was 81% during supervised phases and 50% during independent phases. No adverse events occurred, and 76% of participants completed the program.

**Why it matters:** This study demonstrates that older adults with fall histories can safely use technology-enhanced exercise programs, though social connection and professional support appear important for maintaining participation.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you've fallen before and want to reduce your risk, a program combining group exercise classes with home practice using a smartphone app may be worth exploring. The social connection of group sessions may help you stay motivated.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Community-based organizations rather than healthcare systems may be more effective recruitment channels for fall prevention programs, suggesting senior centers and community groups should be primary partners for outreach.

**Caveats:** Single-arm study (no control group); small sample size (N=45); adherence dropped significantly when supervision ended.

### 5. AI-Powered Home Safety Checks: A New Frontier in Fall Prevention

**Journal:** *JMIR Aging* | **Published:** May 2026
**PMID:** 42127229 | **DOI:** 10.2196/77591

**The study:** Researchers developed and tested a model using multimodal large language AI models that can analyze photos of living spaces, identify fall hazards, and generate visualized recommendations for home modifications. A pilot study found the AI could produce safe, actionable advice and create realistic before-and-after images showing recommended changes.

**Why it matters:** This emerging technology could make personalized home safety assessments more accessible, potentially allowing older adults to identify and address fall hazards without waiting for professional home visits.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** While still experimental, AI tools that can review photos of your home and suggest safety improvements may become available in coming years. In the meantime, consider asking family members to help photograph your living spaces for review with your healthcare provider.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living facility managers and home care agencies should monitor the development of AI-powered safety assessment tools, which could supplement professional evaluations and help prioritize home modifications.

**Caveats:** Concept demonstration only; not clinically tested or compared to professional assessments; requires further validation before practical use.

### 6. Telemedicine Fall Prevention Program Likely Cost-Effective

**Journal:** *Age and Ageing* | **Published:** June 2026
**PMID:** 42315165 | **DOI:** 10.1093/ageing/afag141

**The study:** Researchers analyzed the cost-effectiveness of the STEADI (Stopping Elderly Accidents, Deaths, and Injuries) telemedicine program compared to standard care for adults 65 and older at increased fall risk. The intervention group had fewer medically treated falls and lower healthcare costs (-$873 per person), with a positive incremental net benefit of $1,995 per enrollee. The program was cost-saving in 72% of simulations.

**Why it matters:** This evidence supports broader implementation of telemedicine-based fall prevention, suggesting it can reduce both falls and healthcare spending.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Ask your healthcare provider whether telemedicine-based fall prevention programs like STEADI are available in your area. These programs may help reduce your fall risk while being convenient and potentially cost-saving.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Healthcare systems and senior living organizations should consider implementing telemedicine fall prevention as a cost-effective intervention, particularly as the model showed benefits across a wide range of assumptions.

**Caveats:** Differences in falls and costs were not statistically significant in the trial itself; cost-effectiveness analysis relies on modeling assumptions; cannot rule out possibility of no effect.

### 7. Hip Strength and Diuretics: Overlooked Factors in Predicting Injurious Falls

**Journal:** *Archives of Gerontology and Geriatrics* | **Published:** June 2026
**PMID:** 42296796 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.archger.2026.106333

**The study:** Analyzing 952 Australian adults aged 65 and older over multiple years, researchers found that hip abductor strength and diuretic medication use contributed most strongly to predicting falls causing emergency department visits. Adding these factors to standard assessments (age, sex, mobility, walking aids) improved prediction accuracy by 3-3.5 percentage points each.

**Why it matters:** This research identifies specific, actionable targets—strengthening hip muscles and reviewing diuretic medications—that may improve fall risk prediction and prevention.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you take water pills (diuretics), discuss with your doctor whether they're still necessary and if dosage adjustments might be appropriate. Also ask about exercises specifically targeting hip strength, which appears particularly important for preventing serious falls.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Medication reviews in senior living should pay particular attention to diuretics, and exercise programs should ensure hip abductor strengthening is included—these two modifiable factors showed the strongest predictive value for injurious falls.

**Caveats:** Single Australian cohort; observational design; prediction improvement was modest; may not generalize to other populations.

### 8. Dutch Fall Clinics Show Gaps in Heart-Related Fall Assessments

**Journal:** *European Geriatric Medicine* | **Published:** June 2026
**PMID:** 42230523 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s41999-026-01481-3

**The study:** Researchers interviewed 14 healthcare professionals across eight Dutch fall clinics to compare recommended cardiovascular fall assessments with actual practice. While basic assessments like ECGs were performed consistently, more advanced testing (such as tilt-table testing to detect blood pressure drops) varied widely due to staffing shortages, knowledge gaps, and limited resources.

**Why it matters:** Cardiovascular problems are important but often overlooked causes of falls. This study reveals that even specialized fall clinics may not be fully evaluating these factors, potentially missing preventable causes.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you've had unexplained falls, ask whether your heart and blood pressure responses have been fully evaluated. Tests like tilt-table testing can identify blood pressure drops that cause fainting or unsteadiness.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living communities should ensure their fall assessment protocols include cardiovascular screening and establish referral pathways to specialists when advanced testing is needed.

**Caveats:** Qualitative study of Dutch clinics only; did not measure patient outcomes; barriers identified may differ in other healthcare systems.

### 9. Pelvic Floor Training Reduces Incontinence and May Help Prevent Falls

**Journal:** *Archives of Gerontology and Geriatrics* | **Published:** May 2026
**PMID:** 42229075 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.archger.2026.106315

**The study:** This systematic review of 20 randomized controlled trials involving 2,002 women aged 60 and older found that pelvic floor muscle training significantly reduced urinary incontinence severity. Multimodal programs combining pelvic floor exercises with mobility, strength, or fall-prevention training showed additional benefits for functional independence.

**Why it matters:** Urinary incontinence often leads to rushing to the bathroom—a common fall scenario. Addressing incontinence through physical therapy may serve double duty by reducing both bathroom accidents and fall risk.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If bladder control issues have you rushing to the bathroom, pelvic floor exercises may help. Ask your doctor about physical therapy options—addressing incontinence may also reduce your fall risk.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living communities should consider integrating pelvic floor training into their wellness programming, as incontinence is both underreported and a contributor to fall risk.

**Caveats:** Meta-analysis focused on incontinence outcomes; fall prevention benefits were secondary findings; most participants were motivated volunteers.

### 10. Large-Movement Exercises May Beat Traditional Programs for Balance

**Journal:** *Journal of Aging and Physical Activity* | **Published:** May 2026
**PMID:** 42219157 | **DOI:** 10.1123/japa.2025-0400

**The study:** Seventy nursing home residents aged 65 and older were randomly assigned to either the modified Otago Exercise Program (a well-established fall prevention approach) or the Large-Amplitude Functional Exercise Program (LAFEP), originally developed for Parkinson's disease. After 12 weeks of supervised group sessions three times weekly, the LAFEP group showed significantly greater improvements in dynamic balance, specifically in the functional reach test.

**Why it matters:** The LAFEP approach, which emphasizes large, exaggerated movements, may offer superior balance benefits for nursing home residents compared to conventional fall prevention exercises.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're in a care setting and participate in exercise classes, ask whether programs emphasizing large, expansive movements might be available. These may provide additional balance benefits beyond traditional exercises.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Activity directors in nursing homes should consider evaluating the Large-Amplitude Functional Exercise Program as an alternative or supplement to traditional fall prevention exercises, particularly for improving dynamic balance.

**Caveats:** Small sample (N=70); single-site nursing home study; only dynamic balance showed statistically significant between-group differences.

### 11. High Exercise Adherence Key to Fall Prevention Success

**Journal:** *Archives of Gerontology and Geriatrics* | **Published:** October 2026
**PMID:** 42176369 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.archger.2026.106304

**The study:** This meta-analysis of 41 publications covering 20,202 community-dwelling older adults found that multicomponent exercise programs reduced fall rates by 22%, risk of any fall by 9%, and fall-related injuries by 25%. Critically, programs achieving 75% or higher adherence showed nearly double the effectiveness, with 50% reduction in fall rates versus 22% for lower-adherence programs.

**Why it matters:** This research demonstrates that showing up consistently matters enormously—programs work much better when participants complete at least three-quarters of sessions.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Committing to your fall prevention exercise program makes a measurable difference. Programs that combine individual and group formats may help you stay motivated—the social connection of group sessions combined with flexibility of home practice showed the highest adherence rates.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living exercise programs should track adherence rates and implement strategies to maintain participation above 75%. Hybrid formats combining group and individual sessions appear to optimize both engagement and effectiveness.

**Caveats:** Adherence was often self-reported; evidence for injury prevention was more limited than for fall prevention; optimal program duration unclear.

### 12. Pickleball Improves Movement Coordination in Pre-Frail Older Adults

**Journal:** *Experimental Gerontology* | **Published:** June 2026
**PMID:** 42049158 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.exger.2026.113154

**The study:** Sixty-one pre-frail community-dwelling adults (median age 67) were randomly assigned to eight weeks of twice-weekly supervised pickleball or a control group. While walking speed didn't change, the pickleball group showed improved coordination between hip, knee, and ankle joints during dual-task walking (walking while performing another mental or physical task)—a scenario that commonly precedes falls.

**Why it matters:** Pickleball's rapid decision-making and movement variability may train the brain-body connection needed for safe walking in real-world conditions where attention is divided.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're looking for an enjoyable way to improve your balance, pickleball may offer benefits beyond traditional exercise. The sport's quick reactions and varied movements appear to improve how your joints work together during everyday activities.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living recreation programs should consider pickleball not just as social activity but as potential fall prevention programming, particularly for residents showing early signs of frailty.

**Caveats:** Small sample (N=61); pre-frail participants only (may not apply to frailer individuals); no direct fall outcome measured; short intervention period.

### 13. Neighborhoods with Parks and Walking Paths Linked to Fewer Falls

**Journal:** *Archives of Gerontology and Geriatrics* | **Published:** August 2026
**PMID:** 42025148 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.archger.2026.106258

**The study:** Following 27,346 Japanese adults aged 65 and older over three years, researchers found that living in areas with abundant parks and walking paths was associated with 11% lower fall risk. Surprisingly, moderate presence of challenging terrain (hills, steps) was associated with 6% lower risk, while very flat areas showed no protective effect.

**Why it matters:** Where you live may influence your fall risk. Access to safe walking areas encourages physical activity, and some environmental challenge may help maintain balance abilities.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If possible, seek out neighborhoods or living situations with accessible parks and walking paths. Some terrain variety in your walking routes may actually help maintain your balance skills, though extremely challenging terrain should be avoided.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living site selection and community design should prioritize walkable outdoor spaces. Facility grounds that offer some gentle terrain variation rather than exclusively flat surfaces may support better balance maintenance.

**Caveats:** Japanese population (may not generalize to other settings); observational design; environmental factors measured at neighborhood level rather than individual exposure.

### 14. Provider Perspectives Reveal Barriers to Community Fall Prevention Programs

**Journal:** *Canadian Journal on Aging* | **Published:** April 2026
**PMID:** 42010817 | **DOI:** 10.1017/S0714980826100646

**The study:** Researchers interviewed 20 exercise providers delivering fall prevention programs in Ontario, Canada. Providers valued evidence-based training but struggled with tailoring exercises in large groups. Key barriers varied by setting: kinesiologists lacked resources, independent providers lacked professional networks, and municipal/nonprofit staff faced low pay and competition between organizations.

**Why it matters:** Understanding what exercise instructors face helps explain why effective fall prevention programs don't always reach older adults who need them.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** When choosing fall prevention programs, ask about instructor qualifications and class sizes. Smaller classes may allow for better individualized attention, though the social connections in larger groups also provide value.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Organizations offering fall prevention should address provider-specific needs: professional development for isolated practitioners, adequate compensation for nonprofit staff, and resources for clinical tailoring in group settings.

**Caveats:** Ontario-specific context; qualitative study (provider perspectives, not outcome data); small sample of providers.

### 15. World Guidelines Algorithm Tested in Brazil Reveals Screening Approach Matters

**Journal:** *European Geriatric Medicine* | **Published:** April 2026
**PMID:** 41917319 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s41999-026-01446-6

**The study:** Using data from 7,515 Brazilian adults aged 60 and older, researchers compared two methods of applying the World Guidelines for Falls Prevention algorithm. Using fall history alone, 82% were classified as low risk. Using key screening questions (fear of falling, balance problems) plus gait speed, only 50% were low risk—dramatically increasing identification of intermediate-risk individuals who might benefit from intervention.

**Why it matters:** How healthcare providers screen for fall risk significantly affects who gets identified for prevention. Simply asking about past falls misses many at-risk individuals.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Don't wait until you fall to discuss fall prevention with your doctor. If you're concerned about falling or notice balance changes, bring these up—screening methods that include these questions identify more people who could benefit from prevention programs.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living communities should consider implementing standardized fall risk screening that goes beyond fall history to include questions about fear of falling and simple balance tests, which identify substantially more at-risk residents.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional study (single time point); Brazilian population; did not track whether different screening approaches led to different outcomes.

### 16. Virtual Fall Prevention Programs Reduce Falls but Miss Social Benefits

**Journal:** *Journal of Aging and Physical Activity* | **Published:** March 2026
**PMID:** 41871377 | **DOI:** 10.1123/japa.2024-0211

**The study:** Comparing 382 in-person and 214 virtual fall prevention program participants aged 50 and older, researchers found virtual participants reported 44% fewer falls at six months. However, only in-person participants showed improvements in social connectedness and loneliness. Virtual programs also reached fewer Black participants (8% vs. 44%) and lower-income individuals.

**Why it matters:** Virtual fall prevention programs effectively reduce falls but sacrifice the social benefits of in-person participation and may worsen health disparities by reaching less diverse populations.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're choosing between in-person and virtual fall prevention programs, consider what you need most. Virtual programs effectively reduce falls, but in-person programs offer social connection that also benefits health and wellbeing.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Organizations offering virtual fall prevention should actively develop strategies to foster social connection within programs and conduct targeted outreach to ensure racially diverse and lower-income communities can access these services.

**Caveats:** Non-randomized comparison; self-selected participants; demographic differences between groups may confound results.

### 17. European Initiative Improves Osteoporosis Treatment and Fall Prevention After Hip Fracture

**Journal:** *European Geriatric Medicine* | **Published:** May 2026
**PMID:** 42168731 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s41999-026-01497-9

**The study:** Six European orthogeriatric units implemented a quality improvement initiative focusing on osteoporosis treatment and fall prevention after hip fracture. Using education, patient engagement materials, and standardized protocols, the initiative increased treatment rates and established new fall-prevention clinics in multiple countries. Healthcare workers were satisfied with the training, and patients reported feeling more informed.

**Why it matters:** After a hip fracture, preventing the next fracture and fall is critical. This practical, low-cost approach shows how hospitals can systematically improve these outcomes.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or a loved one has had a hip fracture, ask about osteoporosis treatment and fall prevention referrals before leaving the hospital. These follow-up steps are critical for preventing another fracture.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living communities receiving residents after hip fracture should verify that osteoporosis assessment and fall prevention services were initiated during hospitalization, and continue these interventions during rehabilitation.

**Caveats:** Quality improvement project (not a controlled trial); early implementation phase; outcomes measured over short timeframe.

### 18. Multiple Risk Factors Combine to Increase Falls in Indian Older Adults

**Journal:** *European Geriatric Medicine* | **Published:** April 2026
**PMID:** 41528689 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s41999-026-01408-y

**The study:** Analyzing data from 26,780 community-dwelling Indians aged 60 and older, researchers found 11.6% reported falls in the past year, with higher rates among women and increasing age. Independent risk factors included female sex, underweight BMI, tobacco and alcohol use, chronic joint disease, prior injuries, sensory impairments, depression, difficulty with daily activities, and trouble climbing stairs.

**Why it matters:** This comprehensive analysis of a large, diverse population identifies multiple modifiable risk factors that could be targeted for fall prevention in community settings.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Fall risk involves many factors working together. If you have joint pain, vision or hearing problems, feel depressed, or struggle with daily activities like climbing stairs, discuss fall prevention strategies with your healthcare provider.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Fall prevention programs in senior living should screen for and address the full range of risk factors—physical, sensory, psychological, and functional—rather than focusing on any single domain.

**Caveats:** Indian population (cultural and healthcare context may differ); self-reported falls and health conditions; cross-sectional design limits causal conclusions.

### 19. Multiple Exercise Types Reduce Fall Risk More Than Walking Alone—Especially for Women

**Journal:** *Journal of Aging and Physical Activity* | **Published:** June 2026
**PMID:** 40992429 | **DOI:** 10.1123/japa.2024-0354

**The study:** Using data from nearly 69,000 Korean adults aged 65 and older, researchers identified distinct physical activity patterns and their relationship to falls. Among women, those who only walked or walked plus did flexibility exercises had 9-13% higher fall risk compared to women who combined moderate-intensity activity, walking, and flexibility exercises. Among men, no activity pattern showed significantly different fall risk after adjusting for other factors.

**Why it matters:** For older women especially, variety in exercise matters—walking alone may not provide adequate fall protection compared to combining different types and intensities of activity.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If walking is your main exercise, consider adding moderate-intensity activities and flexibility work. For women especially, this combination appears to provide better fall protection than walking alone.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living activity programming should offer varied exercise options beyond walking groups, with particular attention to ensuring women residents have access to moderate-intensity and flexibility activities.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional Korean data; self-reported physical activity and falls; sex differences observed may reflect cultural patterns.

### 20. Fear of Falling Shaped by Psychological Factors, Not Just Physical Risk

**Journal:** *BMC Geriatrics* | **Published:** June 2026
**PMID:** 42249310 | **DOI:** 10.1186/s12877-026-07784-1

**The study:** Through in-depth interviews with 20 older Thai adults with concern about falling, researchers found that how people interpret falls and aging shapes their perceived control and behavior. Those who transformed initial fear into "authentic awareness," accepted aging adaptively, and used proportionate safety strategies maintained activity and confidence. Those with persistent worry, fatalistic beliefs, or excessive caution showed restricted participation and declining confidence.

**Why it matters:** Fear of falling can be protective (increasing appropriate caution) or harmful (causing activity avoidance). Understanding psychological patterns can help distinguish healthy awareness from problematic fear.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Some concern about falling is healthy and can motivate good choices. But if worry about falls is causing you to avoid activities you value, speaking with a healthcare provider about addressing both the physical and psychological aspects of fall fear may help.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Fall prevention programs should address not just physical risk but also psychological factors—helping residents develop adaptive rather than avoidant responses to fall concerns, maintaining activity while taking appropriate precautions.

**Caveats:** Qualitative study with 20 Thai participants; cultural context may influence findings; no intervention tested.

### 21. VR Serious Game for Fall Prevention Shows Promise

**Journal:** *Geriatric Nursing* | **Published:** May 2026

**The study:** Brazilian researchers developed "PrevineQuedas," a virtual reality game using smartphone and Google Cardboard headset to teach fall prevention through immersive home environment scenarios. Players have two minutes to identify risk objects or preventive elements by moving their head and eyes. Community-dwelling older adults rated usability at 91% and player experience at 100%.

- **To older adults and seniors:** Learning about fall hazards might become more engaging through virtual reality games. If such programs become available in your community, they offer an interactive way to practice identifying risks in home environments.

### 22. Fear of Dependency and Tiredness Predict Fear of Falling

**Journal:** *Geriatric Nursing* | **Published:** May 2026
**PMID:** 42167083 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.gerinurse.2026.104088

**The study:** In 509 Portuguese adults aged 65 and older, researchers identified factors associated with fear of falling. Beyond known physical factors (mobility, balance, previous falls), fear of becoming dependent on others and experiencing tiredness emerged as significant independent predictors. Mobility/balance problems (measured by the Timed Up and Go test) showed the strongest association, but psychological factors explained additional variance.

**Why it matters:** Fear of falling isn't just about physical risk—concerns about losing independence and feeling tired also contribute, suggesting that comprehensive approaches addressing these factors may be more effective.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're afraid of falling, the fear may relate to concerns about losing independence or to feeling tired, not just physical risk. Addressing fatigue and discussing independence concerns with your healthcare provider may help alongside physical interventions.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Fall prevention assessments should include screening for fear of dependency and fatigue, which independently predict fear of falling and may respond to different interventions than physical risk factors.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional Portuguese study; self-reported measures; cultural context may influence fear of dependency findings.

### 23. What Older Australians Want in Fall Prevention Programs

**Journal:** *BMC Geriatrics* | **Published:** May 2026
**PMID:** 42163166 | **DOI:** 10.1186/s12877-026-07602-8

**The study:** A discrete choice experiment with 383 Australians aged 60 and older revealed preferences for fall prevention program features. Participants generally preferred lower-cost, individual programs lasting 1-3 months, delivered in-person at home with on-demand professional support. Three distinct groups emerged: "opt-out" (19% unlikely to join any program), "cost-sensitive" (37% responsive to price), and "support-oriented" (44% valuing professional guidance regardless of cost).

**Why it matters:** Understanding what older adults actually want in fall prevention programs can improve uptake and adherence, which are critical for effectiveness.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If fall prevention programs haven't appealed to you before, consider what features matter most. Options may include in-home programs, short-term commitments, or different cost structures that better match your preferences.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Fall prevention programs should offer flexible configurations—subsidized options for cost-sensitive individuals, enhanced support options for those willing to pay, and recognition that about one in five older adults may need different outreach approaches entirely.

**Caveats:** Australian preferences may not generalize globally; hypothetical choices may differ from actual behavior; online survey excluded those without internet access.

### 24. Polypharmacy Linked to Worse Outcomes After Staircase Falls

**Journal:** *BMC Geriatrics* | **Published:** May 2026
**PMID:** 42163109 | **DOI:** 10.1186/s12877-026-07627-z

**The study:** Analyzing 529 older adults who visited a Swiss emergency department after staircase falls, researchers found that 46% were taking five or more medications (polypharmacy). These patients were more likely to require hospital admission (71% vs. 62%) and had lower long-term survival rates. After adjusting for age and other factors, polypharmacy remained an independent predictor of mortality.

**Why it matters:** Taking multiple medications is common among older adults and appears to worsen outcomes even when falls occur, independent of the underlying health conditions requiring those medications.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you take five or more medications regularly, ask your doctor or pharmacist about a medication review to identify any that might be unnecessary or contributing to fall risk. After a fall, this review becomes especially important.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living medication review protocols should be particularly thorough for residents who have fallen, as polypharmacy independently predicts poorer outcomes. Deprescribing efforts may improve not just fall prevention but also post-fall recovery.

**Caveats:** Single Swiss hospital; retrospective design; polypharmacy may be a marker for underlying illness severity despite statistical adjustments.

### 25. Diabetes Linked to Balance Problems and Fall Risk

**Journal:** *Gerontology* | **Published:** May 2026
**PMID:** 42160236 | **DOI:**
