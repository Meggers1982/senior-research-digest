### Study 5: The lab test that looks least like real life predicts it best
**PMID:** 42765144 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Self-report finding underplayed** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "The speed of executive function performance, not the realistic-looking clinic tasks, was the only measure that reliably flagged people who were inaccurate or late with their real-world tasks."
- **Abstract says:** "Analyses showed that (a) EF performance speed and self-reported IADLs emerged as unique predictors (i.e., beyond each other) of the accuracy of the DAILIES task completion... However, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses revealed that only EF performance speed had utility in identifying participants with below average accuracy and timeliness"
- **Problem:** The ROC statement is accurate, but the regression results showed self-reported IADLs also uniquely predicted accuracy. The digest mentions this only obliquely in Caveats and the family-facing angle implies a checklist adds little.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add a clause such as 'self-report questionnaires also independently predicted task accuracy, though only executive function speed reliably identified below-average performers.'"

**Issue B. Institution/single-center claim** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Researchers at the University of Utah gave 114 community-dwelling older adults... Small sample (N=114), single-center"
- **Abstract says:** "Author affiliations list Psychology, University of Utah Health, USA and Psychiatry and Behavioral Health, The Ohio State University Wexner Medical Center, USA; the abstract does not state the number of recruitment sites."
- **Problem:** The abstract lists two institutions and never describes the study as single-center, so both the attribution and the 'single-center' caveat go beyond what can be verified.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say 'researchers at the University of Utah and Ohio State' and drop or hedge 'single-center' as unverifiable from the abstract."

### Study 8: Medicare billing records miss most dementia cases — and miss more of them in Medicare Advantage plans
**PMID:** 42760257 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Specificity described as ruling out** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Both algorithms were highly accurate at ruling dementia out (specificity above 97.9%)"
- **Abstract says:** "both algorithms demonstrated comparable performance in MA and FFS populations, with high specificity (>97.9%) and negative predictive value [NPV] (>92.9%)"
- **Problem:** High specificity means few false positives (good for ruling dementia in), not that a negative claims result rules dementia out; the 'rule-out' claim depends on the high NPV, which is driven by low prevalence, and sits awkwardly next to sensitivity of only 24-49%.
- **Suggested fix:** "Rewrite as 'rarely labelled someone with dementia who did not have it (specificity above 97.9%), and a negative result was usually correct in this population (NPV above 92.9%) — but sensitivity was low.'"

**Issue B. CCW sensitivity range omits one round** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "in Medicare Advantage the Chronic Conditions Warehouse algorithm caught just 30-35% of cases versus 47-49% in traditional fee-for-service"
- **Abstract says:** "The CCW algorithm demonstrated greater MA-FFS differences, with lower sensitivity in MA for Rounds 9 (30.8% vs. 46.9%) and 11 (35.0% vs. 49.3%) and similar sensitivity for Round 7 (40.4% vs. 42.2%)."
- **Problem:** The quoted figures come from two of the three rounds; in Round 7 sensitivity was essentially the same in MA and FFS, which the digest does not mention, making the MA gap look more consistent than reported.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add 'in two of the three rounds studied; in 2017 the two plan types performed similarly (40.4% vs 42.2%).'"

**Issue C. Medicare Advantage enrollment share** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "especially for the roughly half of beneficiaries now in Medicare Advantage"
- **Abstract says:** "The abstract notes only 'growing enrollment in Medicare Advantage (MA) plans' and gives no enrollment share."
- **Problem:** The 'roughly half' figure is external context and cannot be checked against the abstract.
- **Suggested fix:** "Attribute the enrollment figure to a separate source or soften to 'the growing share of beneficiaries in Medicare Advantage.' Unverifiable from abstract only."

### Study 10: Alzheimer's doesn't always start with memory loss — a new framework maps the variants that get misdiagnosed
**PMID:** 42760345 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Misdiagnosis and delay claims not in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "are frequently sent to eye doctors, psychiatrists or written off as stressed, delaying diagnosis by years at a time when new amyloid-targeting treatments depend on early identification"
- **Abstract says:** "This framework could reduce diagnostic mismatches, make cohorts more comparable and support trials that include outcomes tailored to the affected brain networks."
- **Problem:** The specific referral pathways, the multi-year delay and the link to amyloid-targeting therapy are plausible context but are not stated in the abstract.
- **Suggested fix:** "Flag as background or attribute separately; the abstract supports only 'diagnostic mismatches.' Unverifiable from abstract only."

**Issue B. Conflict-of-interest disclosures conflated** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "both authors report industry ties (grant funding from Eli Lilly, consulting for Biogen and UCB, speaker fees from Eisai)"
- **Abstract says:** "L.T.G. received an honorarium for educational material from Orator, consulting fees from UCB and an honorarium from Springer Nature for reviewing applications. M.E.M. received grant funding from Eli Lilly and Company, consulting fees from Biogen, and speaker fees from Eisai."
- **Problem:** The digest pools two authors' separate disclosures into one list and omits the Orator and Springer Nature honoraria, so readers cannot tell which author has which relationship.
- **Suggested fix:** "Separate the disclosures by author and include the omitted honoraria."

### Study 12: Just three mornings with a wrist monitor may flag who's headed for frailty
**PMID:** 42754817 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Equivalence to longer wear protocols not tested** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "The finding suggests a short, more tolerable wear period can carry the same predictive signal as the usual 5-7 day protocols older adults often abandon."
- **Abstract says:** "Whether accelerometry measures collected over shorter wear periods predict frailty decline is unknown... Higher activity levels and higher day-to-day morning activity variance measured over just two to three mornings differentiate those at higher risk of death and frailty decline, increasing the feasibility of using accelerometry as a digital frailty screening tool."
- **Problem:** The study measured only a 72-hour wear period; it made no head-to-head comparison with 5-7 day protocols, so "the same predictive signal" asserts an equivalence the data cannot support.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to: "The finding suggests a short, more tolerable wear period can still carry a useful predictive signal, though it was not compared directly with longer 5-7 day protocols.""

### Study 15: Three simple questions about chewing and swallowing tracked survival in 50,000 older adults
**PMID:** 42754543 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Effect size characterized as "substantial"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "appear to identify older adults at substantially higher risk of dying, pointing to a cheap screening opportunity in primary care and senior living"
- **Abstract says:** "Chewing difficulty (aHR 1.19 [95% CI 1.14-1.23]) and dysphagia (aHR 1.18 [95% CI 1.14-1.23]) were each independently associated with mortality... the aHRs for scores 1, 2, and 3 were 1.09, 1.26, and 1.49"
- **Problem:** Individual-symptom hazard ratios near 1.18-1.19 (and 1.49 for all three symptoms) are modest rather than substantial elevations in risk; the wording inflates the magnitude.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change to "appear to identify older adults at modestly but consistently higher risk of dying" and cite the 1.09/1.26/1.49 gradient."

### Study 17: A two-year program held functioning steady in older adults with and without intellectual disability
**PMID:** 42753686 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Causal attribution from uncontrolled study** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "A two-year program held functioning steady in older adults with and without intellectual disability"
- **Abstract says:** "Overall, findings suggest that the programme may contribute to maintaining or enhancing multiple domains of functioning in both groups over time."
- **Problem:** The headline and the "can support several domains" phrasing in Why it matters assert a program effect, while this was a single-arm prospective study with no control group (a limitation the digest does note in Caveats) and several domains actually declined.
- **Suggested fix:** "Soften to "functioning held broadly steady during a two-year program" and use "may support" rather than "can support" in Why it matters."

**Issue B. Setting detail not in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "conducted within a single Spanish disability organization's centers, limiting generalizability"
- **Abstract says:** "The abstract reports only that this was a prospective longitudinal study of 247 older adults with and without intellectual disability during implementation of a multicomponent, person-centred programme; author affiliations include ATADES residential and occupational centres."
- **Problem:** That all participants — including the 146 without intellectual disability — were recruited from one disability organization's centers cannot be confirmed from the abstract.
- **Suggested fix:** "Mark as inferred from author affiliations, or reword to "conducted in Spanish care and occupational centres" (Unverifiable from abstract only)."

- **Problem:** The abstract's conclusion is hedged ("may not"); the digest presents it as an established finding from 28 qualitative interviews.

### Study 21: Breathing exercises after brain surgery cut pneumonia and clot rates in older patients
**PMID:** 42754223 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Causal verb in headline for nonrandomized retrospective data** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Breathing exercises after brain surgery cut pneumonia and clot rates in older patients"
- **Abstract says:** "In this retrospective cohort study, MBRS was associated with lower rates of postoperative pulmonary infection and lower extremity DVT ... Prospective RCTs are needed to confirm these findings."
- **Problem:** 'Cut' asserts a causal effect, while the authors describe an association from a nonrandomized retrospective cohort. The body text and caveats do hedge appropriately, but the headline does not.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to something like 'Breathing exercises after brain surgery linked to lower pneumonia and clot rates in older patients.'"

**Issue B. Study location stated as China** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "across multiple centers in China"
- **Abstract says:** "A total of 258 patients aged ≥65 years undergoing surgery for CSDH were retrospectively enrolled from multiple centers. [Author affiliations: West China Hospital of Sichuan University-Ziyang Hospital & Ziyang Central Hospital, Ziyang, Sichuan, China; Department of Neurosurgery, Agostinho Neto Central Hospital, Praia, Cape Verde]"
- **Problem:** The abstract does not state where the participating centers were located; most authors are in China but one is in Cape Verde, so an exclusively Chinese multicenter setting is not verifiable from the abstract alone.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say 'across multiple centers (author institutions in China and Cape Verde)' or simply 'across multiple centers,' and note the location as unverifiable from the abstract."

### Study 22: Cancer patients who can't finish a meal rarely mention it — and no one asks
**PMID:** 42751765 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Applied to older adults without age data** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Early satiety drives weight loss, weakness and social isolation around mealtimes in older cancer patients, yet this study suggests it is essentially invisible in clinical encounters because neither side raises it."
- **Abstract says:** "Twenty-nine patients reported ES in the abPG-SGA and thus completed the SEESQ (ATG n = 16, ACG n = 13) ... Themes of 'distress', 'bloating', 'loss of enjoyment', and 'inability to eat' were highlighted in descriptions of ES."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports no participant ages and makes no claim that early satiety causes weight loss, weakness or social isolation; the digest both narrows the population to older adults and adds a causal mechanism not tested here. The caveat notes the age range was unspecified, but the body text still frames the finding as being about older patients.
- **Suggested fix:** "Rephrase as 'Early satiety is associated with poor nutritional status in cancer patients of all ages, and this small study suggests it is rarely raised in clinical encounters,' and drop the causal 'drives.'"

**Issue B. Unverifiable site count** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "two clinical sites"
- **Abstract says:** "A cross-sectional study was conducted in an Advanced Cancer Group (ACG) and an Active Treatment Group (ATG)."
- **Problem:** The abstract describes two patient groups, not two clinical sites; the number of recruiting sites is not stated. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change to 'two patient groups (active treatment and advanced cancer)' or remove the site count."
