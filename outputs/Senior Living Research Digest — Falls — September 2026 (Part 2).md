### 1. Telling your doctor about a fall could change your osteoporosis treatment

**Journal:** *JBMR Plus* | **Published:** 2026 Jul 28
**PMID:** 42689245 | **DOI:** 10.1093/jbmrpl/ziag120

**The study:** Researchers analyzed records of 28,708 people in the Swiss Osteoporosis Registry (88% women, average age 65.8) to see how the updated FRAXplus fracture risk calculator changes estimates compared with the standard FRAX tool. FRAXplus adds factors such as falls in the past year, recency and location of prior fractures, spine bone density and trabecular bone score. Most people (72%) came out with a higher 10-year fracture risk under FRAXplus (average increase of 4.8 percentage points), and 16% of major osteoporotic fracture estimates were reclassified upward — meaning treatment would now be recommended under Swiss guidelines.

**Why it matters:** A single fall in the past year, or a recent spine or hip fracture, can move an older adult from 'monitor' to 'treat' on fracture risk — but only if the doctor knows about it.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have fallen in the past year — even if you weren't injured and didn't go to the hospital — mention it at your next bone health appointment. Also make sure your record notes the date and location of any past fracture. These details had the biggest individual effect on risk estimates in this analysis, and can be the difference between being offered treatment or not.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For care staff and family members: fall incident logs are clinical data, not just paperwork. Bringing a documented fall history and fracture timeline to medical appointments gives clinicians the inputs newer risk calculators need, and may surface residents who are currently being under-treated for bone fragility.

**Caveats:** Registry-based modeling study, not a trial of outcomes — it shows how risk scores change, not that treating reclassified patients prevents fractures. Swiss population and Swiss treatment thresholds may not generalize elsewhere. Two FRAXplus inputs (hip axis length, diabetes duration) were unavailable. Numerous authors report honoraria or consultancy fees from pharmaceutical companies including Amgen, UCB Pharma and Eli Lilly; one author is a member of the FRAX design team and a director of Osteoporosis Research Ltd.

### 2. A broken wrist often means fragile bones — but hospitals rarely record it that way

**Journal:** *Bone* | **Published:** 2026 Nov
**PMID:** 42448012 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.bone.2026.118014

**The study:** Austrian researchers analyzed nationwide hospital insurance records for 1,705,684 patients aged 50-79 treated between 2003 and 2014, mapping which diagnoses appear alongside osteoporosis and which fractures get coded as osteoporosis-related. Hip fractures were strongly linked to an osteoporosis diagnosis, especially in women aged 60 and older. But forearm, wrist, hand and upper-arm fractures — classic early warning signs of fragile bones — showed no osteoporosis diagnoses among their significant co-diagnoses in any age group. Under-recognition was most pronounced in younger patients and in men.

**Why it matters:** An early wrist or arm fracture is a chance to diagnose and treat osteoporosis before a hip fracture happens — and this analysis suggests that chance is routinely missed, particularly for men.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you break a wrist, forearm, hand or upper arm after a minor fall, don't let it be treated as bad luck alone. Ask directly whether you should have a bone density scan and a fracture risk assessment — this data suggests these fractures often go unlinked to osteoporosis in the medical record, and men are especially likely to be overlooked.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and senior living staff: treat any low-impact fracture in a resident — not just a hip — as a trigger to request a bone health review and a falls assessment. Discharge paperwork from a wrist fracture may contain no mention of osteoporosis even when it is present.

**Caveats:** Observational analysis of administrative billing data, which reflects coding practice rather than confirmed clinical diagnosis and cannot establish causation. Data are from Austria and from 2003-2014, so practice may have changed and findings may not generalize to other health systems. Limited to ages 50-79, excluding the oldest and highest-risk adults.

### 3. Uneven ground trips up some older walkers far more than others — and brain scans hint at why

**Journal:** *Neurobiology of Aging* | **Published:** 2026 Dec
**PMID:** 42679689 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.neurobiolaging.2026.08.004

**The study:** Researchers had 86 older adults (average age 75) and 22 young adults walk on a treadmill across flat surfaces and three levels of uneven terrain, while also undergoing brain MRI scans of white matter. Older adults were split into higher- and lower-functioning groups based on a standard physical performance test. Lower-functioning older adults had more variable, less consistent steps even on flat ground, and their step variability climbed more sharply as the terrain got rougher. Their brain scans showed more extracellular free water — a marker of white matter deterioration — which was more strongly tied to unsteady walking in this group, particularly in a major band of fibers connecting the two hemispheres.

**Why it matters:** It suggests that difficulty on uneven surfaces isn't only about leg strength; changes in the brain's wiring contribute, which supports targeting both physical conditioning and challenging, varied-surface practice in fall prevention.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Notice whether gravel paths, grass, cracked sidewalks or thresholds feel harder for you than flat hallways. That gap is measurable and meaningful. Ask a physical therapist about a Short Physical Performance Battery test and about training that safely includes varied surfaces, rather than limiting all walking practice to smooth floors.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living operators and caregivers: grounds and walkways matter. This study quantifies how sharply step-to-step variability rises with terrain unevenness among lower-functioning residents — an argument for auditing outdoor paths, garden routes and transitions, and for supervising residents with lower physical function on those surfaces specifically.

**Caveats:** Observational and cross-sectional — it cannot establish that white matter changes cause unsteady walking. Young adult comparison group was small (n=22). Treadmill-simulated terrain may not fully reflect real-world outdoor walking. Single-center study; no fall outcomes were actually tracked.

### 4. In the Oldest Old, Slow Walking Predicts Falls — and for Men, Memory Problems Do Too

**Journal:** *Geroscience* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 14
**PMID:** 42734897 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11357-026-02516-0

**The study:** Researchers followed 1,099 participants in The 90+ Study (749 women, 350 men, average age 93.2) with evaluations every six months, testing whether slow gait (below the sex-specific median on a four-meter walk), cognitive impairment, or both predicted future falls. In women, slow gait alone raised fall rates by about a third, while cognitive impairment alone did not. In men, slow gait (70% higher rate), cognitive impairment (75% higher), and especially both together (more than double) all predicted more falls.

**Why it matters:** People in their 90s are the fastest-growing age group and fall often with serious consequences, yet a simple timed walk — and, for men, a cognitive screen — can flag who needs prevention efforts first.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or your spouse is in your late 80s or 90s, ask your clinician to time a short walk at your next visit and to note whether memory changes are part of the picture; these two simple checks together give a much clearer read on your fall risk than either alone.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living staff and family caregivers: fall-risk screening may need to be sex-aware. Cognitive impairment was a strong independent signal in men but not in women in this cohort, suggesting men with memory changes warrant particular attention even if they still walk reasonably well.

**Caveats:** Observational design cannot establish causation; participants come from a single long-running California cohort of largely white, well-educated nonagenarians and may not generalize; gait was defined relative to the sample median rather than an absolute clinical cutoff.

### 5. A Group Exercise and Coaching Program Cut Painkiller Use — and That Helped Prevent Falls

**Journal:** *J Am Geriatr Soc* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 17
**PMID:** 42755166 | **DOI:** 10.1111/jgs.70696

**The study:** This secondary and mediation analysis of the BOOST randomized controlled trial included 435 older people with neurogenic claudication (leg pain from spinal narrowing). At baseline, 63% were taking at least one pain medication and 34% were on a controlled medication such as an opioid. Compared with best-practice advice alone, a 12-week physical and psychological group intervention reduced the odds of using controlled pain medications by 38% at six months, and that reduction was a key mechanism explaining the program's lower fall rate at 12 months.

**Why it matters:** It offers a concrete, non-drug route to fewer falls: improving pain and walking through structured group exercise appears to reduce reliance on medications that themselves make falling more likely.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If back and leg pain from spinal stenosis has you leaning on opioids or other controlled pain medicines, ask your doctor or physical therapist about a structured group exercise and coaching program — in this trial, people who joined one needed less of that medication and fell less over the following year.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For care teams and senior living programming directors: this is evidence that fall prevention and deprescribing are the same project. Group-based physical and psychological programs may deliver a medication-reduction benefit that shows up months later as fewer falls.

**Caveats:** Secondary, post hoc mediation analysis of an existing trial rather than a prespecified test; the medication advantage narrowed by 12 months; medication use and falls were largely self-reported; conducted in UK settings in people with one specific spinal condition.

### 6. Taiwan Study of 245,000 Seniors: Which Combinations of Decline Send People to the ER After a Fall

**Journal:** *Exp Gerontol* | **Published:** 2026 Oct 1
**PMID:** 42575258 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.exger.2026.113274

**The study:** Researchers linked Taiwan's national ICOPE screening database to insurance claims and death records, following 244,899 adults aged 65 and older from 2022 to 2023. They assessed six domains of "intrinsic capacity" — cognition, mobility, vitality, vision, hearing, and psychological well-being. Mobility problems (58% higher rate), cognitive impairment (23%), and vision impairment (20%) each independently predicted fall-related emergency department visits, and risk climbed as more domains were impaired; the highest-risk profile combined physical and cognitive decline with sensory impairment (87% higher rate).

**Why it matters:** It shows that fall risk is not a single problem but a stacking of deficits, and that a brief multi-domain screening already used in primary care can identify who is heading for an ER visit.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Fall risk adds up: if you have more than one issue — say, slower walking plus fading eyesight or memory slips — your risk is meaningfully higher than any one problem alone. Ask for a checkup that covers mobility, vision, hearing, memory, nutrition and mood together, not one at a time.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For care professionals: the specific combination matters. Residents with physio-cognitive decline plus sensory impairment were the highest-risk group and may warrant prioritized vision checks, environmental adaptations and supervised mobility support.

**Caveats:** Observational cohort — cannot establish causation; only about two years of follow-up with a relatively low absolute event rate (6.95 fall-related ED visits per 1,000 person-years); funded by Taiwan's Health Promotion Administration, which also runs the screening program studied; findings reflect Taiwan's health system and may not generalize.

### 7. Frailty — Not Muscle Loss — Was the Stronger Warning Sign for Falls Over Six Years

**Journal:** *Arch Gerontol Geriatr* | **Published:** 2026 Nov
**PMID:** 42600484 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.archger.2026.106369

**The study:** Investigators followed 2,067 community-dwelling older adults in the Korean Frailty and Aging Cohort Study, reassessing them every two years for six years. Frailty alone predicted higher risk of falls (60% higher), difficulty with basic daily activities, instrumental activity disability, and severe mobility limitation. Sarcopenia alone — low muscle mass and strength by 2025 Asian Working Group criteria — was not significantly associated with any of these outcomes.

**Why it matters:** For deciding who needs fall prevention, a frailty assessment that captures exhaustion, slowness, weakness, low activity and weight loss appears more informative than muscle-mass measurement alone.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you've been told you're losing muscle, that's worth addressing — but the pattern that most predicted falls here was frailty: feeling exhausted, walking slowly, losing weight, and doing less. Ask your clinician for a frailty check, and be honest about fatigue and reduced activity.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For assessment protocols in senior living and primary care: prioritizing frailty screening may catch more of the residents headed toward falls and disability than body-composition testing alone, and it requires no special equipment.

**Caveats:** Observational cohort — cannot establish causation; the combined frailty-plus-sarcopenia group had smaller numbers, which may explain some non-significant findings; Korean community-dwelling sample may not generalize; falls were self-reported at biennial assessments.

### 8. A Simple Questionnaire Predicted Falls as Well as a Physical Test in Older Adults With MS

**Journal:** *Arch Phys Med Rehabil* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 22
**PMID:** 42772541 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.apmr.2026.09.014

**The study:** Researchers followed 109 ambulatory older adults with multiple sclerosis (average age 64.7, 70% women) for an average of 2.2 years, tracking falls through monthly phone interviews. They compared a self-report scale of mobility disability (the patient determined disease steps, or PDDS) with an in-person physical performance test (the short physical performance battery). Both predicted future falls, and statistical equivalence testing showed the questionnaire performed comparably to the objective test.

**Why it matters:** A short self-report questionnaire that patients can complete without a clinic visit or equipment could make fall-risk screening feasible in busy practices and by telehealth for older adults with MS.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're aging with multiple sclerosis, your own honest rating of how much your walking has changed carries real predictive weight — bring it up at appointments and ask whether it should trigger a fall-prevention referral, even if you're still walking independently.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For clinicians and rehab staff: a brief patient-reported mobility scale may be a practical substitute when timed physical testing isn't feasible, including in remote or home-based assessment of fall risk.

**Caveats:** Small sample (N=109), single-region cohort of ambulatory older adults with MS that may not generalize to other diagnoses or to those with greater disability; falls were self-reported; follow-up duration varied widely between participants; observational design.

### 9. Worry About Falling Plus Slow Walking: The Combination That Predicts Falls

**Journal:** *Clin Interv Aging* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 3
**PMID:** 42708074 | **DOI:** 10.2147/CIA.S620118

**The study:** Researchers followed 2,445 community-dwelling adults aged 60-79 in Zhejiang Province, China, for one year, tracking falls with daily calendars and monthly check-ins. Each participant was classified by a Timed Up and Go (TUG) walking test (fast under 12 seconds, slow at 12 seconds or more) and by a simple yes/no question about whether they were concerned about falling. Nearly 10% fell at least once. Both worry about falling (adjusted hazard ratio 1.60) and slow TUG performance (1.46) independently predicted falls, and people who had both had nearly double the hazard of those who were slow but unworried.

**Why it matters:** A one-question screen about fall worry adds real predictive information beyond a timed walking test, meaning a quick conversation can sharpen fall-risk assessment at no cost.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you find yourself quietly worrying about falling, that feeling is information worth sharing — this study suggests your own concern predicts falls independently of how fast you can get up and walk, so mention it at your next appointment rather than keeping it to yourself.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For care staff and family members: don't assume a normal mobility test means low risk, and don't dismiss a resident's stated fear as 'just anxiety.' Screening tools that pair a timed walk with a simple question about fall concerns capture more people who go on to fall.

**Caveats:** Observational cohort — cannot establish causation; falls were self-reported; fall concerns measured with a single yes/no item in the main analysis; population was Chinese adults aged 60-79 and may not generalize to other settings or to adults over 80.

### 10. A Short Safety Talk at the Right Moment Cut Falls Nearly in Half After Stroke

**Journal:** *Arch Phys Med Rehabil* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 12
**PMID:** 42731691 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.apmr.2026.09.006

**The study:** At a Japanese post-acute rehabilitation hospital, 1,437 stroke patients were compared before and after the introduction of a standardized fall-prevention education session delivered at the moment a patient was first cleared to walk independently. The session stressed that walking independence does not mean absolute safety, spelled out which activities were safe, and urged patients to ask for help with risky tasks. Compared with 816 patients receiving usual care, the 621 who got the standardized education had a 42% lower adjusted hazard of a first fall and a 32% lower overall fall rate.

**Why it matters:** The moment someone regains independent walking is a high-risk window, and a brief, structured conversation at exactly that point appears to meaningfully reduce falls during rehabilitation.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or someone you love has just been cleared to walk on your own after a stroke, that milestone is when fall risk spikes — ask your rehab team to walk you through exactly which activities are safe alone and which still warrant calling for help.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For rehab and senior living professionals: this is a low-cost, protocol-level change — standardize the safety briefing and time it to the independence milestone rather than to discharge, when patients may already have fallen.

**Caveats:** Retrospective pre-post design at a single hospital rather than a randomized trial; secular trends or other concurrent practice changes could contribute; participants were subacute stroke inpatients in Japan and findings may not generalize to community-dwelling older adults.

### 11. How Tired Activities Make You Feel May Predict Your Next Fall

**Journal:** *J Gerontol A Biol Sci Med Sci* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 5
**PMID:** 42605068 | **DOI:** 10.1093/gerona/glag199

**The study:** Researchers followed 223 community-dwelling adults aged 60 and older — 109 with multiple sclerosis and 114 healthy controls — tracking falls by structured monthly phone calls from 2019 to 2025. At baseline, participants completed the Pittsburgh Fatigability Scale, which asks how tired specific activities would make you feel rather than how tired you feel in general. Higher physical fatigability (odds ratio 1.54) and mental fatigability (1.41) each predicted greater odds of falling after adjusting for prior falls and other factors, and the link held whether or not a person had MS.

**Why it matters:** Fatigability is a simple questionnaire measure that may flag fall risk earlier than physical performance testing, and unlike age it is potentially modifiable through conditioning and activity pacing.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If ordinary activities like a short walk or a household chore leave you far more worn out than they used to, that is worth raising with your clinician as a possible fall-risk signal — not just a normal part of getting older.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For caregivers and senior living staff: notice when residents describe routine tasks as exhausting. Pacing activities, scheduling demanding tasks earlier in the day, and revisiting conditioning programs may be as relevant to fall prevention as grab bars and lighting.

**Caveats:** Observational cohort — cannot establish causation; modest sample size (N=223) from a single research setting enriched with MS participants; fatigability and falls were self-reported; mental fatigability's association weakened once MS status was accounted for.

### 12. Falls, Depression and Untreated Hearing Loss Track With Dementia — and the Burden Falls Unequally

**Journal:** *J Prev Alzheimers Dis* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 12
**PMID:** 42731172 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.tjpad.2026.100676

**The study:** Using 7,574 older adults from the nationally representative 2023 National Health and Aging Trends Study, researchers classified cognitive status as probable, possible, or no dementia and analyzed risk factors by race and ethnicity. Advanced age, low education, stroke, falls, and depression or anxiety were all associated with higher odds of probable dementia, while vision correction and hearing aid use were associated with lower odds. Disparities were stark: Hispanic adults aged 85 and older had a 60.2% predicted probability of probable dementia versus 35.1% for non-Hispanic White adults, and depression roughly doubled overall risk.

**Why it matters:** Falls and dementia risk travel together, and several of the associated factors — depression, uncorrected hearing and vision loss, chewing and swallowing problems — are treatable, though insurance coverage for hearing and dental care remains a barrier.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have had a fall, are struggling with low mood, or have been putting off getting hearing aids or new glasses, these are all things this study links to cognitive status — ask your doctor to address them together rather than one at a time, and ask what your coverage actually pays for.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and senior living professionals: fall events, untreated sensory loss, and depression may cluster as an early warning pattern. This study also underscores that standardized programs may serve Hispanic and Black older adults poorly — culturally responsive outreach and help navigating audiology and dental coverage gaps matter.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional observational analysis of a single survey year — cannot establish causation or direction (falls may follow cognitive decline rather than precede it); dementia status classified by survey algorithm, not clinical diagnosis; funding support reported from the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services.

### 13. Falls in Australian nursing homes held steady for eight years — even as sedative prescribing dropped sharply

**Journal:** *Australas J Ageing* | **Published:** 2026 Sep
**PMID:** 42786600 | **DOI:** 10.1111/ajag.70240

**The study:** Researchers analyzed electronic health records from 27 long-term care facilities in New South Wales covering 8,298 residents between July 2014 and June 2022, tracking quarterly rates of falls and four medication-related quality indicators. Antipsychotic use fell by an average of 2.14% per quarter and anti-anxiety/hypnotic use by 1.63%, with declines beginning around the time Australia's Royal Commission into aged care was established. Yet the share of residents with any fall (28.5%-33.3%) and injurious falls (14.5%-18.6%) stayed flat, and falls requiring hospitalization actually crept upward by about 1% per quarter.

**Why it matters:** Cutting back on sedating medications is a widely promoted fall-prevention strategy, but this eight-year record suggests deprescribing alone does not move the needle on falls — facilities need mobility, environment, and staffing strategies too.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or your spouse live in residential care, it's reasonable to ask what the facility is doing about falls beyond reviewing medications — things like strength and balance classes, footwear checks, lighting, and how quickly staff respond to call bells.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For operators and clinical leads: medication quality indicators are improving, which is genuine progress, but this data suggests they shouldn't be treated as a proxy for fall safety. Consider whether your fall-prevention program has anything in it other than a medication review.

**Caveats:** Observational, repeated cross-sectional design — cannot establish causation; limited to 27 facilities in one Australian state, so findings may not generalize to other systems; overlapping COVID-19 disruptions make trend attribution difficult.

### 14. Fear of falling before hip surgery predicted who survived it

**Journal:** *Eur Geriatr Med* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 22
**PMID:** 42771096 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s41999-026-01597-6

**The study:** Researchers followed 1,092 patients aged 65 and older who had hip fracture surgery at a Beijing hospital between November 2018 and November 2019, measuring fear of falling at admission with the modified Falls Efficacy Scale (mFES) and tracking deaths by phone at 30 days, 120 days, 1 year, and 3 years. After adjusting for other factors, patients with more confidence (higher mFES scores) had significantly lower odds of dying within 30 days (adjusted OR 0.780) and lower mortality risk over three years (adjusted HR 0.925). The link was strongest in the first month after surgery.

**Why it matters:** A simple confidence questionnaire, taken at admission and costing nothing, may flag which hip fracture patients are at highest risk of a poor outcome — information that could shape how aggressively rehabilitation and support are provided.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you've broken a hip, how confident you feel about moving without falling is worth telling your care team about honestly — it appears to be meaningful clinical information, not just a feeling, and it can open the door to more intensive rehab support.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For discharge planners and rehab staff: a low pre-surgery falls-confidence score may identify patients who need closer monitoring in the first 30 days, the window where the association was strongest. Note that fear of falling here likely reflects underlying frailty rather than causing death itself.

**Caveats:** Observational post-hoc analysis — cannot establish causation, and low confidence may simply be a marker of pre-existing frailty rather than a modifiable cause; single-center Chinese cohort, so may not generalize; mortality confirmed by telephone follow-up.

### 15. Chronic pain plus a slower-thinking brain tripled fall odds in older adults

**Journal:** *Ann Geriatr Med Res* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 3
**PMID:** 42687648 | **DOI:** 10.4235/agmr.26.0079

**The study:** Japanese researchers surveyed 320 community-dwelling older adults, classifying each by whether they had chronic pain (lasting or recurring more than 3 months) and whether they had slower mental processing speed on the Digit Symbol Substitution Test. Nearly 19% reported a fall in the past year. After adjusting for age, sex, BMI, mobility (Timed Up and Go), and fear of falling, people with both chronic pain and slower processing speed had roughly triple the odds of falling compared with those with neither (OR 3.20).

**Why it matters:** Neither chronic pain nor mild cognitive slowing is typically treated as an urgent fall risk on its own, but this suggests the combination deserves specific attention in fall risk screening.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you live with ongoing pain and have also noticed you're slower to process things — reading a menu, following directions, reacting in traffic — it's worth raising both with your doctor together, not as separate complaints. Ask whether your fall risk has been assessed recently.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For caregivers and community program staff: fall risk screening often focuses on gait and balance. This study suggests asking about persistent pain and watching for cognitive slowing may catch people who look mobile but are still at elevated risk.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional observational design — cannot establish causation or direction; falls were self-reported over the past year and subject to recall error; modest sample from a single Japanese community health survey, with a wide confidence interval (1.04-9.90) around the key finding; may not generalize to other populations.

### 16. Tai chi, Baduanjin and yoga improved balance in frail and pre-frail older adults, review finds

**Journal:** *Front Aging* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 8
**PMID:** 42775218 | **DOI:** 10.3389/fragi.2026.1915136

**The study:** Researchers pooled 10 randomized controlled trials (12 comparisons) testing mind-body exercises — mainly tai chi, Baduanjin, and yoga — against usual care, health education, or no intervention in pre-frail and frail older adults. Mind-body exercise significantly improved overall balance, dynamic balance, and static balance. Exploratory subgroup analysis suggested programs run 2-4 times a week, 31-60 minutes per session, for at least 13 weeks produced the most consistent results, and benefits appeared larger for those earlier in the frailty trajectory.

**Why it matters:** Balance is the front line of fall prevention, and these are low-intensity, low-cost activities that frail older adults can realistically do — including in a chair-modified form in many programs.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're starting to feel unsteady, a tai chi or gentle yoga class may be one of the more practical things you can do — and the evidence points toward going two to four times a week for at least three months rather than trying it a few times.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For activity directors and family caregivers: the dose signal here matters. A once-a-month demonstration class is unlikely to help; building a sustained twice-weekly, 30-60 minute program into the schedule is what the pooled data supports. Benefits looked stronger in pre-frail participants, arguing for enrolling residents early.

**Caveats:** Only 10 trials with a modest total participant pool; substantial heterogeneity across studies with wide confidence intervals; subgroup findings are exploratory and derived from indirect comparisons, not formal dose-response analysis; blinding is difficult in exercise trials, and several included studies come from Chinese databases with variable methodological reporting.

### 17. Remote dual-task exercise classes improved balance and cut fear of falling in Parkinson's patients

**Journal:** *J Aging Phys Act* | **Published:** 2026 Jul 16
**PMID:** 42468927 | **DOI:** 10.1123/japa.2026-0190

**The study:** Thirty people with idiopathic Parkinson's disease were randomly assigned to one of three groups for eight weeks, all delivered entirely by telerehabilitation: a structured exercise program alone (control), the program plus additional motor tasks, or the program plus additional cognitive tasks performed while moving. Both dual-task groups improved significantly in walking speed, functional mobility, balance, leg strength, cognition, and fear of falling, while the exercise-only control group showed no significant improvement on any measure. Quality of life was the one outcome that did not differ between groups.

**Why it matters:** It suggests that adding a second task — counting, naming words, or handling an object — while exercising may be what makes the difference, and that it can be delivered over video to people who struggle to travel to a clinic.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have Parkinson's and are already doing home exercises, ask your physical therapist about layering a second task on top — like naming animals while you walk — and whether a video-based dual-task program is available to you.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and care staff supporting someone with Parkinson's: telerehabilitation removed the travel barrier here entirely. If transport to therapy is the obstacle, it's worth asking providers whether a supervised remote program is an option.

**Caveats:** Very small sample (N=30, only 10 per group) from a single Turkish center; short 8-week intervention with no longer-term follow-up; participants and therapists could not be blinded; actual falls were not measured, only fear of falling and balance test scores.

### 18. A Five-Question Checklist Beats Grip Strength at Predicting Who Recovers After a Hip Fracture

**Journal:** *J Am Med Dir Assoc* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 4
**PMID:** 42696807 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jamda.2026.106450

**The study:** Finnish researchers followed 430 adults aged 70 and older for one year after hip fracture surgery at a single university hospital, comparing three ways of spotting sarcopenia (age-related muscle loss): handgrip strength, calf circumference, and the five-item SARC-F questionnaire, which asks about strength, walking, rising from a chair, climbing stairs, and falls. Only SARC-F consistently predicted outcomes: patients flagged by it were far less likely to get moving in the first days after surgery (odds ratio 7.56 by day 3) and had worse walking ability and steeper functional decline at four months. Neither group improved meaningfully between four and 12 months, and the physical measurements — grip and calf size — did not reliably predict anything.

**Why it matters:** A one-minute questionnaire that anyone can answer identifies, before or right after surgery, which hip fracture patients are heading for a poor recovery — and the four-month mark appears to be the point beyond which further gains are unlikely, making early rehabilitation intensity crucial.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you've had a fall or a hip fracture, ask your care team whether they've done a SARC-F screen — five simple questions about your strength, walking, chair rises, stair climbing, and fall history. A high score doesn't mean you won't recover, but it's a signal to push for a more intensive, earlier rehabilitation plan rather than waiting to see how things go.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For rehab staff and senior living clinical teams: this study argues for triaging hip fracture patients by SARC-F on admission rather than relying on dynamometers or tape measures, and for front-loading therapy resources into the first four months, since the data show recovery largely plateaus after that window.

**Caveats:** Single-center study in Finland; observational design cannot prove that acting on SARC-F scores changes outcomes; SARC-F relies on patient self-report; population may not generalize beyond a Nordic hospital system. One author has served on an Amgen advisory board.

### 19. Hearing, Vision and Mood — Not Just Knee Pain — Drive Falls in People With Knee Arthritis

**Journal:** *Arch Gerontol Geriatr* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 11
**PMID:** 42759165 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.archger.2026.106410

**The study:** Researchers analyzed 1,097 adults with knee osteoarthritis from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), testing 10 machine learning approaches against 28 demographic, health, and behavioral variables to predict a first fall. The best model reached an AUC of 0.836 and held up when tested in an independent hospital-based cohort. The most influential predictors were not joint-related at all: hearing and vision problems and depressive symptoms topped the list.

**Why it matters:** It suggests that fall prevention for people with arthritic knees should look well beyond the knee — routine sensory checks and depression screening may matter as much as strengthening exercises.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have knee arthritis, don't assume your fall risk is only about your joints. Getting your hearing and vision tested, and being honest with your doctor about low mood, may be among the most useful things you can do — these were the strongest warning signs in this analysis.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and senior living staff: a resident with knee osteoarthritis who has also skipped their eye exam, turned the TV up, or withdrawn socially may be a higher-priority fall risk than the mobility diagnosis alone would suggest. Sensory and mental health reviews belong in fall risk huddles.

**Caveats:** Observational data — the model predicts, it does not prove causation, and correcting hearing or vision has not been shown here to reduce falls. Prediction models developed in Chinese cohorts may not generalize to other populations or health systems; no evidence yet that using the model changes real-world fall rates.

### 20. AI Fall-Prediction Tools Are Arriving in Nursing Homes — But Nobody Has Shown They Prevent Falls Yet

**Journal:** *J Am Med Dir Assoc* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 4
**PMID:** 42696799 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jamda.2026.106433

**The study:** This scoping review screened more than 1,100 records and found just 11 studies of digital fall-prediction technologies — machine learning on electronic health records, Minimum Data Set analytics, wearable and passive sensors, predictive dashboards — used in nursing homes, assisted living, and other long-term care settings between 2016 and 2026. Prediction windows ranged from daily fall probability to six-month risk, and model performance varied from proof-of-concept to good discrimination. Critically, nearly all studies focused on building or validating models; almost none tested whether the technology actually reduced falls.

**Why it matters:** Sensor systems and AI risk dashboards are being marketed to long-term care operators, and this review makes clear the evidence base supports them as decision aids only — not as a substitute for hands-on nursing assessment or a proven fall-reduction intervention.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If a senior living community tells you it uses AI or sensors to prevent falls, it's fair to ask what happens when the system flags someone — who reviews the alert, and how quickly a person follows up. The technology's value lies entirely in the human response it triggers.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For operators and directors of nursing: when evaluating vendors, ask specifically for evidence of fall reduction in a real facility, not just model accuracy statistics. The review also flags unresolved issues around workflow integration, staff response, cost effectiveness, and ethical governance of resident monitoring.

**Caveats:** Scoping review of only 11 heterogeneous studies with no pooled analysis or formal quality grading; the underlying evidence is largely developmental, with limited data on real-world fall reduction or sustained outcomes; English-language studies only.

### 21. A Simple Prompt Doubled Bone-Health Referrals in a Parkinson's Clinic

**Journal:** *Australas J Ageing* | **Published:** 2026 Sep
**PMID:** 42723140 | **DOI:** 10.1111/ajag.70234

**The study:** People with Parkinson's disease face elevated risk of osteoporosis and fracture, yet bone health is routinely overlooked. Australian researchers audited 283 outpatient consultation letters from a Parkinson's and related disorders clinic before and after introducing a screening prompt called the FRAGILE tool. Referral for osteoporosis screening among at-risk patients rose from 23% to 48%, with the biggest gains for patients who had osteopenia, falls, or prior fractures — though risk factors like bone-thinning medications and comorbidities were still frequently missed.

**Why it matters:** Even after this improvement, more than half of at-risk Parkinson's patients still weren't referred for bone density assessment, showing how easily a fixable fracture risk falls through the cracks in specialty clinics focused on movement symptoms.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or someone close to you has Parkinson's disease, ask your neurologist directly whether your bone health has been assessed and whether you need a bone density scan — particularly if you've had a fall, a fracture, or take medications known to weaken bone. Don't assume a movement disorders clinic is tracking it.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For clinic managers and care coordinators: this is a low-cost demonstration that a structured prompt embedded in the consultation workflow can roughly double appropriate screening referrals — but also that prompts alone don't catch the less obvious risk factors like medication history.

**Caveats:** Single-center audit of clinic letters, not a controlled trial; no patient outcomes (scans completed, fractures prevented) were measured; the before-after design cannot rule out other changes in practice over the same period.

### 22. Dedicated Hospital Delirium Units Show Promise — With Less Restraint Use and Better Function

**Journal:** *Geriatrics (Basel)* | **Published:** 2026 Sep 4
**PMID:** 42776711 | **DOI:** 10.3390/geriatrics11050122

**The study:** Swiss and UK researchers conducted the first systematic review of specialized hospital delirium care environments — delirium rooms, delirium units, and psychogeriatric units — pooling nine studies covering 2,226 patients aged 65 and older. Most of these settings combined staff training, enhanced surveillance, environmental adaptation, and non-drug care. The review found the most consistent benefits in shorter delirium duration, better discharge outcomes, lower use of physical restraints, and improved functional recovery, while results for falls, length of stay, and mortality were inconsistent. All included studies carried a high or critical risk of bias.

**Why it matters:** Delirium is common, frightening, and has no proven drug treatment; this review suggests the physical and staffing environment of the ward itself may influence how quickly an older patient clears confusion and whether they end up restrained.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're facing a hospital admission and have risk factors for confusion — prior delirium, dementia, advanced age, or major surgery — it's reasonable to ask whether the hospital has a delirium-trained unit or protocol, and to request non-drug approaches like familiar objects, glasses, hearing aids, and consistent staff before sedating medication or restraints are considered.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and senior living professionals accompanying a resident to hospital: the ingredients that appear to help — trained staff, reduced restraint use, environmental adaptation — are things you can advocate for on any ward. Bringing hearing aids, glasses, and a familiar face are concrete contributions.

**Caveats:** Every included study was rated at high or critical risk of bias; interventions, comparators, and outcome definitions varied widely; exploratory pooled estimates were not statistically significant and the authors explicitly say they should not be read as proof of effectiveness. Findings for falls specifically were inconsistent.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**
- **Self-report keeps matching or beating equipment-based testing.** "A Simple Questionnaire Predicted Falls as Well as a Physical Test in Older Adults With MS" (PMID 42772541), "Fear of falling before hip surgery predicted who survived it" (PMID 42771096) and "A Five-Question Checklist Beats Grip Strength at Predicting Who Recovers After a Hip Fracture" (PMID 42696807) line up with the previous digest's "A Five-Question Survey Predicted Hip Fracture Recovery Better Than Grip Strength" (PMID 42696807), "Worried about falling? That fear predicts falls even when your walking test looks fine" (PMID 42708074) and "A Quick Reaction Test Spotted Past Fallers Better Than the Standard Walking Test" (PMID 42678611).
- **Fall risk is a stack of deficits, not a leg problem.** "Hearing, Vision and Mood — Not Just Knee Pain — Drive Falls in People With Knee Arthritis" (PMID 42759165) and "Falls, Depression and Untreated Hearing Loss Track With Dementia" (PMID 42731172) reinforce the multidomain message carried over in "Taiwan Study of 245,000 Seniors" (PMID 42575258) and echoed by the previous digest's "Older Canadians living at home fall more often than those in care facilities" (PMID 42497575), where people with no serious mobility impairment still fell.
- **Frailty and fatigue over body composition.** "Frailty — Not Muscle Loss — Was the Stronger Warning Sign for Falls Over Six Years" (PMID 42600484) and "How Tired Activities Make You Feel May Predict Your Next Fall" (PMID 42605068) carry forward unchanged from the previous digest (same PMIDs), now sitting alongside SARC-F (PMID 42696807) as a consistent case against equipment-based screening.
- **Bone health and falls are still run as separate services.** "Telling your doctor about a fall could change your osteoporosis treatment" (PMID 42689245), "A broken wrist often means fragile bones — but hospitals rarely record it that way" (PMID 42448012) and "A Simple Prompt Doubled Bone-Health Referrals in a Parkinson's Clinic" (PMID 42723140) all document the gap the previous digest's seven-organization position paper called out (PMID 42720850).

**Advances**
- FRAXplus (PMID 42689245) gives that position paper (PMID 42720850) a concrete mechanism: a fall in the past year is now an input that can move someone from "monitor" to "treat," with 16% of major fracture estimates reclassified upward.
- Medication reduction moves from association to mediation. The previous digest linked anticholinergic burden to falls (PMID 42542092); "A Group Exercise and Coaching Program Cut Painkiller Use — and That Helped Prevent Falls" (PMID 42755166) now shows a non-drug program lowering controlled-medication use, with that reduction explaining lower fall rates a year later.
- Hospital and rehab protocol changes gain a second data point. Following "A hospital told staff to get patients moving 'unless contraindicated'" (PMID 42689465), "A Short Safety Talk at the Right Moment Cut Falls Nearly in Half After Stroke" (PMID 42731691) shows that *when* a low-cost intervention lands — at the moment of walking independence — may matter as much as what it contains.
- Exercise dose gets firmer. The previous digest's perturbation-training dose analysis (PMID 42716502) is joined by "Tai chi, Baduanjin and yoga improved balance in frail and pre-frail older adults" (PMID 42775218), pointing to two to four sessions a week for at least 13 weeks, with larger gains earlier in the frailty trajectory.
- Screening gets sex-aware. "In the Oldest Old, Slow Walking Predicts Falls — and for Men, Memory Problems Do Too" (PMID 42734897) sharpens the cognition-plus-mobility thread from the previous digest's "Why Seniors With Memory Problems Fall May Differ From Why Others Do" (PMID 42666693).

**Counterpoints**
- **Deprescribing alone did not move falls.** "Falls in Australian nursing homes held steady for eight years — even as sedative prescribing dropped sharply" (PMID 42786600) directly complicates the medication-burden lever highlighted in the previous digest's anticholinergic analysis (PMID 42542092), and sits in tension with the mediation result in PMID 42755166 — cutting medication helped when it came packaged with exercise, not on its own.
- **The added task, not the technology, may be what works.** "Remote dual-task exercise classes improved balance and cut fear of falling in Parkinson's patients" (PMID 42468927) found the exercise-only control improved on nothing, which pushes back gently on the previous digest's "Virtual reality walking games improved gait in people with early memory loss — but not more than simpler training" (PMID 42475977) and "Wobble-Board Strength Training... Adding Mindfulness Did Not Help" (PMID 42677025).
- **Better wards, but not fewer falls.** "Dedicated Hospital Delirium Units Show Promise" (PMID 42776711) found restraint use and functional recovery improved while fall results stayed inconsistent — a caution against reading the previous digest's hospital mobility success (PMID 42689465) as a general rule about ward redesign.

**New themes**
- Confidence and fear as prognostic, not just predictive: low falls-confidence before hip surgery tracked with 30-day and three-year mortality (PMID 42771096).
- Equity in fall and dementia risk: Hispanic adults 85 and older had a 60.2% predicted probability of probable dementia versus 35.1% for non-Hispanic White adults, with untreated hearing and dental care as coverage-gap barriers (PMID 42731172).
- Disease-specific fall prevention as its own field this run — MS (PMIDs 42772541, 42605068), Parkinson's (PMIDs 42468927, 42723140), stroke rehabilitation (PMID 42731691), knee osteoarthritis (PMID 42759165).

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** The two levers long-term care leans on hardest showed the least to show for themselves this batch, while active programs did the work. Australian nursing homes cut antipsychotic and sedative prescribing steadily for eight years and their fall rates stayed flat, with hospitalized falls edging up (PMID 42786600), and a review of digital fall prediction found almost no evidence that AI or sensor tools reduce falls at all (PMID 42696799). What did move outcomes: a group exercise and coaching program that reduced controlled pain medication use, which in turn explained fewer falls a year later (PMID 42755166), a brief standardized safety talk timed to the moment stroke patients were cleared to walk (PMID 42731691), and sustained tai chi, Baduanjin or yoga classes run two to four times a week for at least three months (PMID 42775218).

**Why pitch this now:** Medication quality indicators and AI risk dashboards are both being sold and reported as fall prevention, and this batch suggests neither is one by itself — a distinction families touring communities and administrators signing vendor contracts are making right now with no clear evidence to hand.

**Angle:** A feature that separates fall *measurement* from fall *prevention*, built around what a real program contains — scheduled movement with a dose, a conversation delivered at the right moment, pain and medication handled together — written for families evaluating a community or home care agency, with a companion read for operators and directors of nursing.

**Potential headlines:**
- Cutting Sedatives Didn't Lower Falls — Exercise Classes and Well-Timed Conversations Did
- Nursing Homes Prescribed Fewer Sedatives for Eight Years. Residents Fell Just as Often.
- What to Ask a Care Community That Says It Prevents Falls

**Potential outlets:**
- **National Council on Aging** — their falls prevention programming is built on dose-based group exercise, and this batch gives them fresh evidence on frequency, duration, and why medication review alone isn't a program.
- **Caring.com** — families comparing communities need a plain checklist of what a credible fall program includes; this pitch supplies one rooted in new data rather than marketing claims.
- **McKnight's Long-Term Care News** — the eight-year Australian trend data and the AI scoping review speak directly to operators being asked to treat medication indicators and predictive dashboards as safety performance.
- **HomeCare Magazine** — the exercise-plus-pain-management mechanism and the telerehabilitation dual-task result both translate into home-based service design for agencies serving clients who can't travel to a clinic.
