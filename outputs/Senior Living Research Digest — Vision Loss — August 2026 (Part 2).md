### 1. Women bear a heavier burden of vision impairment worldwide — and the gap isn't closing

**Journal:** *British Journal of Ophthalmology* | **Published:** July 28, 2026
**PMID:** 42521465 | **DOI:** 10.1136/bjo-2025-328771

**The study:** Researchers analysed 33 years of global data (1990–2023) on blindness and vision loss, breaking results down by sex. Across 204 countries, women consistently had higher rates of vision-related disability than men — 14.5% higher overall — with the largest gaps in cataracts (+26.7%) and age-related macular degeneration (+18%). The disparity was greatest in South Asia and strongly linked to national-level gender inequality. Without targeted intervention, projections suggest these gaps will persist or widen through 2040.

**Why it matters:** Older women are disproportionately affected by preventable and treatable causes of vision loss. Policy changes and improved access to eye care could close this gap.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you are a woman over 50 — especially if you live in a region with limited healthcare access — regular eye exams are particularly important. Don't dismiss changes in your vision as "just getting older."
- **About older adults and seniors:** Families and senior living professionals should be aware that older women may face greater barriers to eye care. Advocacy for gender-sensitive eye health policies and proactive screening programs in senior communities could help address this inequity.

**Caveats:** Ecological study design (country-level data) cannot capture individual-level variation; relies on modelled estimates from the Global Burden of Disease study.

### 2. Having multiple age-related eye diseases dramatically increases risk of severe vision loss

**Journal:** *British Journal of Ophthalmology* | **Published:** July 22, 2026
**PMID:** 41249002 | **DOI:** 10.1136/bjo-2025-327643

**The study:** Using national health records from South Korea (N=500,000 adults, median follow-up ~15 years), researchers examined how glaucoma, macular degeneration, and diabetic eye disease interact. Each condition alone raised the risk of severe vision impairment — but having two conditions nearly doubled the risk compared to having one, and three conditions raised it even further.

**Why it matters:** Many older adults live with more than one eye condition. Integrated eye care that monitors and manages all conditions together may help prevent severe vision loss.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you've been diagnosed with one eye condition — such as glaucoma or diabetic eye disease — ask your eye doctor about your overall risk profile. Keeping all your conditions well managed, not just one, can help protect your sight.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For caregivers and senior living staff, this finding underscores the value of comprehensive annual eye exams for residents, especially those with diabetes or a family history of eye disease. Care plans should address multiple diagnoses together.

**Caveats:** Based on South Korean health system data; patterns may differ in other populations. Observational design — cannot prove causation.

### 3. Childhood adversity linked to poorer cognitive function in later life — untreated vision loss is one pathway

**Journal:** *The Gerontologist* | **Published:** August 5, 2026
**PMID:** 42371784 | **DOI:** 10.1093/geront/gnag147

**The study:** Researchers analysed data from over 20,000 adults aged 40+ in China, examining how adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) — such as neglect, parental divorce, or household violence — relate to cognitive function in later life. They found that certain childhood hardships were linked to poorer cognition decades later, and that part of this link ran through modifiable factors in adulthood, including untreated vision loss.

**Why it matters:** Vision problems aren't just about the eyes — they're connected to brain health. Addressing sensory deficits like uncorrected vision loss may be one way to support cognitive function in older adults.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you've lived through difficult childhood experiences, you're not destined for cognitive decline — but taking care of your overall health, including your vision, may help protect your brain as you age.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Healthcare providers and senior living professionals should consider ACE history as a marker of cognitive risk. Ensuring residents have access to vision screening and correction is a practical, modifiable step.

**Caveats:** Observational study — cannot establish that correcting vision loss prevents cognitive decline. Based on self-reported ACEs, which may be subject to recall bias.

### 4. Dementia risk factors vary widely by country — but often cluster together

**Journal:** *The Lancet Healthy Longevity* | **Published:** June 2026
**PMID:** 42437564 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.lanhl.2026.100867

**The study:** This cross-national analysis harmonised data from 14 countries (N=214,251 adults aged 50+) to compare the prevalence of 12 modifiable dementia risk factors — including vision loss, hearing loss, diabetes, and depression. More than half of individuals in every country had at least two risk factors. Although specific patterns varied by region, risk factors tended to cluster into cardiovascular, behavioural, and social/sensory groups.

**Why it matters:** Dementia prevention is not one-size-fits-all, but the consistent co-occurrence of risk factors suggests that multi-pronged interventions addressing sensory, cardiovascular, and social health together could be effective across settings.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Reducing your dementia risk may mean tackling more than one lifestyle factor at a time — for example, staying socially active while also managing blood pressure and keeping up with hearing and vision check-ups.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For senior living professionals, these findings support integrated wellness programs that address sensory health alongside chronic disease management and social engagement.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design (cannot track changes over time). Risk factor definitions were harmonised across studies but may not be perfectly comparable.

### 5. Older adults often don't recognise their own vision loss — life circumstances may overshadow eye problems

**Journal:** *BMC Geriatrics* | **Published:** June 10, 2026
**PMID:** 42271249 | **DOI:** 10.1186/s12877-026-07788-x

**The study:** Danish researchers interviewed 15 older adults (aged 75+) who were clinically identified with vision loss during a home-based screening program but had not previously sought care. Through home visits and narrative interviews, they found that many participants did not recognise their vision loss or prioritise it — often because other health concerns, caregiving responsibilities, or accumulated life hardships took precedence.

**Why it matters:** Clinical findings don't always match how older adults experience their own vision. Understanding these gaps can help healthcare providers reach those who might otherwise fall through the cracks.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** You may not notice gradual changes in your vision, especially if you're managing other health challenges. Regular eye exams — even when you feel fine — can catch problems early.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and care professionals, this study highlights the importance of proactive outreach and gentle inquiry about vision, especially for older adults juggling multiple health or life demands. Home-based screening programs may reach people who wouldn't otherwise seek care.

**Caveats:** Small qualitative sample (N=15); findings reflect the Danish context and may not generalise to other healthcare systems.

### 6. Audiobooks can restore reading pleasure after vision loss — but the transition takes effort

**Journal:** *Disability and Rehabilitation* | **Published:** July 25, 2026
**PMID:** 42500929 | **DOI:** 10.1080/09638288.2026.2704461

**The study:** Researchers conducted in-depth interviews with adults aged 60+ who had switched to audiobooks after losing vision. Participants described vision loss as a major life disruption requiring emotional and technological adjustment. Learning to listen immersively — navigating audiobook apps, managing attention, pacing — was a skill that took time to develop. Once mastered, audiobooks supported social connection, companionship, and continuity of reading identity.

**Why it matters:** Audiobooks are often recommended as a simple substitute for print, but this study shows the transition isn't automatic. Tailored support can help older adults with vision loss regain the joy of reading.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're struggling to enjoy audiobooks after vision loss, you're not alone — and it does get easier. Ask your library or vision rehabilitation service about training on audiobook apps and strategies for staying engaged.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Vision rehabilitation professionals and senior living staff should not assume that recommending audiobooks is enough. Providing hands-on technology training, follow-up support, and referrals to reading groups can make a meaningful difference.

**Caveats:** Qualitative study with a small sample; findings highlight barriers and facilitators but do not measure outcomes quantitatively.

### 7. Photobiomodulation shows promise for slowing early dry macular degeneration

**Journal:** *British Journal of Ophthalmology* | **Published:** June 8, 2026
**PMID:** 42259594 | **DOI:** 10.1136/bjo-2025-328389

**The study:** In a 12-month randomised controlled trial across multiple centres in Europe and the UK, 138 eyes with early or intermediate dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) were assigned to receive photobiomodulation (PBM) — a light-based therapy targeting retinal cells — or a sham treatment. The PBM group showed significant reductions in drusen (deposits under the retina) and a modest improvement in visual acuity, while the sham group experienced slight worsening. No cases of retinal phototoxicity were observed.

**Why it matters:** Dry AMD has no approved treatment to slow progression, so these results are encouraging. However, longer-term studies are needed to confirm whether PBM can meaningfully preserve vision.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have early or intermediate dry AMD, ask your eye doctor about emerging therapies like photobiomodulation — but be aware that it is not yet a standard treatment and more research is ongoing.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and clinicians, this trial adds to evidence that PBM may be a safe option worth discussing, though it should not replace routine AMD monitoring and lifestyle measures.

**Caveats:** 12-month follow-up; longer-term effects on disease progression and vision preservation are unknown. Some study authors reported industry honoraria.

### 8. Diabetic eye disease–related vision loss is rising globally — especially in middle-income countries

**Journal:** *British Journal of Ophthalmology* | **Published:** July 20, 2026
**PMID:** 42476825 | **DOI:** 10.1136/bjo-2025-328581

**The study:** Using Global Burden of Disease data from 1990 to 2021, researchers tracked trends in blindness and vision loss caused by diabetic retinopathy. Overall, the age-standardised prevalence rose nearly 40% — driven almost entirely by type 2 diabetes. The steepest increases occurred in middle-income countries. In contrast, vision loss related to type 1 diabetes showed modest declines.

**Why it matters:** As type 2 diabetes becomes more common worldwide, so does the eye disease it causes. Expanded screening and earlier intervention could help stem this tide.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have type 2 diabetes, regular dilated eye exams are essential — diabetic eye disease can develop silently before you notice any vision changes.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For healthcare systems and senior living communities, these trends underscore the need for integrated diabetes and eye care, particularly in regions experiencing rapid increases in diabetes prevalence.

**Caveats:** Relies on modelled global estimates; country-level data quality varies. Observational analysis — cannot prove that any single intervention would reverse trends.

### 9. Inherited retinal diseases affect about 1 in 1,600 people in Sweden — often going unspecified

**Journal:** *British Journal of Ophthalmology* | **Published:** July 10, 2026
**PMID:** 42431723 | **DOI:** 10.1136/bjo-2026-329787

**The study:** Researchers analysed Swedish national health registers from 2006 to 2021 to estimate the incidence and prevalence of inherited retinal diseases (IRDs) — genetic conditions that can cause progressive vision loss. They identified over 6,500 cases, yielding a prevalence of about 63 per 100,000 people. More than half of cases were classified as "unspecified IRD," highlighting gaps in diagnostic precision.

**Why it matters:** As gene therapies for certain IRDs become available, accurate diagnosis is increasingly important. This study provides baseline data for healthcare planning and underscores the need for improved genetic testing.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or a family member has been told you have an inherited eye condition without a specific diagnosis, ask about genetic testing — it may open doors to new treatments or clinical trials.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For families and clinicians, this study highlights the importance of precise diagnosis. Advocacy for expanded genetic testing and specialist referrals can help ensure patients are not left with "unspecified" labels.

**Caveats:** Register-based study; relies on diagnostic codes, which may undercount cases or misclassify subtypes. Limited to Sweden — prevalence may differ elsewhere.

### 10. Home-based hearing and vision support for people with dementia is feasible — but variable

**Journal:** *Disability and Rehabilitation* | **Published:** May 29, 2026
**PMID:** 42212629 | **DOI:** 10.1080/09638288.2026.2676067

**The study:** The SENSE-Cog trial enrolled 252 dyads (a person with dementia plus a care partner) across Europe. Participants randomised to the intervention received up to 18 weeks of home-based sensory support, including hearing aid fitting and vision assessments. Fidelity — completing all core components — was achieved in 60% of cases. Among those assessed for hearing loss, 81% were prescribed hearing aids with acceptable adherence. Care partners played a central role in supporting behaviour change.

**Why it matters:** Sensory impairments are common in dementia but often under-addressed. This trial shows that home-based support is feasible and can be tailored to individual needs, though implementation requires flexibility.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you or a loved one is living with dementia and experiencing hearing or vision difficulties, ask your healthcare team about sensory support options. Simple interventions like hearing aids can make a real difference.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For caregivers and senior living professionals, this study highlights the importance of involving care partners in sensory rehabilitation and tailoring interventions to cognitive ability.

**Caveats:** Fidelity was variable; COVID-19 restrictions required a streamlined protocol. This report focuses on delivery and uptake, not clinical outcomes.

### 11. Contrast sensitivity testing may detect vision changes in macular degeneration earlier than standard eye charts

**Journal:** *British Journal of Ophthalmology* | **Published:** July 1, 2026
**PMID:** 42386395 | **DOI:** 10.1136/bjo-2025-329091

**The study:** In a prospective study of 509 eyes (358 participants), researchers compared contrast sensitivity function (CSF) measures to standard visual acuity testing across stages of dry AMD. Half of eyes with intermediate AMD showed deficits in contrast sensitivity even when standard acuity was still normal. Structural biomarkers on imaging were more strongly correlated with CSF than with standard acuity.

**Why it matters:** Standard eye charts may miss early functional changes in AMD. Contrast sensitivity testing could help detect vision loss sooner and may be useful for monitoring disease progression or treatment response.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have early AMD and your standard vision test looks good, ask your eye doctor whether contrast sensitivity testing might provide additional information about your vision.
- **About older adults and seniors:** For clinicians and researchers, these findings support incorporating CSF measures into AMD monitoring and clinical trial endpoints.

**Caveats:** Single-centre study; findings need replication. Observational design — cannot determine whether early CSF deficits predict long-term outcomes.
