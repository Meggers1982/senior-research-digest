### 1. Eating More Flavonoid-Rich Foods Linked to Lower Risk of Hearing Loss

**Journal:** *Journal of Nutrition Health and Aging* | **Published:** July 2026
**PMID:** 42235109 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jnha.2026.100891

**The study:** Researchers followed 55,859 UK Biobank participants without baseline hearing loss for an average of 11 years, tracking their intake of flavonoid-rich foods (berries, citrus, tea, red wine, dark chocolate) through dietary recalls. Those in the highest quartile of flavonoid consumption had a 16% lower risk of developing hearing loss compared to the lowest quartile (HR 0.84). The protective effect appeared to work partly through reduced inflammation, with C-reactive protein mediating about 5% of the association.

**Why it matters:** This large-scale study suggests that commonly available, affordable foods may help preserve hearing as we age—offering a practical dietary strategy alongside other hearing protection measures.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Adding more berries, citrus fruits, and tea to your daily diet may help protect your hearing over time. These are simple, enjoyable additions that could complement other healthy habits you're already practicing.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Nutrition programming in senior living communities could emphasize flavonoid-rich options at meals. Families might consider these foods when preparing meals for aging relatives or stocking pantries.

**Caveats:** Observational design (cannot establish causation); dietary intake was self-reported; UK population may not generalize to all groups.

### 2. Hearing Loss Consistently Linked to Depression Across Three Countries

**Journal:** *Experimental Gerontology* | **Published:** July 2026
**PMID:** 42119796 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.exger.2026.113166

**The study:** Researchers analyzed longitudinal data from three major aging cohorts—CHARLS (China), HRS (United States), and ELSA (England)—to examine the relationship between self-reported hearing status and depression symptoms. Across all three countries, people with poor hearing had significantly higher odds of depressive symptoms compared to those with good hearing, with odds ratios ranging from 1.52 to 1.64 after adjusting for demographic, lifestyle, and health factors.

**Why it matters:** The consistency of this finding across diverse cultural contexts strengthens the case that hearing loss and depression are linked, regardless of where you live or your cultural background.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you've noticed your hearing isn't what it used to be and you're also feeling down, these experiences may be connected. Talking with your doctor about both concerns together may be worthwhile.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Mental health screening in senior care settings should consider hearing status. Family members noticing a loved one becoming more withdrawn might explore whether unaddressed hearing difficulties could be contributing.

**Caveats:** Observational design (cannot establish causation); hearing was self-reported rather than objectively measured.

### 3. Hearing Aids May Reduce Frailty Risk in Older Adults

**Journal:** *Journal of the American Geriatrics Society* | **Published:** July 2026
**PMID:** 42423142 | **DOI:** 10.1111/jgs.70581

**The study:** Using NHANES data from 2003-2018 (N=2,644 adults aged 65+), researchers found that worse hearing was associated with greater frailty, measured using a 52-item frailty index. Each 10 dB increase in hearing threshold (indicating poorer hearing) was linked to 24% higher odds of being frail. However, among people who used hearing aids, this association disappeared entirely—hearing aid users showed no increased frailty risk related to their hearing loss.

**Why it matters:** This study provides population-level evidence that hearing aids may do more than improve communication—they may help older adults maintain overall physical resilience.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have hearing loss and have been putting off getting hearing aids, this research suggests they may help you stay stronger and more independent overall, not just hear better.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living professionals should consider hearing aid use as part of comprehensive frailty prevention. Families might view the cost of hearing aids as an investment in maintaining a loved one's overall health and independence.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design (cannot establish causation); hearing aid use was self-reported; does not prove hearing aids directly prevent frailty.

### 4. Gamified Brain-and-Hearing Training Shows Promise for Older Adults

**Journal:** *JMIR Aging* | **Published:** June 2026
**PMID:** 42302249 | **DOI:** 10.2196/84083

**The study:** In this pilot randomized controlled trial, 60 community-dwelling older adults with mild-to-moderate hearing loss (mean age 68) were assigned to either a web-based gamified auditory-cognitive training program or a waitlist control. Over 12 weeks, the training group showed significant improvements in attention and some cognitive measures, along with slightly greater reduction in emotional hearing handicap. Participants found the program engaging and reported increased self-efficacy.

**Why it matters:** This culturally-adapted Chinese-language program demonstrates that enjoyable, home-based digital training can help older adults with hearing loss improve both listening skills and cognitive function.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Technology-based training programs designed specifically for people with hearing loss can be both fun and beneficial. If you're comfortable with computers or tablets, this type of program might be worth exploring.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living activity directors might consider incorporating validated auditory-cognitive training programs. Families could help set up and encourage participation in such digital interventions.

**Caveats:** Small sample size (N=60); single-center study in Hong Kong; waitlist control rather than active control; industry funding from Sonova AG (hearing device manufacturer).

### 5. Osteoporosis Treatment May Protect Against Hearing Loss

**Journal:** *Journal of the American Geriatrics Society* | **Published:** August 2026
**PMID:** 42574660 | **DOI:** 10.1111/jgs.70654

**The study:** Analyzing Taiwan's national health database (2011-2017), researchers compared 331,867 adults aged 65+ with osteoporosis or fragility fractures to matched controls without these conditions. People with osteoporosis had 34% higher risk of developing hearing loss. However, among those treated with zoledronic acid, hearing loss risk was 13% lower than untreated patients; denosumab showed similar benefits in men (9% lower risk).

**Why it matters:** This large population study suggests that certain osteoporosis medications may have an unexpected protective effect on hearing, potentially offering dual benefits for older adults already taking these treatments.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you're being treated for osteoporosis with zoledronic acid or denosumab, this research suggests these medications may offer additional benefits beyond bone health. This is another reason to stay consistent with treatments your doctor has prescribed.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Healthcare providers managing osteoporosis in older patients should be aware of the potential hearing protection benefits. Senior living health staff might monitor residents with untreated osteoporosis more closely for hearing changes.

**Caveats:** Observational design (cannot establish causation); retrospective database study; Taiwan population may not generalize to all groups.

### 6. Four Distinct Depression Trajectories Found in Older Adults with Hearing Loss

**Journal:** *BMC Geriatrics* | **Published:** June 2026
**PMID:** 42226155 | **DOI:** 10.1186/s12877-026-07687-1

**The study:** Using four waves of data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (N=1,556 older adults with hearing loss), researchers identified four distinct patterns of depressive symptoms over time: stable (no symptoms), increasing, decreasing, and severe-stable. Being female, having poor self-rated health, and low life satisfaction predicted membership in symptomatic groups. Longer sleep duration and better daily functioning were protective.

**Why it matters:** Depression doesn't follow the same course for everyone with hearing loss—identifying who is at highest risk allows for targeted intervention before symptoms become entrenched.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have hearing loss, paying attention to your sleep, staying active in daily activities, and maintaining social connections may help protect your mental health. Depression is not inevitable.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior care professionals should screen for depression in residents with hearing loss, particularly women and those with other health concerns. Addressing sleep quality and supporting daily functioning may help prevent depression trajectories from worsening.

**Caveats:** Single-country sample (China); self-reported measures; observational design.

### 7. Self-Reported Hearing Loss Tied to More Sedentary Time

**Journal:** *Journals of Gerontology: Medical Sciences* | **Published:** July 2026
**PMID:** 42363407 | **DOI:** 10.1093/gerona/glag168

**The study:** In a nationally representative sample of 3,512 Chilean adults aged 40+, researchers found that self-reported hearing loss was associated with 81% higher odds of sitting 8+ hours daily. However, hearing loss was not associated with overall physical activity levels or specific activity domains (work, transport, leisure).

**Why it matters:** People with hearing loss may not be less active overall, but they may spend more time sitting—a distinct health risk worth addressing separately from exercise habits.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Even if you stay physically active, hearing loss might be contributing to more sitting time during the day. Consider ways to break up extended sitting, regardless of your exercise routine.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living programming should address sedentary behavior specifically, not just offer exercise classes. Families might encourage standing activities or walking conversations with hearing-impaired relatives.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design (cannot establish causation); self-reported hearing and activity measures; Chilean population may not generalize.

### 8. Patterns of Loneliness and Social Isolation Differ by Race Among Adults with Hearing Loss

**Journal:** *Journal of Aging and Health* | **Published:** August 2026
**PMID:** 40645228 | **DOI:** 10.1177/08982643251360309

**The study:** Using 2022 Health and Retirement Study data (N=1,817 adults 50+ with objectively measured hearing loss), researchers found that Black adults with hearing loss reported lower social isolation than White adults, while Hispanic adults showed lower loneliness but higher social isolation. These patterns shifted when contextual factors were considered, revealing complex relationships between race, hearing loss, and social connection.

**Why it matters:** The experience of hearing loss and its social consequences varies across racial and ethnic groups, suggesting that one-size-fits-all interventions may miss important differences.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Your cultural background and community connections may shape how hearing loss affects your social life differently than for others. Support strategies should fit your particular circumstances and strengths.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living communities serving diverse populations should tailor social engagement programs to recognize different cultural patterns of connection. Hearing care providers should consider cultural context when discussing social impacts of hearing loss.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design; single time point; US population only.

### 9. Older Adults with Hearing Loss Constantly Self-Advocating in Healthcare Settings

**Journal:** *Geriatric Nursing* | **Published:** July 2026
**PMID:** 42167081 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.gerinurse.2026.104083

**The study:** In qualitative interviews with 15 older adults with hearing loss and chronic illness (plus their partners), a dominant theme emerged: the constant need to self-advocate for hearing accommodations in healthcare settings. Participants reported never being asked about their hearing needs but wanting to know important medical information. Small office settings, competing health concerns, and lack of accommodations compounded difficulties.

**Why it matters:** Healthcare systems place the burden of accommodation on patients with hearing loss, creating barriers to understanding critical health information—especially problematic for those managing chronic conditions.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** You shouldn't have to repeatedly remind healthcare providers about your hearing loss, but until systems change, consider bringing a family member to appointments, requesting written summaries, or asking providers to face you when speaking.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Healthcare facilities and senior living communities should proactively ask about hearing needs and provide accommodations without requiring patients to constantly advocate for themselves. Simple changes like quieter exam rooms and written instructions can make a significant difference.

**Caveats:** Small qualitative study (N=15); exploratory pilot design; US sample.

### 10. Biological Aging Markers Linked to Hearing Loss—and to Dementia, Heart Disease, Diabetes

**Journal:** *Experimental Gerontology* | **Published:** September 2026
**PMID:** 42419408 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.exger.2026.113232

**The study:** Researchers analyzed data from two cohorts (NHANES, N=2,530; HRS, N=2,849) using GrimAge, a DNA-based measure of biological aging. After adjusting for chronological age, accelerated biological aging was independently associated with hearing loss. Prospective analysis showed that hearing loss predicted later dementia (HR 2.94), cardiovascular disease (HR 1.43), and diabetes (HR 1.14), though chronological age explained 28-50% of these associations.

**Why it matters:** This research positions hearing loss not as an isolated sensory problem but as a potential marker of systemic aging processes—reinforcing the importance of addressing hearing loss as part of comprehensive health management.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Hearing loss may be connected to your overall biological aging, not just your ears. Taking care of your hearing is part of taking care of your whole-body health.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living health programs should integrate hearing assessments into comprehensive wellness monitoring. Family members noticing hearing decline might consider it a prompt for broader health evaluation.

**Caveats:** Observational design (cannot establish causation); mediation analysis shows chronological age explains substantial portions of associations; exploratory hypothesis-generating study.

### 11. Viewing Age-Related Losses Realistically May Prompt Hearing Help-Seeking

**Journal:** *Gerontology* | **Published:** June 2026
**PMID:** 42275258 | **DOI:** 10.1159/000552795

**The study:** Among 503 adults with hearing difficulties (mean age 64), those who acknowledged more age-related losses were more likely to have sought formal hearing help (OR 1.06) and to use hearing aids (OR 1.13). Interestingly, those with positive gain-focused views of aging were more likely to seek informal help (talking to friends/family about hearing) only among older participants.

**Why it matters:** Recognizing hearing challenges as real, age-related changes—rather than denying them—appears to motivate help-seeking, while positive aging attitudes may encourage informal support conversations.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Acknowledging that your hearing isn't what it used to be isn't defeatist—it may actually be the first step toward getting help. Talking openly with friends and family about hearing concerns is also valuable.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Health messaging should balance acknowledging hearing decline with optimistic framing about available solutions. Family members can help normalize conversations about hearing changes without stigma.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design; mostly mild hearing difficulties (80.5%); self-reported outcomes; 69.4% female sample.

### 12. Hearing Loss Identified as Key Modifiable Dementia Risk Factor Across 14 Countries

**Journal:** *Lancet Healthy Longevity* | **Published:** June 2026
**PMID:** 42437564 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.lanhl.2026.100867

**The study:** Researchers analyzed harmonized data from 214,251 adults aged 50+ across 14 countries, examining 12 established dementia risk factors. Hearing loss emerged as one of several prevalent modifiable risk factors, with similar clustering patterns observed globally. More than 50% of participants had at least two risk factors, and factors clustered into cardiovascular, behavioral, and social/sensory groups across diverse settings.

**Why it matters:** This massive cross-national analysis confirms that hearing loss is a globally relevant dementia risk factor that tends to cluster with other modifiable risks—suggesting coordinated prevention opportunities.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Hearing loss is one of several factors you can address to potentially reduce dementia risk. If you have hearing concerns plus other risks like high blood pressure or social isolation, addressing them together may be beneficial.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Dementia prevention programs in senior living should screen for clusters of risk factors including hearing loss. Healthcare systems should consider integrated approaches addressing multiple modifiable risks simultaneously.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design; some variation in how hearing loss was measured across countries; prevalence data rather than causation.

### 13. Public Awareness of Hearing Loss as Dementia Risk Factor Remains Low

**Journal:** *Journal of Prevention of Alzheimer's Disease* | **Published:** June 2026
**PMID:** 41966599 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.tjpad.2026.100565

**The study:** This systematic review and meta-analysis of 155 studies (164,644 participants across 41 countries) found that public recognition of dementia risk factors was moderate overall, with physical activity (71.5%) and social isolation (66.6%) best recognized. However, qualitative studies revealed limited knowledge specifically about dementia-specific risk factors like hearing loss—suggesting a particular gap in awareness.

**Why it matters:** Despite scientific consensus that hearing loss is a major modifiable dementia risk factor, the general public largely doesn't know this—representing a critical education gap.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** You may know that exercise helps protect your brain, but did you know that hearing loss is also considered a significant risk factor for dementia? Addressing hearing problems may be an important part of brain health.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Public health campaigns should specifically highlight the hearing-dementia connection, which remains underrecognized. Senior living wellness programs have an opportunity to educate residents about this less-known risk factor.

**Caveats:** Systematic review of existing studies with varying quality; recognition rates varied widely across studies; recall (unprompted) was much lower than recognition (when prompted).

### 14. Hearing Loss Linked to Elder Financial and Emotional Mistreatment

**Journal:** *Journal of the American Geriatrics Society* | **Published:** June 2026
**PMID:** 41981819 | **DOI:** 10.1111/jgs.70454

**The study:** Using nationally representative data from 5,231 older adults (National Social Life, Health, and Aging Project), researchers found that multisensory impairment (affecting multiple senses) was associated with higher rates of emotional or financial mistreatment (28% vs. 20% for those with no impairments). Self-reported vision and hearing impairment that interfered with communication were specifically linked to financial mistreatment.

**Why it matters:** Sensory impairments may increase vulnerability to exploitation and abuse, highlighting the need to consider hearing and vision in elder protection efforts.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If hearing or vision loss makes it harder to communicate clearly, be aware that this could potentially increase your vulnerability to scams or manipulation. Consider involving trusted family members in financial decisions if communication is challenging.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Adult protective services and senior care providers should screen for sensory impairments when assessing mistreatment risk. Addressing hearing and vision problems may be an important elder abuse prevention strategy.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design (cannot establish causation); mistreatment was self-reported; different assessment methods across data collection rounds.

### 15. Systematic Review Supports Hearing Aid Benefits for Cognition and Auditory Processing

**Journal:** *Archives of Gerontology and Geriatrics* | **Published:** July 2026
**PMID:** 42447643 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.archger.2026.106357

**The study:** This systematic review analyzed 61 research papers (2005-2025) examining the effects of hearing aid use on cognitive function and auditory processing. The collective evidence showed benefits of hearing aid use for both neurocognitive functions and central auditory processing abilities.

**Why it matters:** While individual study results vary, this comprehensive review of the literature supports the value of early hearing aid intervention for preserving cognitive and auditory abilities.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you've been hesitant about hearing aids, this review of decades of research suggests they may help maintain both your hearing abilities and cognitive sharpness over time.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Senior living health staff should encourage early hearing aid adoption rather than waiting until hearing loss becomes severe. The evidence base supports rehabilitation benefits beyond just better hearing.

**Caveats:** Systematic review; heterogeneous outcomes across included studies; study quality varied.

### 16. Machine Learning Model Can Prioritize Who Needs Hearing Screening

**Journal:** *Journal of the American Geriatrics Society* | **Published:** July 2026
**PMID:** 42535298 | **DOI:** 10.1111/jgs.70634

**The study:** Brazilian researchers developed a decision tree algorithm using data from 60 older adults to identify who should be prioritized for hearing examinations in primary care settings where audiology resources are limited. Key predictors included tinnitus, not having a partner, female sex, hypertension/diabetes, and age 65+. The model achieved good accuracy (AUC 0.825) and outperformed traditional statistical approaches.

**Why it matters:** Where hearing specialists are scarce, this tool could help primary care providers identify which patients most urgently need hearing assessment—potentially improving detection in underserved communities.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** If you have tinnitus, high blood pressure, or diabetes, you may be at higher risk for hearing loss and could benefit from asking your doctor about a hearing check, even if screening isn't routinely offered.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Primary care practices and senior living communities in areas with limited audiology access might use risk factor profiles to prioritize hearing referrals. This approach could help allocate scarce resources more effectively.

**Caveats:** Small sample size (N=60); single-center study in Brazil; requires larger-scale validation.

### 17. Different Hearing Devices Show Similar Cognitive Performance in Users

**Journal:** *Frontiers in Aging Neuroscience* | **Published:** August 2026
**PMID:** 42621042 | **DOI:** 10.3389/fnagi.2026.1887207

**The study:** Researchers compared cognitive performance across 617 adults aged 40+ using hearing aids (N=387), cochlear implants (N=127), or bimodal devices (N=103). Overall, no significant differences in cognition were found between device types. Age-stratified analysis revealed some domain-specific differences: cochlear implant users under 70 had faster reaction times, while those 70+ showed slower visual attention compared to hearing aid users.

**Why it matters:** This reassures patients and clinicians that different hearing device options appear to support similar cognitive outcomes, with device choice appropriately driven by hearing needs rather than cognitive concerns.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Whether you use hearing aids, cochlear implants, or both, your cognitive abilities appear similarly supported. Focus on which device best addresses your hearing needs rather than worrying about cognitive differences.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Clinicians counseling patients on device options can reassure them that cognitive outcomes appear comparable across device types. The choice should be based on audiological factors and patient preference.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design; industry co-funding (Cochlear Ltd.); cannot determine whether devices caused similar cognition or whether similar patients chose similar devices.

### 18. Brain Network Changes Detected in Age-Related Hearing Loss Using EEG

**Journal:** *Frontiers in Aging Neuroscience* | **Published:** August 2026
**PMID:** 42609411 | **DOI:** 10.3389/fnagi.2026.1850657

**The study:** Researchers used resting-state EEG to compare brain activity patterns in 45 older adults with age-related hearing loss and 50 normal-hearing controls. Those with hearing loss showed altered patterns of brain network activity—specifically, changes in how sensorimotor and higher-order cognitive networks communicate. These changes correlated with both hearing thresholds and speech-in-noise perception.

**Why it matters:** This research reveals that hearing loss is associated with broader changes in how the brain organizes activity, not just the auditory system—providing neurophysiological evidence for the cognitive impacts of hearing decline.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Hearing loss affects more than just your ears—it appears to change how different parts of your brain communicate with each other. This underscores the importance of addressing hearing concerns as part of brain health.
- **About older adults and seniors:** These findings strengthen the scientific basis for including hearing assessment in cognitive health programs. Senior living communities might frame hearing care as part of comprehensive brain wellness.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design; moderate sample size (N=95); technical EEG methodology may not translate directly to clinical applications.

### 19. Individual Hearing Decline Patterns Predict Brain and Cognitive Changes

**Journal:** *Frontiers in Aging* | **Published:** July 2026
**PMID:** 42591333 | **DOI:** 10.3389/fragi.2026.1867663

**The study:** Following 51 healthy men from a birth cohort at ages 63 and 70-71, researchers found that the combination of pure-tone hearing thresholds and speech-in-noise testing together best predicted which cognitive trajectory groups participants belonged to. Machine learning analysis identified temporal lobe volume as key to auditory contrast detection, while cerebellum and fourth ventricle volumes were linked to speech-in-noise performance.

**Why it matters:** This longitudinal study in healthy aging men shows that individual patterns of hearing decline track with specific brain regions and cognitive changes, supporting personalized monitoring approaches.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** How quickly your hearing changes over time may tell doctors something about your brain health too. Tracking hearing changes regularly—not just once—may provide valuable health information.
- **About older adults and seniors:** Longitudinal hearing monitoring may offer insights into cognitive aging trajectories. Senior living health programs might benefit from serial hearing assessments rather than one-time screenings.

**Caveats:** Small sample (N=51); all-male cohort; industry involvement (Oticon); observational design.

### 20. Brain Structure Changes Identified in Presbycusis Patients

**Journal:** *Frontiers in Aging Neuroscience* | **Published:** July 2026
**PMID:** 42568626 | **DOI:** 10.3389/fnagi.2026.1889317

**The study:** Using MRI brain scans from 129 presbycusis patients and 121 age-matched healthy controls, researchers found that hearing loss was associated with reduced gray matter volume and reorganization of brain structural networks. Changes were observed in the insula, hippocampus, frontal gyrus, and visual areas. Cognitive assessments revealed impairments in executive function, memory, and visual-spatial abilities.

**Why it matters:** This large-sample MRI study provides detailed mapping of how age-related hearing loss relates to structural brain changes across multiple regions beyond just auditory areas.

**Story angles:**
- **To older adults and seniors:** Age-related hearing loss appears connected to changes in multiple brain areas, not just the parts that process sound. This is another reason to take hearing concerns seriously as part of overall brain health.
- **About older adults and seniors:** The findings suggest that presbycusis should be understood as a condition with broader neurological implications. Cognitive screening may be particularly important for residents with significant hearing loss.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional design; single-center study in China; cannot determine whether brain changes caused hearing loss or vice versa.

**Advances:**

- Public awareness gap: A new systematic review (PMID 41966599) documents that public recognition of hearing loss as a dementia risk factor remains low despite scientific consensus—a critical education gap not covered previously.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Several studies in this batch reveal a widening gap between scientific evidence and public action on hearing loss as a dementia risk factor. The 14-country Lancet Healthy Longevity analysis (PMID 42437564) confirms hearing loss is a globally prevalent, clustered, modifiable dementia risk factor. Yet a 155-study meta-analysis (PMID 41966599) shows the public largely doesn't know it. Meanwhile, neuroimaging studies (PMIDs 42609411, 42568626) demonstrate that hearing loss is associated with measurable brain network reorganization, and a systematic review (PMID 42447643) supports that hearing aids can help cognition—but uptake remains low.

**Why pitch this now:** The Lancet Dementia Commission's 2024 update elevated hearing loss to the top modifiable risk factor, yet two years later this awareness gap persists. With Medicare hearing-aid coverage debates ongoing and OTC hearing aids now available, the disconnect between evidence and action is increasingly newsworthy.

**Angle:** "The Dementia Risk You Can Actually Do Something About—If Only You Knew" — a feature exploring why hearing loss remains the most underrecognized modifiable dementia risk factor despite the strongest evidence base, what the brain imaging shows about why it matters, and what policy and public-health messaging could close the gap. Primary audience: older adults and family caregivers; secondary: policymakers and senior living professionals.
