### Study 2: An "Antioxidant Lifestyle" Score Tracked With a Younger Biological Age in 20,000 Americans
**PMID:** 42912690 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. OBS components not described in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "scoring each person's diet and lifestyle on an Oxidative Balance Score — a composite that rewards antioxidant-rich foods and nutrients and penalizes pro-oxidant factors like smoking and obesity"
- **Abstract says:** "The abstract names the Oxidative Balance Score (OBS) but never lists its components or how it is scored."
- **Problem:** The specific components (smoking, obesity, antioxidant nutrients) are standard background knowledge but are not verifiable from this abstract, and the same applies to the story-angle line about "not smoking and staying active."
- **Suggested fix:** "Attribute the component description to the general OBS literature (e.g., "the score, as generally constructed, combines...") or drop the specifics."

### Study 3: Feeding Tube Decisions in Advanced Head and Neck Cancer: Clinicians Describe a Narrow "Window of Opportunity"
**PMID:** 42922280 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Direction of family-patient mismatch not in abstract** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Clinicians in this study repeatedly described families and patients wanting different things, with families often pushing for more aggressive feeding."
- **Abstract says:** "Theme 3 is simply "Differing expectations and experiences of patients and family"; the abstract does not say which way the mismatch ran or that families pushed for more aggressive feeding."
- **Problem:** A specific, directional claim is attributed to the study's participants when the abstract reports only that expectations differed.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say the study reported differing expectations between patients and families, without specifying who wanted more feeding, or mark the detail as unverifiable from the abstract."

**Issue B. End-of-life intake claim not from this study** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Caregivers may find it useful to know that declining intake near the end of life is expected"
- **Abstract says:** "The abstract notes nutrition "is likely to deteriorate as the disease progresses" but makes no statement about expected intake decline at end of life as a reassurance to caregivers."
- **Problem:** General palliative-care knowledge is presented in a way that reads as a finding of this interview study.
- **Suggested fix:** "Attribute the point to general palliative care practice rather than to this study."

### Study 9: Older Americans Are Taking More Pills Than Ever — and the Mix Depends on How Frail You Are
**PMID:** 42870717 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Medication-data collection method not in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Medication data rely on household inventories at a single point in time"
- **Abstract says:** "The abstract states only that outcomes included the mean number of prescription medications overall and by therapeutic class, with no description of how medication data were captured."
- **Problem:** The inventory-based collection method cannot be confirmed from the abstract.
- **Suggested fix:** "Drop the detail or label it as a general feature of NHANES medication data rather than a stated method of this paper."

**Issue B. Omits contrasting hyperpolypharmacy composition** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "moderate-to-severe frailty is where psychoactive drugs — the class most tied to falls, confusion, and reduced appetite — accumulate fastest"
- **Abstract says:** "CNS/psychotherapeutic agents increased primarily among adults with moderate to severe frailty, but in 2017-2020 hyperpolypharmacy among robust older adults "predominantly used CNS/psychotherapeutic and topical agents, whereas frail adults primarily used cardiovascular and metabolic agents.""
- **Problem:** The rate-of-increase claim is correct, but readers directed to look for psychoactive drugs only in the frailest may miss that heavy CNS use also characterized robust adults on ≥10 medications.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add a clause noting that among robust adults taking 10+ medications, CNS/psychotherapeutic agents also predominated."

### Study 12: Muscle Weakness, Not Just Muscle Size, Predicts Who Struggles With Cancer Treatment After 70
**PMID:** 42782098 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Chair-stand test framed as standalone predictor** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "It suggests a simple, free bedside test — how long it takes to stand up from a chair five times — may flag which older cancer patients are at risk of harsh treatment side effects better than an expensive scan"
- **Abstract says:** "Sarcopenia was defined by reduced strength (chair stand > 16.7 s for five rises) and either a reduction in muscle quantity ... or muscle quality ... SMI alone was not significantly associated with severe nonhematologic toxicity or survival ... Our findings highlight the importance of assessing muscle strength, quantity, quality and performance."
- **Problem:** The predictive signal came from a composite sarcopenia definition that required both the chair-stand result and CT-based muscle quantity/quality. The study never tested the chair stand on its own, so it cannot support the claim that a bedside test alone beats a scan.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to: "It suggests that adding a simple strength test to CT-based muscle measures flags risk that the scan alone misses.""

**Issue B. Survival comparison restricted to first-line therapy** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "worse one-year survival (46% vs 60%)"
- **Abstract says:** "In participants receiving first-line therapy, sarcopenia was associated with worse 1-year survival (46% survival ...) compared to those without sarcopenia (60% survival ...)"
- **Problem:** The survival difference was observed only in the subgroup receiving first-line therapy, not the full sample of 161.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add "among those receiving first-line therapy" to the survival figures."

### Study 13: A Metabolic Score Meant to Beat BMI Mostly Didn't — and After 65, Heavier Still Tracked With Lower Death Risk
**PMID:** 42753933 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. TyG-BMI treated as a measure of body weight** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "in this analysis it was the lowest — not the highest — body mass that accompanied higher death rates in older adults"
- **Abstract says:** "high TyG-BMI associated with lower mortality among adults aged ≥65 years (HR = 0.59, 95% CI: 0.48-0.73) ... Additional analyses showed that BMI alone also exhibited significant non-linear mortality associations"
- **Problem:** The age-stratified finding concerns the TyG-BMI composite (triglycerides × glucose × BMI), not body mass itself; the abstract reports no age-stratified analysis of BMI alone. The headline's "heavier" makes the same substitution.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say "the lowest — not the highest — TyG-BMI scores accompanied higher death rates in adults 65 and older" and adjust the headline accordingly."

### Study 14: Older adults with higher vitamin D levels sleep longer and wake less, study finds
**PMID:** 42696426 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline narrows a middle-aged and older sample** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Older adults with higher vitamin D levels sleep longer and wake less, study finds"
- **Abstract says:** "We studied 460 participants aged 40 and older (70.9 ± 12.0 [SD] years ...) ... Higher vitamin D levels are associated with longer and less disturbed sleep in middle-aged and older adults."
- **Problem:** The cohort spans middle-aged and older adults, and the authors frame their conclusion that way; the headline attributes the finding to older adults alone.
- **Suggested fix:** "Use "middle-aged and older adults" in the headline, or note the age span explicitly."

### Study 15: For muscle quality after 60, lifting beats supplements — and the combination didn't add much
**PMID:** 42570706 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. I² attributed to all pooled trials** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Very high statistical heterogeneity (I²=83.5%) across pooled trials"
- **Abstract says:** "Resistance exercise significantly improved muscle-specific strength (SMD = 0.65, 95% confidence interval: 0.22-1.09), although heterogeneity was observed (I² = 83.5%). ... High heterogeneity was observed across all included studies."
- **Problem:** The 83.5% figure belongs to the resistance-exercise analysis specifically, not to the review as a whole.
- **Suggested fix:** "Write "heterogeneity in the resistance-exercise analysis was high (I²=83.5%), and heterogeneity was high across the review generally.""

**Issue B. "Beats" implies direct comparison** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "For muscle quality after 60, lifting beats supplements — and the combination didn't add much"
- **Abstract says:** "Resistance exercise significantly improved muscle-specific strength ... In contrast, interventions such as aerobic exercise, concurrent training, combined exercise and nutrition, and nutritional supplementation did not lead to significant improvements."
- **Problem:** Each intervention type was pooled against its own controls; the meta-analysis did not compare resistance training head-to-head with supplementation, so "beats" overstates the design.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reframe as "resistance training showed a benefit where supplements did not.""

**Issue C. Unsupported dosing advice** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "put your money and time into supervised resistance training at least twice a week before you put it into supplements"
- **Abstract says:** "Eligible studies included adults aged 60 years or older, with interventions lasting at least 8 weeks."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports no training frequency, so the "at least twice a week" prescription is not supported by the source as given.
- **Suggested fix:** "Drop the frequency or attribute it to general guidelines rather than this review; note it is unverifiable from the abstract alone."

### Study 16: More than half of LGBTQ+ adults in one state went hungry — and shame kept many from food pantries
**PMID:** 42848400 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Food insecurity equated with hunger** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "More than half of LGBTQ+ adults in one state went hungry"
- **Abstract says:** "Food insecurity, measured using the 10-item US Department of Agriculture Food Security Survey Module ... over half of LGBTQ+ adults experienced food insecurity in 2024"
- **Problem:** Food insecurity as measured by the USDA module covers uncertain or limited access to adequate food and is broader than going hungry.
- **Suggested fix:** "Use "experienced food insecurity" in the headline."

**Issue B. Added detail not in the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "grocery gift cards, home delivery, and online ordering with discreet pickup"
- **Abstract says:** "LGBTQ+ adults frequently endorsed grocery gift cards (42.5% ...), home delivery (42.4% ...), and online ordering (34.0% ...) as strategies to increase pantry use."
- **Problem:** "Discreet pickup" is an embellishment; the abstract lists online ordering only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Delete "with discreet pickup.""

**Issue C. Claim about LGBTQ+ older adults' living situations** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "LGBTQ+ older adults — who are more likely to live alone and without adult children — face an added layer of exposure fear"
- **Abstract says:** "Results are reported by LGBTQ+ status with no age-stratified findings; the abstract contains no data on living arrangements or children."
- **Problem:** Unverifiable from abstract only; the statement is presented as if it follows from this study.
- **Suggested fix:** "Attribute this background claim to another source or remove it."

### Study 17: Swallowing trouble after stroke leaves a mark in the gut, Japanese hospital study suggests
**PMID:** 42854972 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Causal tone in headline** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Swallowing trouble after stroke leaves a mark in the gut, Japanese hospital study suggests"
- **Abstract says:** "The exploratory findings do not establish independent oral-health effects, oral origin, causality, or clinical utility."
- **Problem:** "Leaves a mark in the gut" implies that swallowing impairment produces the stool microbial change, which this cross-sectional, explicitly exploratory analysis cannot show.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to "Swallowing trouble after stroke tracks with a different gut bacterial profile.""

**Issue B. Direction of the stool finding blurred** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Patients with impaired oral intake and those with worse oral health both showed significantly different stool profiles of these oral-associated bacteria"
- **Abstract says:** "the score was lower with FILS <7 (β -0.99 ...), higher per FILS point (β 0.15 ...), and lower with each 1-SD increase in ROAG (β -0.32 ...)"
- **Problem:** The abstract reports a specific direction — a lower oral-associated taxa score — not merely a "different" profile.
- **Suggested fix:** "State that impaired oral intake and worse oral health were each associated with a lower oral-associated taxa score in stool."
